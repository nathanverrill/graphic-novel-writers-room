# EVOKE AVALANCHE — Campaign Bible

## The premise in one paragraph

Two kinds of snow, one question. In Colorado, an avalanche forecaster reads a slope that has
been quietly rotten all winter and looks perfectly safe from the road. In Antarctica, a sea-ice
scientist watches fast ice that has held an emperor penguin colony for as long as anyone has
recorded it, and knows it is the same problem at a different scale: a system loaded past its
strength, holding only because nothing has touched it yet. Alpha Dynamics offers to hold it up.
The book is about what that offer costs.

## The story question

**When a thing you love is going to break, is it better to have it held up by someone else, or
to know when it will fall?**

Wren wants to save the colony. Alpha can. The book does not pretend otherwise — his
intervention works, at least at first, and that is the trap.

## The principle

Every EVOKE campaign carries one Prosperity Principle. Avalanche's is **AGENCY**:

> **Being rescued is not the same as being able.**

A community, a species, or a scientist kept alive by a system they cannot question has been
preserved, not saved. The book never argues that Alpha's help fails on the merits. It argues
that help which cannot be refused is a different thing from help.

## The two settings

### The Antarctic Peninsula — the colony

A small international research station on the Peninsula, a boat day from an emperor colony that
breeds on fast ice in a sheltered bay. Fast ice is sea ice frozen fast to the shore; emperors
need it to hold from roughly April, when they arrive, until the chicks fledge around December
(**T**). If it goes out early, the chicks go into the water without waterproof feathers, and a
season is lost (**T** — this happened to several colonies in the Bellingshausen Sea in 2022,
and is why the emperor penguin was listed as threatened under the US Endangered Species Act in
October 2022).

Write the station the way stations actually are: containers, cable runs, a galley that is the
only warm social room, weather that cancels everything, and science that is mostly logistics.
Nobody is alone out there by choice.

### The Colorado San Juans — the snowpack

A mountain town, a highway that closes, and a forecast office. Colorado's is a continental
snowpack: thin, cold, and famous for persistent weak layers buried deep and staying dangerous
for months (**T**). Forecasters here do not ask whether a slope will slide, they ask what is
underneath and what would be enough.

The link between the two settings is real and is not a coincidence the book invents: the
long-term record of Antarctic sea ice is kept in Boulder, Colorado (**T**). People who study
ice at the bottom of the world do it from a state where the snow tries to kill them every
winter. Use that. Wren flies eleven thousand miles to look at ice she could have plotted from
her desk, because a number is not the same as standing on it.

## What the protagonists are trying to save

**The emperor colony.** Named in the book as the Weddell-side colony the station has counted
for thirty years. Not the last colony on earth, not a symbol — a specific place with a specific
history of counts, which is what makes losing it legible.

**And the thing at home.** In Colorado, the American pika: a small talus-dwelling mammal that
cannot tolerate heat, cannot move downhill, and is already retreating upslope until there is no
up left (**T**). Pikas are not federally listed (**T**), which is part of the point — the book
does not need a species to be officially doomed for its loss to count.

The two are the same animal in the story's terms: **a creature that needs one specific place to
stay exactly as it is, in a world where nothing is staying as it is.** Emperors need fast ice
in a particular bay. Pikas need talus at a particular elevation. Neither can be relocated into
safety by anyone who means well.

## The avalanche

The book's hinge, around the midpoint. A large dry-slab avalanche in the San Juans, released
naturally, running further than the historical record says it should and taking out the road
and the remote power and data link that the station's satellite downlink and the pika study both
depend on.

It must be written honestly:

- The weak layer formed in an early, thin, cold snowpack in November — a long dry spell turning
  the base to sugar (**T**, this is ordinary Colorado).
- A dust-on-snow event from the drying Colorado Plateau darkened a layer in March, which is
  documented and is worsening (**T**).
- A warm, wet storm loaded it fast and put liquid water where there had never been liquid water
  that early (**EG** — the direction is well established, the specific event is invented).
- It runs into terrain that has no avalanche history in living memory, which is what makes it a
  climate event and not just a bad day (**EG**).

Tomas is caught at the edge of it. He lives. **Nobody dies in the avalanche** — the book is not
about a death, it is about a man who read the snow correctly, said so, and was not able to stop
what he predicted. That is the same helplessness Wren feels about the ice, made physical.

**Do not** have the avalanche be triggered by Alpha Dynamics equipment or sabotage. It is not a
crime. Making it anyone's fault lets the reader off.

## Alpha in this book

Alpha, Alpha Dynamics and Alpha AI are canon and are defined in `evoke/rules/alpha.md`, which
this campaign inherits whole. Nothing here overrides it.

His angle in Avalanche is **cryosphere intervention**: Alpha Dynamics proposes, and then
begins, an engineering programme to keep specific ice where it is — the real menu of proposals
is in `input/cryosphere-intervention.md`, and the book should use the grounded end of it.

Why it fits him exactly:

- It is a problem of coordination and capital that no government has managed (he is **right**
  about that).
- It produces measurable outcomes on a schedule (his feedback loop).
- It makes the colony's survival **a service** — something delivered, maintainable, and
  withdrawable. That is dependency, which alpha.md establishes as his real power.

Alpha should be right at least once in a way that costs the protagonists something to admit.
The intervention should **work**. The horror is not that the penguins die anyway; it is that
they live, on a subscription.

## The license log

Avalanche spends **no new large license**. It inherits the one EVOKE license:

| L | The departure | Nearest honest version | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alpha AI** | An unusually capable autonomous executive system modeled on Alpha's judgment, holding extraordinary delegated authority across the conglomerate. | Narrow AI systems coordinating industrial operations under human sign-off, which exist now. | `evoke/rules/alpha.md` §21 |
| **L, clarity** | A single research station stands in for the several institutions that would really share this work — a station, a university, a national programme and a treaty body. | Palmer-style US LTER station plus a national ice service plus CCAMLR. | This book, for page count |

Everything else in the book is T, EG or S and must be labeled as such where it is invented.
**If a scene wants a new license, it must be logged here first.** The Antarctic engineering is
the most tempting place to cheat: do not let the intervention do anything the physics does not
allow, and do not let it be built faster than steel and ships allow.

## Rules for this book

1. **No villain technology.** Everything Alpha Dynamics builds is something a well-funded
   consortium could plausibly attempt. The threat is the arrangement, not the machine.
2. **The ice does not care.** Weather and ice behave on their own schedule and never dramatically
   time themselves to a character's argument.
3. **Science is slow and social.** Findings need a season, a ship, a second instrument and
   somebody else's agreement. A character cannot discover something alone overnight.
4. **No last-minute rescue by discovery.** Nothing is saved because someone runs the numbers
   again at the right moment.
5. **Nobody explains the climate to the reader.** The characters know it. They argue about what
   to do, never about whether.
6. **The penguins are animals.** They are not noble, they do not look at anyone meaningfully,
   and they are loud and they smell. Their value in the book is that they exist and are specific.
7. **Cold is a character.** Everything takes longer with mittens on, metal burns, batteries die,
   and nothing works the first time.

## What this book is not

- Not a disaster story. The avalanche is at the middle, not the end, and the world does not end.
- Not an eco-thriller with a conspiracy. Nobody is hiding data or poisoning anything.
- Not hopeless. Something is genuinely saved. The question is what it cost and who holds it.
