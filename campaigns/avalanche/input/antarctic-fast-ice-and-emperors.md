# Antarctic fast ice, emperors, and a station

Everything here is labeled with the vocabulary in `hard-sf-rules.md` — **T** truth, **EG**
educated guess, **S** speculation. Nothing in this document is canon by itself. What is true in
the book is in `rules/bible.md`.

## Fast ice, and why the birds need it

- **Fast ice is sea ice frozen fast to the shore** — held in place rather than drifting. It
  forms in sheltered bays, often anchored behind grounded icebergs (**T**).
- **Emperor penguins breed on it, not on land.** They arrive around April, lay in May–June, the
  males incubate through the winter dark, and chicks fledge around December–January (**T**).
- The ice has to hold for that whole stretch. **If it breaks out before the chicks have
  waterproof adult feathers, they go into the water and drown or freeze** (**T**).
- In 2022, several colonies in the Bellingshausen Sea lost essentially their entire cohort when
  fast ice broke out early (**T**). This is the documented event the book's bad season is
  modeled on.
- The emperor penguin was listed as **threatened under the US Endangered Species Act in October
  2022**, on the basis of projected sea-ice loss rather than present numbers (**T**).
- Projections under high emissions have most colonies quasi-extinct by 2100 (**T** as a
  published projection; the outcome itself is **EG**).

### What that means on the page

The disaster is not a storm. It is **absence** — a bay that is open water in October when it
has always been ice. Write the horror as a flat grey sea where a map says there should be a
colony. Nobody has to explain it.

## What a colony is actually like

- Loud. Continuous braying and trumpeting; adults find chicks by voice in a crowd of thousands
  (**T**).
- It smells strongly — guano, fish, ammonia — and the smell is in your clothes for weeks (**T**).
- The ice is stained pink-brown with guano for hundreds of metres, visible from the air and in
  satellite imagery. **Colonies are actually located and counted from space by their guano
  stains** (**T**) — a good, true, visually strong detail for a graphic novel.
- Chicks huddle in crèches; adults huddle in rotating packs in winter, individuals cycling from
  the cold outside to the warm middle (**T**).
- Emperors are big — around 1.1–1.3 m, the largest penguin (**T**). Draw them at human
  waist-to-chest height, not as cartoon birds.

## Sea ice, the larger picture

- Antarctic sea-ice extent was long the awkward case — it did not decline the way the Arctic's
  did, and for years it even trended slightly up (**T**).
- That changed sharply. Record lows in the 2016–2023 stretch, with 2023 far outside the previous
  range (**T**).
- The long-term satellite record of sea-ice extent is maintained in **Boulder, Colorado** (**T**).
  This is the book's real link between its two settings and needs no invention.
- Attribution is genuinely harder here than in the Arctic; scientists argue about how much is
  forced and how much is variability (**T**). **Let the characters have this argument honestly**
  — it is more interesting than certainty, and Wren's caution is accurate to the field.

## Krill, and the food underneath

- Antarctic krill (*Euphausia superba*) is the keystone: penguins, seals, whales, seabirds
  (**T**).
- Krill larvae depend on sea ice — they feed on algae on its underside and shelter there (**T**).
- There is a commercial krill fishery, managed by CCAMLR, concentrated in the same Peninsula
  waters the wildlife uses (**T**).
- Less ice means less larval habitat, and a southward shift in krill distribution is documented
  (**T**).

A useful, true complication: **the fishery and the penguins want the same square kilometres.**
This is a real governance fight and does not need a villain.

## The Peninsula and its penguins

- The western Antarctic Peninsula is one of the fastest-warming regions on earth (**T**).
- Adélie penguins there have collapsed at northern sites, while gentoos — more flexible, less
  ice-dependent — have moved in and increased (**T**).
- So a Peninsula station sees **replacement, not emptiness**: the wrong birds thriving where the
  right ones used to be. That is a better image than a dead landscape, and it is true.

## Living at a station

- Containers, corrugated buildings on piles, cable runs, and a galley that is the only warm
  social room in the world (**T**).
- Everything is logistics: ships twice a season, weather cancelling flights and boats for days,
  a cargo manifest as a genuine source of drama (**T**).
- Small population, everyone doing three jobs, and the technicians, cooks and mechanics are as
  central as the scientists (**T**). **Write them in.** A station where only the PhDs speak is
  a false station.
- Field work is boat- and weather-dependent, done in survival gear, with mandatory check-ins
  (**T**).
- Satellite bandwidth is limited and shared, and people queue for it (**T**, though improving).
  Useful for making the Colorado data link matter.

## Cold, as a working condition

- Bare skin on metal sticks. Batteries die fast; people keep spares inside their jackets (**T**).
- Everything takes three times as long with mittens, and every task has a mitten-off window
  that is counted in seconds (**T**).
- Goggles fog the instant you come inside; glasses are useless for the first two minutes (**T**).
- Wind, not temperature, is what makes a day impossible (**T**).

## What would be speculation

- A colony relocating itself successfully to a new site on a useful timescale (**S** — some site
  shifts are observed, so it is not impossible, but do not use it as a rescue).
- Emperors breeding successfully on land or on an ice shelf at scale (**S** — a few instances
  of shelf breeding are recorded, which is exactly why it is S and not fantasy; do not let it
  solve the plot).
- A managed feeding or captive-breeding programme for emperors (**S**, and if the book uses it,
  it must be honest about scale and cost).

## Cut list

- A single charismatic penguin the cast names and follows. The birds are a population.
- A last-of-its-kind animal. There are hundreds of thousands of emperors; the tragedy is
  trajectory, not a final individual.
- Anyone rescuing chicks by hand in a way that saves the season.
- Weather that arrives exactly when the plot needs it.
