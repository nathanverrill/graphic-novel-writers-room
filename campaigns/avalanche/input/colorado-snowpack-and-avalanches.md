# Colorado snow, and what it does

Labeled with the vocabulary in `hard-sf-rules.md`. Nothing here is canon by itself; the book's
own truth is in `rules/bible.md`.

## Why Colorado snow is different

- Colorado has a **continental snowpack**: cold, dry, relatively thin, sitting under clear skies
  at high elevation (**T**).
- That combination builds **depth hoar** — large, faceted, sugary crystals with almost no
  strength — usually from early-season snow that sat thin and cold through a long dry spell
  (**T**).
- Buried under later storms, this becomes a **persistent weak layer**: a slab of cohesive snow
  resting on ball bearings. It does not heal for months (**T**).
- The result is Colorado's signature problem, the **persistent slab avalanche**: it can be
  triggered long after the storm that loaded it, from a distance, or from flat ground below the
  slope, and it fails across a whole slope at once (**T**).

### The image that carries the book

A snow pit: a cut wall of clean snow with visible layers, and a forecaster's bare hand at the
one that crumbles. **A slope can look perfect and be rotten at the bottom, and the only way to
know is to dig.** That is the book's thesis in one panel, and it is literally true.

## What forecasters actually do

- Weather stations, storm totals, wind data, and a lot of time on the phone (**T**).
- Field days: digging pits, running stability tests, watching for signs — whumpfing, shooting
  cracks, recent natural avalanches (**T**). **Whumpfing** — the audible collapse of a weak
  layer under you — is the single most useful sound in the book (**T**).
- Issuing a daily danger rating on a five-step scale, by zone, with named avalanche problems
  (**T**).
- For highways: working with the transport department on closures and on **control work** —
  deliberately triggering slides with explosives when the road is shut (**T**). Colorado uses
  howitzers and remote fixed launchers on some paths (**T**).
- The forecaster's authority is real but bounded: they advise, and closures are a decision with
  other people and money in it (**T**).

## Climate change, specifically, in this snowpack

Be careful and specific here. The honest picture:

- Warming shifts precipitation toward rain, shortens the season at the margins, and moves
  snowmelt earlier (**T**).
- **Dust-on-snow**: dust blown off a drying Colorado Plateau settles on the snowpack, darkens
  it, and makes it absorb far more sunlight, accelerating melt by weeks. Documented and studied
  from a long-running site in the San Juans (**T**).
- **Rain-on-snow** events at elevations that used to be reliably below freezing add load and
  liquid water fast (**T**, increasingly).
- Whether avalanche *frequency* rises is genuinely uncertain and debated (**T** that it is
  debated). What is better supported is a shift in **character**: more wet-snow problems,
  changed timing, and less reliable historical analogues (**EG**).
- **Historical run-out records become less trustworthy** as the conditions that produced them
  change (**EG**). This is the book's point and it should be argued, not asserted.

### The book's avalanche, built honestly

1. Thin, cold, early-season snow in November forms depth hoar (**T**, ordinary).
2. A dust event in March darkens a layer (**T**, documented and worsening).
3. A warm, wet storm loads the slab fast and puts liquid water deep for the first time that
   early (**EG**).
4. It releases naturally, large, and **runs into terrain with no slide history in living
   memory** (**EG** — the mechanism is real, this particular event is invented).

March 2019 is the real precedent: a historic Colorado cycle with slides running into mature
forest and across valley floors that had not seen avalanches in generations (**T**). The book's
event should be smaller than that and more local — do not exaggerate past the real record.

## What being caught is like

- It is fast. Slabs move at highway speeds within seconds (**T**).
- Survival depends on staying on the surface, and on being found in minutes — survival odds drop
  steeply after roughly fifteen minutes of burial (**T**).
- Standard kit: transceiver, probe, shovel, and a partner who knows how to use all three (**T**).
- Debris sets like concrete on stopping (**T**).
- Injuries are often trauma, not only asphyxia — trees and rocks (**T**).

**Tomas is caught at the edge, carried, not buried.** Broken wrist, cracked rib. Write the
ninety seconds honestly: no heroism is available in them.

## The San Juans

- Steep, high, heavily mineralised old mining country; towns in valley bottoms with one or two
  roads out (**T**).
- Highways cross avalanche paths with named slide paths that run most winters, and closures are
  ordinary life (**T**).
- Old mine workings and tailings everywhere, and water quality problems from them (**T**).
- Small year-round population, a large seasonal one, and a real tension between them (**T**).

## The species at home

- **American pika**: small talus-dwelling relative of rabbits. Does not hibernate; makes hay
  piles. **Sensitive to heat — can die from a few hours above about 25–26 °C** (**T**). Retreats
  upslope as it warms, until there is no upslope left. Documented local extinctions in parts of
  the Great Basin (**T**). Petitioned for ESA listing and **not listed** (**T**).
- **Canada lynx**: reintroduced to the San Juans starting 1999, considered established;
  depends on snowshoe hare and deep snow (**T**).
- **Boreal toad**: state endangered in Colorado, hit hard by chytrid fungus (**T**).
- **Greenback cutthroat trout**: Colorado's state fish, ESA-listed, reduced to a tiny remnant
  population before recovery work (**T**).

Pika is the one the book uses, because it is the exact analogue of the emperor: **a species
tied to one specific place, that cannot be moved to safety by anyone who means well.**
