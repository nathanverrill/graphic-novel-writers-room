# Research — the real science behind the story

## Larsen Ice Shelf (Antarctica)

The Larsen region is a major climate-change research site — the area surrounding the Larsen Ice Shelf, in four subsections:

- Larsen A — collapsed in 1995
- Larsen B — collapsed in 2002
- Larsen C — fragile, thinning, vulnerable; will be at its thinnest in 10,000 years; total collapse predicted by 2100
- Larsen D

If Larsen C suffers a major structural failure or total collapse around 2046, it triggers the "cork out of the bottle" effect: floating ice shelves act as physical buttresses, so removing Larsen C allows the massive land-based glaciers behind it to accelerate up to five times faster into the ocean. This glacial surge is predicted to ultimately add several millimeters directly to global sea-level rise.

Basin geology influences ice shelf stability.

A 2026 Nature Communications paper examined Föhn-induced melting over the Larsen C Ice Shelf, showing how atmospheric river shape and landfall affect melt patterns.

How scientists already adapt to unstable ice shelves (and will continue to):
- relocating camps frequently
- using airborne surveys instead of ground surveys
- avoiding crevasse zones
- monitoring ice movement in real time

## The 2026 Nepal disaster (Sitara's backstory)

A massive rock-ice avalanche collapsed from the Lirung massif high in the Himalayas. It plunged into the valley, transformed into a fast-moving debris flow, and burst through the Lhende Khola and Trishuli River system, striking the Nepal-Tibet border at Gyirong Port.

The collapse generated:
- a wall of water, mud, and rock
- flash floods that swept through towns and border facilities
- destruction along a 72 km stretch of river in Nepal

Across Nepal and Tibet:
- 1,451+ deaths in Nepal and 43+ in Tibet
- 6,264+ people missing across both sides of the border
- entire border facilities at Gyirong Port wiped out
- houses, roads, bridges, and power projects destroyed in Nepal
- bodies carried up to 240 km downstream into India

Foreign nationals were heavily affected: hundreds of tourists missing, including Americans, Indians, British, Australians, Ukrainians, and others.

## Sherpa biology and culture (Sitara's heritage)

Biological adaptations: evolutionary biologists have found that Sherpas possess unique genetic traits, including the EPAS1 gene variant. Their bodies use oxygen with remarkable efficiency at cellular levels, producing higher energy (ATP) per oxygen molecule while keeping blood flow smooth without dangerously thickening the blood.

Naming and daily life: Sherpa society traditionally relied on yak herding, high-pass trade with Tibet, and cold-climate agriculture. Children are traditionally given a first name for the day of the week they were born: Dawa (Monday), Mingma (Tuesday), Lhakpa (Wednesday), Phurba (Thursday), Pasang (Friday), Pemba (Saturday), Nima (Sunday). Staple foods include tsampa (roasted barley flour), thukpa (noodle soup), fermented yak butter tea (suja), and potatoes, which revolutionized the local food economy after their introduction in the 19th century.

"Lhakpa" is a Tibetan name commonly given to boys born on Wednesday, the fourth day in the Tibetan calendar, associated with Mercury; it carries meanings of strength, courage, and resilience, and is used across the Tibetan Plateau, Nepal, Bhutan, and parts of northern India.

## Waste to value (the organization's real basis)

AI-driven robotic sorting: metal recycling is notoriously difficult due to the labor needed to separate ferrous metals (iron, steel) from non-ferrous metals (copper, aluminum, brass) and trash.

AMP Robotics (Louisville, CO): a globally recognized Colorado startup that builds AI-guided robotics and optical sorting systems. They partner with local material facilities (such as a next-generation $50 million facility in Commerce City) to deploy AI and automated air jets that extract metal and other recyclables with up to 90% efficiency.

Conservation programs connect the Antarctic conservation process and Rocky Mountain conservation.
