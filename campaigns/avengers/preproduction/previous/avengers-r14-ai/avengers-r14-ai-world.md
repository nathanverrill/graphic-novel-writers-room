## Overview

This is a near-future world in which the physical effects of climate change are no longer abstract. Ice shelves fracture, sea ice disappears, krill habitat fails, mountain water systems collapse, Colorado snowpack declines, and cold-water ecosystems lose their margins of survival.

The same world also contains sophisticated but unevenly distributed tools: neural implants, autonomous field robots, local mesh networks, AI sorting systems, extreme-environment laboratories, satellite communications, autonomous mapping, and advanced chemical processing. These systems are powerful enough to expose hidden infrastructure and accelerate research, but they remain dependent on energy, feedstock, maintenance, trained people, authorization, weather, and reliable data.

The most important political conflict is not simply between science and ignorance. It is between open ecological knowledge, corporate control of infrastructure and data, and the urgent pressure to produce useful results before damaged ecosystems pass recovery thresholds.

Antarctica is both a protected scientific commons and a site of concealed commercial pressure. Colorado is where Antarctic discoveries become visible environmental interventions. The Himalayas are a lived environment in which local memory, inherited knowledge, and climate damage challenge the idea that institutions alone own environmental understanding.

## Time and the 2046 Baseline

### The future’s material texture

**Canon — FIXED.** The world follows the hard-SF **80/15/5** model:

- **80% Truth:** recognizable laws, buildings, food systems, roads, clothing, corporate bureaucracy, research practice, and environmental institutions.
- **15% EG:** emerging technologies that have become practical or routine by 2046, including compact autonomous robots, distributed sensors, neural interfaces, AI sorting, and improved remote environmental monitoring.
- **5% S:** the Roman–TJ neural handshake and related consequences.
- **L:** Project 863’s catalytic chemical recycling process, which is deliberately more capable than the real-world baseline but has fixed limits. `(rules/hard-sf-rules.md; rules/decisions.md)`

The future is therefore built by retrofitting older systems rather than replacing them. Paper files remain important where digital records have been redacted, erased, fragmented, or deliberately withheld. Remote bases still need fuel, power, tracked vehicles, aircraft, food, spare parts, and human maintenance. A new sensor can be attached to an old corridor; it does not make the corridor safe.

### Climate conditions

By 2046:

- Food supply is strained and prices are high in the showrunner’s draft material. `(input/team2-mcmurdo-background.md)`
- Colorado rivers and lakes are drying or running earlier and lower.
- Antarctic sea-ice decline is affecting the krill food web.
- Larsen C is thinning and vulnerable.
- Himalayan glacier instability has already produced catastrophic flooding.
- Coastal and ice-dependent communities live with continuous warnings, relocation, monitoring, and insurance or access restrictions.

The book must distinguish among:

1. **Antarctic sea-ice loss**, which removes seasonal habitat important to krill.
2. **Krill decline**, an ecological consequence affecting the food web.
3. **Larsen C fracture or ice-shelf failure**, a separate structural hazard intensified by atmospheric and oceanic warming.
4. **Local drilling vibration and disturbance**, which can worsen local instability but cannot single-handedly cause global climate collapse.

## Geography and Major Regions

### Antarctica

The Antarctic settings occupy an accessible coastal and inland research zone associated with the Antarctic Peninsula and Larsen system. Human bases and homes are placed in or near a coastal research community. Falcon lies inland as a covert drilling and extraction-monitoring site. Nexus occupies a nearby coastal cave system reached by a weather-dependent over-ice route. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The landscape includes:

- Snow-covered mountain ranges beside ocean ice.
- Glaciers and moving ice.
- Crevasses and surface cracks.
- Research bases, homes, laboratories, fuel stores, and tracked-vehicle routes.
- Areas with little visible wildlife.
- A small research community separated from restricted field sites.
- Antarctic sea ice that changes seasonally and is monitored as both infrastructure and habitat.

The two Antarctic sites are connected by tracked vehicles and occasional aircraft. The journey from Falcon to Nexus takes approximately two hours under suitable weather conditions. Satellite communication is delayed or intermittent rather than instantaneous. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### Larsen system

The Larsen region includes the historical Larsen A, Larsen B, Larsen C, and Larsen D subsections.

- Larsen A collapsed in 1995.
- Larsen B collapsed in 2002.
- Larsen C remains a large, thinning ice shelf whose stability is a central scientific concern.
- Basin geology affects ice-shelf stability.
- Atmospheric rivers and Föhn winds influence melt patterns.
- Floating ice shelves act as buttresses for land-based glaciers. Their failure can permit faster glacier flow into the ocean.

A major fracture opens during the story, but **Larsen C does not fully collapse**. The team can stop drilling that is worsening local danger and transmit evidence, but cannot reverse warming or restore the shelf. `(rules/decisions.md; references/team1-scott-base-research.md)`

### Falcon

Falcon is a covert exploratory drilling and monitoring site rather than a conventional producing mine.

Its physical systems include:

- Directional boreholes.
- Seismic equipment.
- Pumps.
- Heated shelters.
- Fuel stores.
- Concealed waste-handling systems.
- Restricted communications.
- Monitoring instruments recording ice movement and extraction activity.

Falcon operates under a legitimate scientific-drilling permit. The operation disguises resource exploration as climate and ice-core work through subcontractors, falsified manifests, restricted communications, and exploitation of isolated weather windows. It is intended to claim data, access, and future rights before the resource becomes legally exploitable; it is not yet commercially extracting a resource. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### Nexus

Nexus was a research laboratory belonging to the same CSG-linked program as Falcon.

- Falcon was the operational and extraction-monitoring site.
- Nexus was the research site.
- Falcon documented damage and monitored the operation.
- Nexus studied ecological consequences and possible waste-to-value responses.
- Nexus ran parallel ecological and materials programs.
- Its research included krill, sea-ice conditions, plastics, and Project 863.
- It was closed after researchers documented the relationship among extraction, sea-ice loss, and krill decline.
- Power is off, but insulated archives and limited emergency systems remain.
- The laboratory is built into or adjacent to a cave system.
- Its archives contain paper records, less-redacted Project 863 material, ecological data, sediment samples, old equipment, and a dead ecosystem tank.

Nexus can be reached by a weather-dependent over-ice route from the active site. Its physical condition includes darkness, damaged infrastructure, sealed areas, ice intrusion, and limited emergency systems.

### Merritt Station

Merritt Station is a separate Antarctic research, remediation, and logistics hub inspired by Wilkes Station and located in Wilkes Land, East Antarctica.

**Canon — FIXED.** Alpha Dynamics restored or partially restored Merritt Station between 2042 and 2046. Alpha Dynamics remains the operator after the exposure, but must open the station to monitored scientific access, publish provenance records, and suspend commercial sampling pending review. `(rules/worldbuilding/alpha_dynamics_merritt_station_reference.md; rules/worldbuilding/merritt_station_hidden_knowledge_reference.md; rules/decisions.md)`

Merritt originally operated from roughly the 1960s through the 1990s and was then abandoned for approximately thirty years. Alpha Dynamics’ restoration creates a functioning but uneven facility with:

- Laboratories.
- Cold storage.
- Data facilities.
- Research and logistics areas.
- Environmental remediation zones.
- Robotics and autonomous-system proving grounds.
- Access to ice cores, legacy archives, subglacial environments, and unusual biological material.

The station contains both restored sections and hazardous sealed areas. Ice movement, snow accumulation, flooding, refreezing, and structural deformation have altered its original layout.

In some locations, the current surface is **3–20 meters higher** than the original station surface. Corridors may be offset, crushed, or blocked. Exploration scenes may show:

- Warped metal corridors.
- Blue ice intrusion.
- Frozen cables.
- Obsolete equipment.
- Snow entering through structural fractures.
- Collapsed bulkheads.
- Rappel shafts leading toward sealed lower sections.
- TJ units identifying narrow routes around ice-blocked access points.

`(rules/worldbuilding/merritt_station_hidden_knowledge_reference.md; rules/worldbuilding/refinements.md)`

Merritt’s value is not a simple mineral deposit. It is a physical archive and a gateway to research involving:

- Ice cores.
- Trapped gases and dust.
- Biological samples.
- Legacy field notes.
- Subglacial sediments.
- Cold-active enzymes.
- Extremophile metabolism.
- Low-temperature remediation.
- Biological and chemical systems that may have applications off-continent.

**Canon — FIXED.** A joint Antarctic research and environmental review placed organisms and associated samples under a provisional non-commercialization restriction until independent ecological assessment was complete. This restriction is also grounded in the Antarctic Treaty, Madrid Protocol, and related environmental agreements. No single person made the decision. `(rules/decisions.md; rules/worldbuilding/alpha_dynamics_merritt_station_reference.md)`

Sitara recovered archived research files, sequence data, enzyme assays, and sealed sample records from a damaged Merritt research area. She did not remove living organisms or physical samples. She concealed copies because the material indicated that commercial access had been restricted pending ecological review. `(rules/decisions.md; rules/worldbuilding/merritt_station_hidden_knowledge_reference.md)`

### Himalayas

The Himalayas are both a setting and a source of environmental knowledge.

The 2026 Lirung–Lhende Khola disaster destroyed a Thakali settlement in the Kali Gandaki region and killed Sitara’s parents. The broader disaster involved a rock–ice avalanche, debris flow, flash flooding, destroyed roads and bridges, and damage extending through the Trishuli system toward the Nepal–Tibet border. `(rules/decisions.md; references/team1-scott-scott-base-research.md)`

The Himalayan environment includes:

- Glaciers and unstable ice.
- High-altitude settlements.
- Mountain routes and expedition infrastructure.
- Flood-damaged homes, roads, and power systems.
- Community knowledge of seasonal water, terrain, weather, and changing ice.
- Environmental observations that may not appear in institutional datasets.

The Himalayas also contain the origin point for Roman and TJ’s unusual connection: in 2042, Roman fell into unstable ice during a personal senior climbing expedition unrelated to Sitara, her family, and Merritt research. Lightning produced the persistent low-bandwidth neural handshake. The meaningful connection between Roman’s expedition and Sitara’s history is thematic and environmental: both protagonists’ lives were changed by unstable ice and water systems. `(rules/decisions.md)`

## History and Institutional Memory

### Antarctic governance

**Canon — FIXED.** The Madrid Protocol and Antarctic environmental framework prohibit mining in Antarctica. `(rules/rules.md; references/team1-scott-base-research.md)`

Formal compliance depends on:

- Permits.
- Environmental assessments.
- Reporting.
- Inspection.
- Restricted operations.
- Waste removal or containment.
- Scientific justification.
- Communications and access records.

The story’s illegal operation exploits the gap between formal scientific activity and concealed exploratory extraction. Its operators attempt to remain inside the appearance of legitimate research while pursuing future resource claims.

The provisional non-commercialization restriction covering Merritt organisms and associated samples operates within this environmental and legal framework. It does not belong to one individual; it is the result of joint scientific and environmental review, reinforced by international Antarctic agreements. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### Abandoned science

Both Merritt and Nexus preserve a recurring institutional problem: research can remain physically present while becoming administratively invisible.

Records may be:

- Undigitized.
- Redacted.
- Buried in paper archives.
- Removed from active servers.
- Separated between operational and scientific sites.
- Held inside obsolete storage systems.
- Hidden behind access credentials or machine authentication.

This makes a paper archive materially important rather than nostalgic. A file can contain coordinates or provenance that has disappeared from current databases.

### The 2026 Himalayan disaster

The disaster remains a living historical condition rather than merely a flashback. It shaped:

- Sitara’s relationship to environmental research.
- Her surviving family network.
- The loss of homes and local infrastructure.
- Her mistrust of institutions that treat catastrophe as a dataset.
- The connection between climate research and personal responsibility.

The settlement remains fictional and unnamed until cultural consultation. Sitara’s aunt and cousins remain living but off-page, represented through communications and household objects rather than invented identities or practices. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### 2042 Himalayan accident

Roman’s 2042 accident is separate from Sitara’s 2026 disaster. It occurs during Roman’s personal expedition in Nepal. The shared connection is changing ice and water conditions, not a single historical event. `(rules/decisions.md)`

- Control of active and archived sites.
- Employment.
- Access credentials.

- Legal staff.

### The waste-to-value organization

**Canon — FIXED.** The waste-to-value organization is a nonprofit public-benefit corporation initially funded by CSG and governed by scientists, conservation representatives, and affected communities. It has authority over conservation deployments but not private extraction. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### Inspection and suppression

A credible corporate suppression system does not need to erase everything. It can:

- Reclassify a project.
- Move it between divisions.
- Redact names and coordinates.
- Revoke credentials.
- Restrict communications.
- Place evidence in incompatible formats.
- Make access weather-dependent.
- Discredit a publication.
- Delay inspection.
- Separate ecological and materials data.
- Present a covert drilling program as scientific work.

The final institutional consequence is realistic rather than total: publication produces hearings and an inspection request, but no immediate arrest. The victory is that suppression becomes impossible and the public record survives. `(rules/decisions.md)`

- A genome, sequence, or observation.
- A scientific explanation.

- An engineered implementation.
- A dataset.

### Colorado’s material economy

The Colorado waste-to-value system connects:

- Waste streams.
- AI-guided sorting.
- Chemical recycling.
- Water restoration.
- Conservation funding.
- Local employment.
- Corporate research.
- Public-benefit deployment.

The pilot is located on a stressed Colorado tributary connected to a CU Boulder/INSTAAR monitoring program. AI-driven optical sorting separates metal and other recyclable material using cameras, classification systems, and automated air jets. Real-world reference material describes efficiencies up to approximately 90% for some sorting operations, but this is research input rather than settled in-book performance. `(references/team1-scott-base-research.md)`

## Technology

### Project 863

**Canon — FIXED.** Project 863 uses catalytic chemical recycling to depolymerize selected waste plastics into hydrocarbon feedstock. It requires:

- Sorting.
- Heat.
- Energy.
- Clean feedstock.
- Controlled chemical processing.

It produces residual waste and emissions. It cannot process all plastics and is useful mainly for localized waste streams rather than replacing fossil fuels. `(rules/decisions.md)`

**Canon — FIXED.** Nexus’s cold-active enzyme research lowers the temperature and energy needed for a bounded Project 863 pretreatment, while the actual depolymerization remains the licensed process. The Colorado success proves value, increasing CSG’s incentive to control Merritt samples and expand Falcon’s search for further commercial applications. `(rules/decisions.md; rules/extremophiles-colorado.md)`

Project 863 is therefore a waste-to-value tool, not a machine that turns arbitrary trash into limitless gasoline. On the page, readers should see:

- Sorting before processing.
- Feedstock rejected as unsuitable.
- Heat and power requirements.
- Residual waste.
- Emissions or capture systems.
- A bounded local stream.
- A useful product or feedstock produced from material that would otherwise require disposal.

### Neural interface

The Roman–TJ connection is a persistent low-bandwidth neural handshake caused by the 2042 lightning event. It is **S**, not ordinary telepathy.

After four years, Roman and TJ have learned one another’s operating patterns. Roman receives only selected alerts, directional impulses, and compressed sensory tags. TJ receives Roman’s attention, urgency, balance, and intent. Shielding or more than local mesh range reduces the link to intermittent status pulses, while power loss or damage can cause false gaps and sensory overload when it returns. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The link provides:

- Status.
- Direction.
- Urgent sensory signals.
- Selected machine perception.
- Limited shared context.

It does not provide:

- Unlimited data transfer.
- Complete access to TJ’s memory.
- Remote control of TJ.
- Perfect interpretation of thoughts.
- Unlimited range.
- Reliable communication in every environment.

Roman receives selected signals through his neural implant, speakers, screens, or other interfaces rather than experiencing all sensor data at once. TJ receives no unrestricted access to Roman’s thoughts; it receives bounded operating information such as attention, urgency, balance, and intent. `(rules/decisions.md)`

## Infrastructure and Logistics

### Antarctic power

Research stations and laboratories require redundant systems, including:

- Fuel stores.
- Heated shelters.
- Insulated archives.
- Emergency power.
- Communications systems.
- Maintenance shops.
- Cold storage.
- Water and waste systems.
- Backup sensors.

Nexus has limited emergency systems but is not fully powered. Merritt’s restored sections use renewable-energy systems alongside the logistical systems needed to operate a remote Antarctic station. `(rules/worldbuilding/alpha_dynamics_merritt_station_reference.md)`

### Transport

Travel uses:

- Tracked vehicles.
- Occasional aircraft.
- Marked or surveyed over-ice routes.
- Rappelling and controlled descent through damaged shafts.
- Robotic route inspection before human entry.

Weather windows control movement. A route that is two hours in good conditions may be unavailable during wind, whiteout, crevasse movement, or communications failure.

### Communication

Antarctic communication is not seamless:

- Satellite links may be intermittent or delayed.
- Local mesh networks extend robot coverage.
- Sensor pods can act as relays.
- Paper archives remain useful when servers are inaccessible.
- Restricted communications can conceal field operations.
- A machine may authenticate a door without opening all connected systems.

### Water and waste

Water is a central infrastructure issue:

- Colorado streams and reservoirs are stressed by reduced snowpack and earlier runoff.
- Antarctic bases require controlled water production, storage, treatment, and waste handling.
- Falcon conceals waste-handling systems as part of the drilling operation.
- Nexus and Merritt function partly as laboratories for waste treatment and remediation.
- The Colorado application must visibly connect waste processing with watershed or ecosystem improvement.

## Environment and Ecology

### Antarctic ecological cascade

The Antarctic cascade has several linked layers:

1. Sea-ice loss changes habitat.
2. Krill reproduction and recruitment decline.
3. The food web loses a keystone resource.
4. Ice-shelf instability and warming intensify structural hazards.
5. Human drilling and extraction monitoring disturb a locally unstable site.
6. Information suppression delays a coordinated response.

The drilling does not create global warming or single-handedly cause Larsen C failure. It worsens local danger and obscures evidence needed for response.

### Krill ecosystem tank

The Nexus tank is dead, but it preserves:

- Sensor data.
- Sediment samples.
- Evidence that krill reproduction failed as sea-ice conditions changed.

The tank is a visual record of an ecosystem that cannot be revived by simply restarting a pump. It connects ecological measurement, laboratory procedure, and loss.

### Colorado ecosystems

Colorado’s environmental pressure is visible in:

- Reduced snowpack.
- Earlier runoff.
- Lower summer streamflow.
- Drying rivers and lakes.
- Stress on greenback cutthroat trout and other cold-water species.
- Contaminated or depleted watersheds.
- Waste accumulation.
- Communities competing for water and conservation funding.

The Colorado pilot is located on a stressed tributary connected to a CU Boulder/INSTAAR monitoring program. A material-recovery facility sorts a bounded PET/polyolefin stream; Project 863 produces feedstock; a cold-active enzyme bioreactor and constructed wetland polish contaminated process water. The first visible success is improved water clarity and the return of aquatic invertebrates at a monitored downstream station. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The visible limits are part of the result:

- Sorting and washing consume water.
- The reactor uses grid power.
- The constructed wetland can polish only a bounded flow.
- Rejected feedstock remains.
- Emissions are monitored.
- Residual solids remain.
- Throughput is temporarily limited.

### Himalayan ecosystems

The Himalayas show climate change through:

- Unstable glaciers.
- Floods and debris flows.
- Shifting seasonal water.
- Damaged roads and bridges.
- High-altitude risk.
- Displacement and loss of homes.
- Community observation of terrain and weather.

Local knowledge is not merely decorative culture. It is a source of environmental history and practical risk assessment.

**Fixed rules:**

- Processes selected plastics, not all plastics.
