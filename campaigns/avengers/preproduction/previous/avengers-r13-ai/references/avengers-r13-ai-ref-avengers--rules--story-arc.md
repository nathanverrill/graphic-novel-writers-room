# Seven-Point Story Arc — Model Reference and Best Practices

## Purpose

This document is a model-facing reference for understanding, generating, evaluating, and revising stories that use a **Seven-Point Story Arc**.

The structure is:

1. Hook
2. Plot Turn 1
3. Pinch 1
4. Midpoint
5. Pinch 2
6. Plot Turn 2
7. Resolution

This framework is especially useful for stories that need a clear escalation pattern, strong causality, meaningful character change, and a climax that resolves both external and internal conflict.

---

# Core Story Rhythm

At the highest level, the seven-point structure often follows this progression:

**Setup → Commitment → Pressure → Reversal → Consequence → Final Insight → Resolution**

A more character-driven version is:

> Something changes → the protagonist commits → pressure increases → the protagonist changes approach → consequences become personal → the missing truth is revealed → the protagonist makes an irreversible choice.

The exact genre can vary, but the structure should feel causally connected rather than episodic.

---

# 1. HOOK

## Narrative Function

The Hook establishes:

- the protagonist
- the world
- the tone
- the central imbalance
- the protagonist's starting state
- the story's most important unresolved tension

The Hook should create forward momentum without explaining everything.

It should make the audience want to know what happens next.

## Best Practices

A strong Hook usually contains at least one of the following:

- an unusual situation
- a disruption
- a mystery
- a contradiction
- a compelling relationship
- an emotionally charged problem
- a striking image or event

The Hook should also establish the protagonist's **before state**.

That state matters because the ending should show how the protagonist has changed.

## Avoid

- excessive exposition
- explaining the full backstory before conflict begins
- introducing too many unrelated characters
- starting with stakes that are already maximal
- withholding so much context that the audience cannot orient themselves

## Useful Question

> What is already unstable before the main plot begins?

---

# 2. PLOT TURN 1

## Narrative Function

Plot Turn 1 moves the protagonist from the setup into the main story.

This is the point where the central conflict becomes unavoidable, relevant, or chosen.

The protagonist's world changes.

## Best Practices

Plot Turn 1 should:

- connect directly to the Hook
- clarify what the story is now about
- force or motivate commitment
- introduce a goal, problem, mission, or relationship that cannot easily be abandoned
- establish what the protagonist stands to gain or lose

This beat should not feel like a random new plot.

It should make the Hook more meaningful.

## Character Function

The protagonist often makes an early decision here that seems reasonable based on who they are at the beginning.

That decision may later create consequences.

## Useful Question

> What happens that makes the protagonist unable to return to normal?

---

# 3. PINCH 1

## Narrative Function

Pinch 1 applies pressure.

It demonstrates that the conflict is more dangerous, complicated, powerful, or personal than the protagonist first understood.

This is often the first major reminder that the antagonist, system, mystery, threat, or underlying problem has real force.

## Best Practices

Pinch 1 should:

- increase pressure
- reveal new information
- complicate the protagonist's assumptions
- create a setback or warning
- connect previously separate story elements
- make the cost of failure more concrete

This beat should not merely repeat the conflict.

It should deepen it.

## Mystery Stories

Pinch 1 is a good place for:

- a disappearance
- a contradictory clue
- a hidden connection
- evidence of surveillance
- proof that someone knows more than expected

## Character Function

The protagonist should begin realizing that their original understanding of the situation was incomplete.

## Useful Question

> What evidence proves that the problem is bigger than the protagonist thought?

---

# 4. MIDPOINT

## Narrative Function

The Midpoint is the major structural pivot.

It should change the protagonist's understanding, strategy, relationship to the conflict, or all three.

The Midpoint often marks the shift from:

**reactive → proactive**

or

**misunderstanding → partial understanding**

or

**separate goals → shared goal**

## Best Practices

A strong Midpoint usually contains:

- a revelation
- a reversal
- a major discovery
- a shift in allegiance
- a difficult truth
- a new goal
- a deliberate commitment

The Midpoint should make the second half of the story feel different from the first.

## Character Function

The protagonist begins acting with more agency.

They may still misunderstand the full problem, but they are no longer simply reacting.

## Relationship Stories

The Midpoint is often where characters:

- reveal secrets
- form an alliance
- betray one another
- admit a truth
- become dependent on each other
- realize they must cooperate despite distrust

## Useful Question

> What changes here that makes the protagonist approach the story differently from this point forward?

---

# 5. PINCH 2

## Narrative Function

Pinch 2 is the strongest pressure point before the final turn.

It reveals the cost of the protagonist's actions, decisions, assumptions, or success.

This beat often makes the conflict personal.

## Best Practices

Pinch 2 should:

- create consequences
- make earlier decisions matter
- expose unintended harm
- challenge the protagonist morally or emotionally
- increase the cost of continuing
- make every obvious solution feel incomplete

A strong Pinch 2 often reveals that the protagonist helped create the problem.

This produces stronger drama than discovering that an outside villain caused everything.

## Character Function

The protagonist confronts responsibility.

They may realize:

- they were complicit
- their success created new harm
- they misunderstood someone
- they used the wrong strategy
- their deepest strength also contains a weakness
- what they wanted is not the same as what they now believe is right

## Useful Question

> What consequence forces the protagonist to confront their own role in the problem?

---

# 6. PLOT TURN 2

## Narrative Function

Plot Turn 2 provides the final key needed for the climax.

This is not necessarily a weapon, clue, or factual reveal.

It can be a new understanding of what the conflict actually means.

The story's apparent problem often transforms into the deeper problem.

## Best Practices

Plot Turn 2 should:

- reframe earlier events
- reveal the missing truth
- connect major story elements
- clarify what must be done
- make the final choice possible
- deepen the theme rather than simply add information

This is often where the protagonist realizes:

> The problem I thought I was solving was not the real problem.

## Mystery Function

This beat can reveal:

- why evidence was hidden
- who was actually responsible
- what a missing character was trying to do
- why the antagonist's actions make sense from their perspective
- what the true conflict is about

## Character Function

The protagonist now has enough understanding to make an informed final choice.

## Useful Question

> What final insight reveals the real nature of the conflict?

---

# 7. RESOLUTION

## Narrative Function

The Resolution answers the central dramatic question and demonstrates character change.

The climax should require action, not merely explanation.

The protagonist should make a choice that would have been difficult or impossible for their earlier self.

## Best Practices

A strong Resolution should:

- resolve the main conflict
- resolve or transform the central relationship
- complete the protagonist's internal arc
- require sacrifice, commitment, or irreversible action
- show consequences
- answer the thematic question through behavior

The best resolutions are rarely consequence-free.

Victory should cost something.

## Character Function

The ending should prove change.

Do not simply state that the protagonist has changed.

Make them act differently.

## Useful Question

> What does the protagonist choose now that they would not have chosen at the beginning?

---

# Structural Progression

A healthy seven-point arc should escalate in both **plot** and **character pressure**.

| Beat | Plot Function | Character Function |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Establish instability | Show starting state |
| Plot Turn 1 | Enter main conflict | Commit based on current worldview |
| Pinch 1 | Apply pressure | Begin doubting assumptions |
| Midpoint | Reframe the story | Become more proactive |
| Pinch 2 | Reveal consequences | Confront responsibility |
| Plot Turn 2 | Reveal final truth | Understand what must change |
| Resolution | Resolve conflict | Make transformed choice |

---

# Cause-and-Effect Best Practice

The seven beats should not feel like seven separate events.

Each major beat should emerge from the previous one.

Prefer:

> Because this happened, the protagonist chose this.  
> Because they chose this, a consequence followed.  
> Because of that consequence, they discovered something new.  
> Because of that discovery, they made a harder choice.

Avoid:

> And then this happened.  
> And then another thing happened.  
> And then a new villain appeared.

Strong story structure is causal.

---

# Character Arc Alignment

The external plot and internal character arc should reinforce each other.

A useful model is:

**Starting belief → pressure → contradiction → realization → changed behavior**

Example patterns:

- control → trust
- ambition → responsibility
- isolation → partnership
- certainty → humility
- dependence → autonomy
- self-interest → stewardship
- fear → courage
- revenge → forgiveness
- obedience → agency

The specific arc depends on the story.

The important point is that the climax should test the character's change.

---

# Antagonist Best Practices

The antagonist does not need to be evil.

An antagonist can be:

- a person
- an institution
- a system
- an environment
- a competing goal
- an ideology
- a misunderstanding
- the protagonist's own flaw

Strong antagonistic forces often:

- have understandable goals
- create real value
- believe their actions are justified
- expose contradictions in the protagonist
- force difficult choices

Avoid making the antagonist wrong about everything.

A stronger conflict often emerges when both sides have legitimate concerns but incompatible priorities.

---

# Mystery Best Practices

If the story contains a mystery, each major beat should change the audience's understanding.

A useful progression is:

1. Something is strange.
2. There is a hidden connection.
3. Someone knows more than expected.
4. The protagonists' own secrets become relevant.
5. Their actions are part of the problem.
6. The original interpretation was incomplete.
7. The truth forces a choice.

Do not let the mystery consist only of withholding information.

The audience should continually receive new evidence that changes interpretation.

---

# Stakes Best Practices

Escalation does not require making the threat larger and larger.

Stakes can escalate by becoming:

- more personal
- more morally difficult
- more irreversible
- more intimate
- more costly
- more connected to the protagonist's identity

A grounded story can become more intense without becoming apocalyptic.

Often:

**personal consequence > larger explosion**

---

# Thematic Best Practices

Theme should emerge from repeated choices and conflicts.

Do not rely on characters simply stating the theme.

Instead:

- place characters in situations where values conflict
- give different characters different answers to the same question
- let actions create consequences
- make the climax force a thematic choice

A useful thematic question often takes the form:

> When does X justify Y?

or

> Who has the right to X?

or

> What do we owe to X?

or

> Is X still good when it requires Y?

---

# Relationship Arc Best Practices

If the story centers on two or more protagonists, their relationship should have its own progression.

A common pattern is:

**separate → suspicious → forced cooperation → conflict → mutual understanding → sacrifice → changed relationship**

The relationship should not become fully resolved at the Midpoint.

The Midpoint usually creates cooperation.

The Resolution proves whether that cooperation became trust, respect, partnership, separation, or something else.

---

# Common Structural Problems

## Weak Hook

Problem:
The story begins with too much explanation.

Fix:
Begin closer to instability, contradiction, discovery, or disruption.

---

## Weak Plot Turn 1

Problem:
The protagonist could simply walk away.

Fix:
Strengthen motivation, obligation, consequence, relationship, or personal investment.

---

## Repetitive Pinch Points

Problem:
Pinch 1 and Pinch 2 do the same thing.

Fix:
Use Pinch 1 to reveal the strength or scope of the problem.

Use Pinch 2 to reveal the protagonist's responsibility or the cost of their choices.

---

## Flat Midpoint

Problem:
The story continues exactly as before.

Fix:
Change knowledge, strategy, allegiance, goal, or agency.

The second half should operate differently.

---

## Exposition-Heavy Plot Turn 2

Problem:
A character explains the whole mystery.

Fix:
Reveal the key truth, but require the protagonist to decide what to do with it.

---

## Easy Resolution

Problem:
The protagonist wins without meaningful cost.

Fix:
Require a sacrifice or irreversible choice tied to the character arc.

---

## Villain Solves the Plot

Problem:
The climax depends only on defeating or exposing the antagonist.

Fix:
Make the protagonist's final decision resolve a deeper conflict than simply removing a bad person.

---

# Model Evaluation Checklist

When evaluating a seven-point story, ask:

## Hook

- Is there immediate instability or tension?
- Is the protagonist's starting state clear?
- Is there an unanswered question?

## Plot Turn 1

- Does the main story clearly begin?
- Is the protagonist meaningfully committed?
- Does this beat grow naturally from the Hook?

## Pinch 1

- Does pressure increase?
- Is new information revealed?
- Does the problem feel larger or more complicated?

## Midpoint

- Does the protagonist's strategy change?
- Does the story become more proactive?
- Is there a meaningful revelation or reversal?

## Pinch 2

- Do earlier decisions produce consequences?
- Does the conflict become more personal or morally difficult?
- Is the protagonist forced to confront responsibility?

## Plot Turn 2

- Is the deeper truth revealed?
- Does this reframe earlier events?
- Does the protagonist now understand what must be done?

## Resolution

- Is the final choice irreversible?
- Does it resolve both plot and character?
- Does the protagonist act differently than they would have at the beginning?
- Is there a meaningful cost?

---

# Model Generation Guidance

When creating a new story using this structure:

1. Define the protagonist's starting belief or flaw.
2. Define the final choice that proves their change.
3. Design the Resolution first.
4. Design Plot Turn 2 as the insight needed to make that choice.
5. Design Pinch 2 as the consequence that makes the old approach impossible.
6. Design the Midpoint as the moment the protagonist becomes proactive.
7. Design Pinch 1 as the first serious evidence that the conflict is larger than expected.
8. Design Plot Turn 1 as the commitment to the main conflict.
9. Design the Hook to establish the starting state and central instability.

This reverse-design process often produces stronger causality than inventing events from beginning to end.

---

# Canonical Seven-Point Summary

Use this as the shortest model reference:

### 1. Hook
Establish the protagonist, world, imbalance, and starting state.

### 2. Plot Turn 1
Pull the protagonist into the main conflict and create commitment.

### 3. Pinch 1
Apply pressure and reveal that the problem is larger or more complicated than expected.

### 4. Midpoint
Reframe the story and shift the protagonist from reactive to proactive.

### 5. Pinch 2
Reveal consequences, complicity, or personal cost.

### 6. Plot Turn 2
Reveal the final truth or insight needed to resolve the real conflict.

### 7. Resolution
Force an irreversible choice that resolves the plot and proves character change.

The overall progression is:

**Setup → Commitment → Pressure → Reversal → Consequence → Final Insight → Resolution**
