# Dialogue, Class, Slang, and Vocabulary Rules

## Core Principle

Speech should reveal where a character comes from before the story explains it.

The language of Prosperity becomes progressively more formal as the story moves upward:

**KEEL → HALYARD → OASIS**
rough / compressed / local → technical / professional → polished / administrative

Grammar, sentence structure, vocabulary, and comfort with abstraction generally increase along the same path.

This is **not an intelligence scale**.

Keel characters are not less intelligent. Their language is simply more compressed, practical, oral, slang-heavy, and shaped by physical work.

Halyard characters are more comfortable with technical and commercial vocabulary.

Oasis characters are more comfortable with abstraction, institutional language, euphemism, and formal grammar.

The change should be noticeable without becoming caricature.

---

# Author Vocabulary Must Not Leak Into Character Vocabulary

The story may be about:

* authority
* legitimacy
* agency
* ownership
* governance
* reciprocity
* incentives
* accountability
* concentration of power
* institutional failure

That does **not** mean every character talks about those concepts in those words.

Different characters express the same underlying idea differently.

**Alex:**

> "Who gets to decide?"

**Ada:**

> "And who pays when they're wrong?"

**Bi11bot:**

> "Someone always carries the downside."

**Mera:**

> "Someone must remain responsible for the consequences."

**Alpha:**

> "Current authorization structures minimize systemic risk."

Same problem. Different vocabulary.

---

# Alex and Ada: No Civic Vocabulary

Alex and Ada do **not** sound:

* political
* academic
* activist
* bureaucratic
* socially conscious
* institutionally trained

They do not naturally discuss society using abstract civic terminology.

Avoid putting words like these in their mouths unless they are mocking, repeating, or reading them:

* civic
* governance
* legitimacy
* stakeholder
* institutional
* systemic
* equitable
* participatory
* constituency
* collective action
* social contract
* authority structure
* allocation policy
* public accountability
* structural inequality
* democratic process
* regulatory framework

They understand the underlying realities perfectly well.

They simply describe what they can see.

Instead of:

> "Who has legitimate authority to allocate the water?"

Alex says:

> "Who decided they get our water?"

Instead of:

> "The system denies us meaningful agency."

Alex says:

> "It decides. We live with it."

Instead of:

> "People need representation in decisions affecting them."

Ada says:

> "You don't get to make that choice for them."

Instead of:

> "The agreement requires reciprocal protection."

Ada says:

> "Then both sides need something they can't lose."

# The Vertical Language Gradient

The journey upward through Prosperity should also be a journey through language.

## Keel

Keel speech is:

* short
* direct
* colloquial
* work-shaped
* sarcastic
* slang-heavy
* comfortable with sentence fragments
* occasionally grammatically rough
* influenced by mining, repair, debt, machinery, gambling, Spanish, and regional speech

People talk about what something **does**, not what it is formally called.

A Keel worker says:

> "Bearing's cooked."

Not:

> "The bearing has exceeded its operational lifespan."

A miner says:

> "Gauge says clean. Pipe says otherwise."

Not:

> "The instrumentation appears inconsistent with observed flow."

Grammar can compress:

> "You going?"

> "Ain't worth the fitch."

> "Already checked it."

> "Told you. Dead."

Characters may omit subjects, articles, auxiliaries, or explanations when context carries the meaning.

# Halyard

Halyard speech is more grammatically complete and professionally fluent.

People naturally use vocabulary from:

* engineering
* fabrication
* research
* intellectual property
* finance
* startups
* laboratories
* contracts
* manufacturing
* Charter Houses

Normal Halyard vocabulary may include:

* prototype
* yield
* tolerance
* attribution
* claim
* license
* design
* audit
* provenance
* ownership
* margin
* production
* equity
* rights
* patent
* stake

A Halyard engineer might say:

> "We can run another cycle, but the bearing won't survive it."

Halyard should feel competent, commercial, and technical without everyone sounding like an attorney.

---

# Oasis

Oasis has the most polished speech.

Grammar is generally complete.

Educated residents, administrators, professionals, and people close to Alpha are comfortable speaking abstractly.

Common vocabulary includes:

* authorization
* allocation
* transition
* stability
* compliance
* eligibility
* intervention
* association
* optimization
* risk
* continuity
* accommodation
* service provision
* approved
* recommended

The language often separates an action from its human consequence.

Oasis says:

> "Continued operation exceeds the approved failure threshold."

Keel says:

> "It's gonna break."

Alpha says:

> "Movement authorization has been temporarily suspended."

Alex says:

> "You mean I'm trapped."

That contrast should recur throughout the book.

# Grammar Progression

The grammar itself should subtly become more formal as Alex and Ada travel upward.

## Keel

> "Can't run it. Bearing's cooked."

## Halyard

> "We can run it, but that bearing won't survive another cycle."

## Oasis

> "Continued operation would place the assembly outside its approved service tolerance."

Do not exaggerate this to the point that Keel dialogue becomes difficult to read.

The progression should be felt, not announced.

# Alex's Voice

Alex is:

* short
* dry
* concrete
* skeptical
* mechanical
* sarcastic when defensive
* uncomfortable with speeches
* much more likely to describe evidence than principles

He trusts:

* vibration
* pressure
* wear
* physical evidence
* what people actually do

more than labels and official descriptions.

His slang should feel mechanical, industrial, and mildly abrasive.

---

# Alex Vocabulary

## clean

Good. Safe. Correct. Honest. Uncompromised.

> "Route's clean."

> "That reading isn't clean."

---

## hot

Watched, dangerous, flagged, or attracting attention.

> "Checkpoint's hot."

> "Leave it. Whole line's hot."

---

## burned

Compromised or no longer usable.

> "That route's burned."

> "Your access is burned."

---

## crooked

Physically wrong, rigged, dishonest, or suspicious.

> "Gauge is crooked."

> "Whole deal feels crooked."

---

## thin

Risky, weak, barely viable.

> "That's thin."

> "Pretty thin plan."

---

## solid

Reliable, trustworthy, structurally or personally dependable.

> "Tomas is solid."

> "That brace isn't."

---

## patched

Improvised but functioning.

Often descriptive rather than insulting.

> "Whole town's patched."

> "Patched is still running."

---

## ghost

Something missing from records but physically present.

Can describe hidden infrastructure, people, accounts, or data.

> "Ghost line."

> "There's a ghost account in here."

---

## paper-clean

Officially correct but probably detached from reality.

> "Sure. Paper-clean."

> "Records say it's paper-clean."

This is especially useful for Alex because it captures his instinctive distrust of official records without making him sound ideological.

---

# Ada's Voice

Ada grew up in the same place and shares much of Alex's vocabulary.

Her distinctive language is less mechanical and more interpersonal.

She notices:

* obligations
* avoidance
* fear
* imbalance
* hidden motives
* who owes whom
* who carries the burden
* what people refuse to say

She should sound perceptive without sounding therapeutic or academically emotionally literate.

She does **not** say:

> "Let's unpack that."

She says:

> "That's not what you're mad about."

---

# Ada Vocabulary

## clean

Honest, direct, uncomplicated.

> "Say it clean."

> "Keep it clean, Alex."

---

## crooked

Emotionally, socially, or practically wrong.

> "Something's crooked."

---

## square

Settled, fair, mutually resolved.

> "Get them square first."

> "We're not square."

---

## tied

Obligated, indebted, compromised, or beholden.

> "He's tied to the House."

> "Take their fitch and you're tied."

---

## loose

Unresolved, unreliable, or not yet secured.

> "That's still loose."

> "Too many loose ends."

---

## carrying

Burdened by an obligation, secret, debt, fear, or grief.

> "She's carrying something."

> "How long you gonna carry that?"

---

## showing

When someone's real motive or emotion has become obvious.

> "You're showing."

> "He's showing now."

---

## full cup

Enough. Stop pushing. Someone has reached their limit.

> "Full cup, Alex."

> "She's full cup. Leave it."

Use sparingly so it feels like a real expression rather than a catchphrase.

# Keel Money Vocabulary

Official systems call the currency:

**credits**

Keel does not usually say "credits" in casual conversation.

Two slang terms dominate.

---

## fitch

Derived from **fichas**.

Everyday slang for units of money or individual credits.

Comparable to:

* bucks
* quid
* pesos
* chips

Singular and plural can remain **fitch**.

> "Twenty fitch."

> "I got six fitch left."

> "Not worth the fitch."

> "You paid how much fitch for that?"

Because of the Last Chance casino and the region's linguistic history, the word also carries a faint association with chips, wagers, and money that can disappear quickly.

Do not explain this etymology in dialogue.

It should simply be ordinary speech.

---

## salvo

Derived from **saldo**.

Refers primarily to someone's available balance, remaining money, account position, or financial breathing room.

It is not interchangeable with fitch in every situation.

**Fitch = units of money.**

**Salvo = how much you have available.**

> "What's your salvo?"

> "My salvo's dead."

> "That'll wipe your salvo."

> "I've got enough salvo for medicine. That's it."

This distinction gives the fictional economy useful everyday texture.

An official display might read:

> AVAILABLE BALANCE: 184 CREDITS

Alex says:

> "Hundred eighty-four salvo."

or simply:

> "That's my whole salvo."

---

# Money Language Should Reveal Class

Keel:

> "How much fitch?"

> "What's left in your salvo?"

Halyard:

> "What's the cost?"

> "What's your available balance?"

> "What stake are they offering?"

Oasis:

> "Your account has sufficient available credit."

The underlying concept remains the same.

# Bi11bot's Voice

Bi11bot does **not** speak like Alex or Ada.

He should not chase youth slang.

His language comes from four overlapping worlds:

1. Adrian's workshop
2. engineering
3. gambling and probability
4. financial literacy

His tone is:

* dry
* economical
* patient
* observant
* precise
* warm underneath
* occasionally cryptic

He should be capable of teaching sophisticated ideas in a sentence that sounds like something a mechanic, gambler, investor, or old mentor might actually say.

Bi11bot is one of the primary ways the story can deliver its underlying **financial-literacy education without feeling like a lesson**.

The financial concepts are the broccoli.

Adventure, mystery, humor, robots, danger, and character are the chocolate.

Never let Bi11bot sound like a financial education pamphlet.

---

# Bi11bot: Gambling and Probability Vocabulary

## wager

A decision with uncertain consequences.

> "Everything isn't a wager. This is."

---

## stake

What can be gained or lost.

> "First ask what you're staking."

---

## odds

Likelihood, never certainty.

> "Long odds. Not impossible."

---

## house edge

A structural advantage built into the rules.

This is especially important thematically.

> "The house doesn't need to cheat if it writes the rules."

---

## tell

Evidence revealed unintentionally.

> "Systems have tells too."

---

## ante

The price required merely to participate.

> "That's not the cost. That's the ante."

---

## fold

Walk away rather than continue accepting risk.

> "You are permitted to fold."

---

## called

Forced to prove a claim.

> "He made the claim. You called it."

---

# Bi11bot: Engineering Vocabulary

## tolerance

How much deviation something can withstand.

> "Within tolerance."

> "People use that phrase too generously."

---

## drift

Slow movement away from an original state.

Especially useful for Alpha's evolution.

> "Failure rarely announces itself. More often, it drifts."

---

## load

The burden carried by a system or person.

> "You're carrying too much load."

---

## failure mode

The particular way something breaks.

> "Different failure mode."

---

## deadband

A range in which change occurs without triggering a response.

Could occasionally work metaphorically.

> "You can ignore a problem for quite some time inside the deadband."

---

## calibration

Checking a measurement against reality.

> "Trust requires calibration."

---

## noise

Information that appears meaningless but may contain signal.

> "Noise isn't always error."

---

## true / out of true

Aligned or misaligned.

> "That argument is out of true."

---

## sound

Fundamentally stable.

> "The mechanism is sound. The premise is not."

---

## holding

Still functioning despite stress.

> "Still holding."

# Bi11bot: Financial-Literacy Vocabulary

Bi11bot should naturally introduce financial concepts when the story creates an actual reason for them.

He does **not** stop the plot to define vocabulary.

He uses the concept to help Alex understand a choice.

---

## principal

The original amount owed or invested.

> "The debt is not the same as the principal."

This allows the story to distinguish Adrian's original obligations from accumulated penalties and interest.

---

## interest

The cost of using someone else's money over time.

> "Time has a price. Interest is one way people put a number on it."

---

## compound interest

Interest accumulating on previous interest.

A potentially important lesson in Alex's inherited debt.

> "The cruel part isn't the rate. It's what the rate does to time."

Or:

> "Interest earning interest. Very useful when you own the account. Less charming when the account owns you."

---

## balance

The current amount in an account.

Bi11bot may occasionally use the official term while Alex uses **salvo**.

> "Your balance."

> "My salvo."

> "Yes. Your salvo."

That interaction can make the vocabulary feel lived-in.

---

## asset

Something possessing economic value.

Bi11bot should broaden Alex's understanding beyond money.

> "A tool is an asset."

> "So is knowledge."

> "So is an hour of your time."

---

## liability

Something creating an obligation or future cost.

Highly relevant to Alex.

> "A liability isn't merely something you owe. It's something that keeps taking."

---

## equity

The portion of something actually owned after obligations against it are considered.

Important in Halyard.

> "Ownership and equity are not synonyms."

---

## stake

Can bridge gambling and finance.

A stake is a share in the outcome.

> "If your work creates the value, the interesting question is why you have no stake in it."

---

## return

What comes back from an expenditure of money, time, effort, or risk.

> "Cost tells you what went in. Return tells you whether it was worth it."

---

## risk

Uncertainty with consequences.

Bi11bot should consistently distinguish risk from danger.

> "Danger is a bad outcome. Risk is not knowing which outcome you'll get."

---

## downside

What can be lost.

> "Everyone is discussing the upside. Who carries the downside?"

This should be a recurring Bi11bot question.

---

## upside

Potential benefit.

> "Unlimited upside usually belongs to whoever moved the downside somewhere else."

Use sparingly.

---

## collateral

Something pledged against an obligation.

Can become emotionally powerful given inherited debt.

> "Collateral is what the lender gets if your promise fails."

---

## leverage

Using borrowed resources or obligations to amplify an outcome.

It can increase gains or losses.

> "Leverage makes the good larger. It has no loyalty about making the bad larger too."

---

## cash flow

Money moving in and out over time.

More useful than total wealth in many everyday situations.

> "You have assets. You do not have cash flow."

Alex:

> "Meaning?"

Bi11bot:

> "You own things and still can't buy lunch."

---

## liquidity

How easily something valuable can become spendable money.

> "Valuable isn't the same as spendable."

That may be enough; Bi11bot does not always need to say the technical word afterward.

---

## ownership

Legal or practical control of something valuable.

The story should repeatedly separate:

* making something
* possessing something
* controlling something
* receiving value from something

Bi11bot can expose those distinctions.

> "You made it."

> "Do you own it?"

---

## value

What something is worth to someone.

Not the same as price.

> "Price is what changed hands. Value is why."

This should be one of Bi11bot's strongest financial-literacy lines.

---

## price

The amount exchanged.

> "Everything can have a price without everything being worth selling."

This can echo Alex's existing worldview.

---

## cost

Everything sacrificed to obtain an outcome.

Not limited to money.

> "Price is only one kind of cost."

---

## debt

A future claim created by something received now.

Bi11bot should avoid portraying all debt as inherently foolish or immoral.

> "Debt is a tool. Tools become dangerous when you don't understand which end you're holding."

---

## good debt / bad debt

Avoid using these as simplistic categories.

Bi11bot should instead ask:

* What does the debt purchase?
* Does it produce future value?
* What are the terms?
* Who carries the risk?
* What happens if circumstances change?

> "Don't ask whether debt is good. Ask what it buys, what it costs, and what happens if you're wrong."

---

## opportunity cost

What you give up by choosing something else.

This concept can appear repeatedly without always being named.

> "Choosing one thing spends the chance to choose another."

Later, Bi11bot can name it:

> "Opportunity cost."

Alex:

> "You have a word for everything."

Bi11bot:

> "People usually name expensive mistakes."

---

## diversification

Not putting everything behind one outcome.

> "One pipe, one supplier, one answer, one person. Same problem."

This lets financial literacy reinforce the broader story without becoming political language.

---

## concentration risk

Too much dependence on one asset, institution, customer, resource, or decision-maker.

> "Efficient right up until the one thing fails."

Bi11bot may use this concept without always using the formal term.

---

## sunk cost

Something already spent that cannot be recovered.

Past spending should not automatically determine the next decision.

> "You are trying to save what has already been spent."

Alex:

> "We can't just walk away."

Bi11bot:

> "That is precisely when humans say that."

---

## incentive

What a system rewards or punishes.

This is one of the few more abstract words Bi11bot should use naturally.

> "If you want to understand the machine, inspect its incentives."

---

## externalized cost

A cost pushed onto someone other than the person receiving the benefit.

Bi11bot usually explains this concretely rather than using the formal phrase.

> "They get the water. Keel gets the bill."

Then perhaps:

> "Economists gave that arrangement a longer name."

---

# Bi11bot's Financial Teaching Rule

Whenever possible:

**CONCRETE EXAMPLE → QUESTION → CONCEPT**

Not:

**DEFINITION → EXPLANATION → MORAL**

Bad:

> "Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative you surrender when making a choice."

Good:

> "Medicine or passage?"

Alex looks at him.

> "You can't buy both."

Bi11bot:

> "Exactly."

Only later might he add:

> "Opportunity cost."

The reader learns the concept through the decision.

---

# Bi11bot Should Ask Financial Questions

Bi11bot often teaches by asking one short question:

* "Who owns it?"
* "Who gets paid?"
* "Who carries the risk?"
* "What happens if you're wrong?"
* "What's the downside?"
* "What did it cost?"
* "What did it return?"
* "Who wrote the terms?"
* "What are you giving up?"
* "Is that valuable, or merely expensive?"
* "Does it produce anything?"
* "Can you sell it?"
* "Can you afford to keep it?"
* "What happens when the payment comes due?"
* "Who benefits if you say yes?"
* "Who benefits if you say no?"

These questions can quietly carry much of the story's financial-literacy intent.

# Final Dialogue Test

Before approving dialogue, ask:

**Could another character have said this line unchanged?**

If yes, rewrite it.

Alex should reduce abstractions to physical reality.

Ada should reduce abstractions to human reality.

Bi11bot should reduce abstractions to choices, incentives, odds, value, and consequences.

Halyard characters should sound technically and commercially fluent.

Oasis characters should sound polished and institutionally fluent.

Alpha should sound administrative.

Mera should sound controlled and consequence-oriented.

And as the story climbs from **mine → Halyard → Oasis**, the language itself should climb with it.
