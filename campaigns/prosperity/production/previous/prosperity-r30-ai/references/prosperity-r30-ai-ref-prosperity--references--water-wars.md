Yes. The key is to define **Water Wars as an era, not a war**. The scientifically credible mechanism is a global synchronization of otherwise local water crises: climate-driven hydrologic volatility collides with mining expansion, urban growth, agriculture, energy demand, and brittle allocation systems. IPCC assessments already describe increasing water insecurity through both scarcity and extremes, while the IEA notes that copper and lithium production are unusually exposed to water stress and that much of current production is concentrated in already stressed regions. ([IPCC][1])

I would answer the research-check roughly like this:

**ANSWER:** Treat the **Water Wars** as the retrospective name for the roughly two-decade period when water repeatedly became the binding constraint on ordinary life and industrial growth, not as a single interstate conflict. The 2030s mineral boom helps trigger the period because lithium and especially copper production expand in many already water-stressed regions while cities, agriculture, power systems, and ecosystems are competing for the same watersheds and aquifers. Climate change adds greater variability: longer droughts in some basins, extreme precipitation and flooding in others, reduced snow and glacier storage, groundwater depletion, and less predictable seasonal flows. `(references/triangle-water-wars.md)`

What links these events globally is **infrastructure and allocation failure**. Reservoir releases, wells, pumps, desalination plants, canals, mine-water systems, treatment plants, pipelines, diversion gates, and digital allocation records become politically consequential control points. A community does not need to conquer territory; it needs to close a gate, occupy a pumping station, block a mine road, cut a pipeline, falsify an allocation record, prevent repairs, or refuse an upstream release. Another group then tries to reopen or bypass it.

The resulting violence is usually **sub-war-scale but socially pervasive**: demonstrations, strikes, road blockades, occupations, sabotage, emergency rationing, lawsuits, police deployments, guarded infrastructure, black-market water, forced relocations, migration, and occasional lethal confrontations. Governments mostly retain sovereignty and national armies rarely fight one another over water. Most disputes remain among municipalities, provinces, utilities, mines, farmers, indigenous or community organizations, unions, migrants, regulators, and security forces.

That is why people later call them the **Water Wars**. The term describes a generation in which thousands of different disputes kept producing the same question: **who gets the water when there is not enough water, storage, pumping capacity, or political trust to satisfy everyone?** It functions more like a historical label for a period of recurring conflict than the name of one declared war.

The Covenant collapse should therefore be one remembered episode within this wider pattern: **one community closes a gate to protect its allocation; the downstream community forces it open; authorities intervene; people are hurt; an emergency agreement follows.** Similar events are happening for different reasons in different places, which is what makes the period feel global without requiring a global war.

The physical logic underneath that is quite strong. Water scarcity is often a **demand/availability mismatch**, not literally “there is no water.” ([IPCC][1]) That gives you much better storytelling because a basin can simultaneously contain a mine with water rights, a dry farming community, a city under restrictions, an ecological minimum-flow requirement, and a new desalination pipeline. Everyone can plausibly say *there is water* while disagreeing about who has the legal, physical, or moral right to move it.

The mineral boom is also a good historical accelerant rather than the sole cause. More than half of lithium and copper production has already been concentrated in high-water-stress areas, and declining copper ore grades can require more processing for a given amount of metal. Mining water systems also create highly visible infrastructure around which disputes can organize. ([IEA][2]) In Chile specifically, mining is already moving strongly toward seawater and desalination as continental water becomes constrained, which gives your 2030s world a believable mix of old freshwater rights and expensive new coastal water infrastructure. ([InvestChile Blog][3])

The thing I would **avoid** is saying climate change simply causes people to fight wars over water. The evidence is much more nuanced: climate and water shocks can increase instability, migration, and conflict risk, but governance, inequality, infrastructure, existing tensions, and institutions determine whether that turns violent. ([IPCC][4]) That nuance actually makes your version of the Water Wars considerably more believable.

A useful shorthand for the bible might be:

**Water Wars = climate volatility + exhausted infrastructure + competing allocations + mineral/urban demand + weak trust.**

And the characteristic “battlefield” is **the valve, gate, well, road, permit, meter, reservoir release, or allocation database**, not a national border.
