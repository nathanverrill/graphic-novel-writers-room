# Analysis — Prosperity, pages 1–64

I read the script cold, as the pages a student, a teacher, the partner and the backer would hold. I go by page number, and I mark it when I'm guessing beyond what the page shows ("inference").

**What the surface invites:** the first 22 pages read as a dystopia about a rigged system. The prologue's thieves say the hero "stole it first" (p2), and the granddaughter says "They say you made water pay for it" (p7). Nobody answers either line. Then "TODAY: DEBT TRANSFER DAY" hands a 17-year-old his father's 418,607 credits (p12), and an algorithm locks his door (p20). A teacher flipping through Issue 01 alone sees debt and pricing as the villain.

**What the story supports:** the villain is central control and falsified records, not trade. The fix is accurate measurement, recorded ownership and agreements anyone can challenge. Honest business pays the debt down (p44, p63).

**Who stops because of the surface:**
- A Young Americans staff member or a Denver teacher previewing only Issue 01 sees "debt as something done to you" (p12) before Issue 03 answers it.
- A progressive teacher who reads p1–10 may take the frame as "the hero owns the water and prices it" and never reach p44.
- Students are more likely to stop for clarity than for politics (see below).

**Does the book conclude, or leave a reader concluding, that free enterprise is bad? No.** Pages 44, 61 and 63 close on licensed sale, property records left untouched, and a priced repair agreed by the customer. One loose end: "Then he stole it first" (p2) and "made water pay for it" (p7) are never answered.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like:**
- Water is now on the page from panel one (p1–10), and Keel's channels and pump hall follow (p11, p13).
- Tomas's loan is set up like a real note: separate from the household account, owed in full, and still tracked at the end: "STATUS: REPAYMENT IN PROGRESS" (p23, p63).
- Alex pays for doing the right thing. Freeing Tomas costs him a wage: "−40 CR," and "You know that costs you." / "I noticed." (p24). That is integrity under pressure.
- Lock's offer to clear the debt (p40) and the executive debt clearance (p50, p58) are both refused. Nobody powerful cancels the debt.
- Issue 03 is the profit-and-IP story they asked for. The registry shows 312 patents (p39). The team fails, then iterates: "Smaller failure" (p42). The Seal records "who has to answer when it fails" (p44). The first sale splits revenue into debt, venture and warranty reserve (p44). A licensed shop sells to a customer who agrees the price (p63).
- Adrian's confession, "I borrowed too much… The mistakes were mine" (p19), is close to their founder's ethic.

**What would bother them:**
- **The credit mechanics.** They are a real bank. A debt transferred to a 17-year-old (p12) goes unexamined. The balance falls from 418,107 to 314,159 (p63), about 104,000 credits, and nothing shown on the page accounts for it. At 12 credits a unit (p44) that is roughly 8,700 purifiers, and the book doesn't say so.
- **The neighbours' 500 credits** (p35) pay his installment. Nobody says it will be repaid, and Alex's only response is "They bought the gate."
- **Who the villains are.** Caldera House reads as a company taking workers' IP (p39) and fiddling its numbers (p62). Nothing on the page marks it as a crony of Alpha rather than a business.
- **Still hard to follow in places:**
  - Ten pages of prologue with thieves who never return (p1–10).
  - Mera (p22) and Nera (p2) have nearly the same name.
  - "Tomas receives the old key's travel token and parts advance" (p23) reads backwards.
  - Riddle lines remain: "The work keeps accounts" (p7), "Badly, eventually" (p16), "Two of three. Again." (p50).
  - Pilar Veyra (p15) shares Ada's surname (p45) and is never explained.
- **The climax** (p57–61) is a civics resolution, not an enterprise one. Taking charge here means declining power, which they will respect, but it is not YouthBiz.
- **Alex never fails personally and owns it.** The team's prototype fails (p42) and Bi11bot is wrong (p25, p29). Alex is right almost every time.

**Likely verdict: partly.** This draft would clear the bar they set ("cannot ethically present"), with notes on credit accuracy and clarity (inference from their own notes).

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like:**
- Adrian says nearly Bill's own sentence: "I borrowed too much. I gambled some of it." (p19).
- No windfall solves the plot. Debt clearance is refused three times (p40, p50, p58). The debt is still mostly owed at the end (p63).
- **Ownership made concrete, de Soto style.** The creator's name "vanishes from the ownership field" (p39) until the Maker's Seal writes it down (p44). After that, the work becomes income (p44, p63). This is the book's strongest match to his beliefs.
- Central control is the villain, and "EMERGENCY AUTHORITY: RESCINDED" (p61) is a libertarian ending.
- The mentor is sometimes wrong: "I predicted we could leave him unseen." / "You were wrong." / "Yes. What would you do?" (p25), and "I read that wrong." (p29). He asks more than he tells: "Who carries the downside?" (p23).
- Bi11bot does not replace people. He mostly fades out after Issue 02. The resolution belongs to Ada, Tomas and the representatives (p57–62).
- "I'm showing up" (p64) echoes "Technology… cannot replace presence."

**What would bother him:**
- The neighbours' collection (p35) is a gift Alex never answers. "We don't believe in charity" is his line.
- The unexplained debt drop (p63) can look like a quiet windfall.
- He never gets his beer-hall scene. No young person says "I can't pay you" and works out terms with the lender. Tomas's "Took you long enough" (p63) is the nearest moment, and it is one line.
- p59–61 is a young man explaining the system to a room. That is a lecture, which he says he never gives.
- "TJ," if it is his Founder dog, appears only in 2099 (p1–9). The name is never explained and the dog is absent from the main story.

**Does Bi11bot sound like him? No, only in part.** By the profile's voice notes (plain, slow, roundabout, Western, question first, then a story from his own life, then one plain line, jokes at his own expense), Bi11bot gets the questions (p23, p25) and the fallibility (p29). He gets nothing else:
- He is clipped and arch, a dry butler-robot: "Then the wrongness travels efficiently" (p20), "B11 is acceptable if the room is on fire" (p18).
- His one joke is at Adrian's expense, not his own: "Adrian's jaw. His poorer timing." (p18).
- There is no story from his own life, no Western cadence, and no "damn hard."

**Likely verdict: partly.** The beliefs are honoured, but the voice that was supposed to be his isn't.

## A comic writer

**The hook:** the real hook is on p12: a 17th birthday, "DEBT BALANCE: 418,607 CREDITS — PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS," and "Happy birthday, Phantum." That is a strong first-issue hook, and it arrives on page twelve. The prologue (p1–10) is a nice robot-dog heist, but it belongs to an old man and two thieves who never come back. Its threads go nowhere: the blinking amber indicator (p7) and the missing leg (p9).

**Page turns:** the right/left plan is used well. The reveals land on left pages:
- p12, the debt
- p16, the bookcase
- p18, Bi11bot
- p32, the 62/18 numbers
- p50, the offer
- p54, Adrian
- p64, the final image

**Splash pages:** two are broken as written. p10 is called "FULL-PAGE IMAGE" and then given Panels 2 and 3. p57 is called "FULL-PAGE CIVIC HALL" and then given three more panels. An artist will send both back. The p64 full-width final panel works.

**Dialogue:**
- It is tight and has good moments: "I'm generous with containers" (p13), "Abandoned mines do not sweat" (p27), "You counted the pipe after you stopped counting the homes" (p62).
- But everyone speaks in the same wry two-beat line. Ada, Bi11bot, Alex, Lock and Tomas are hard to tell apart with the names covered.
- Names collide: Mera/Nera, Lina/Leona, and Ada/Adrian/Alex/Alpha.
- p44's roll call ("Lina: membrane chemistry. Suri: living biofilter…") is the writer talking, not Alex.

**What they would cut:**
- Cut p1–10 to three pages, or drop it.
- Cut p61, a page of nothing but displays.
- Fold p53 and p55 into one page.

**Would they take the job?** Yes, as a rewrite with a free hand on the prologue and the Issue 06 climax (inference). The skeleton holds: debt → Measure → build → Covenant → refusal → audit. It has a clean three-object structure and a good antagonist in Mera.

## A game designer

**Is there a game? Yes, and an obvious one.**

**The loop:** measure (find the gap between what's reported and what's delivered), expose (publish or route privately), build (combine contributors' parts, fail, iterate from 11% to 91%), license and sell, pay down the debt, unlock the next city.

**Choices that cost something:**
- Leave the zone to save Tomas and lose 40 credits (p24).
- The private route or the public one (p33).
- Sell the estate for debt clearance or keep it with commercial rights frozen (p40).
- Take executive authority (p50, p58).

All are real trade-offs with numbers attached.

**Systems a player would push on:**
- Can you game Alpha's allocation?
- The revenue split (p44): what happens if you cut the warranty reserve?
- The evidence-challenge mechanic (p62), which is a ready-made audit minigame.
- Contributor loyalty and compatibility (the sleeve bridging standards, p43).
- A debt meter that visibly ticks (p12, p35, p63).

**The weak spot:** the Covenant bands (p45–56) are a fetch quest with no system under them. "Two of three" is a key count, not a choice.

**Would they want to build it?** Yes. It sits close to the partner's own Young AmeriTowne simulation (inference).

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine:** a kid with an inherited debt, a dead-or-missing father's 312 patents (each one a locked door, so each one an episode), and a three-tier water world where the records lie. That is renewable.

**Characters an audience would follow for a season:**
- **Alex**, who needs a flaw. He is right too often.
- **Ada**, who has the best personal arc (p45–48).
- **Tomas**, a working-class wit and the series' conscience.
- **Mera**, the best character in the book: sympathetic, powerful, and once beaten by her own system. "I was the most powerful person in Oasis. I could not stop a door from closing." (p52).
- **Lock**, underused.

**Season-one cliffhanger:** Adrian behind the sealed bulkhead, "The image cuts before showing what happens beyond it" (p55). Also the 2099 frame: who sent the thieves, and what did the blinking component mean (p7)?

**What's missing:**
- A face for the threat in episode one. Mera gets two panels (p22).
- Personal stakes beyond a number.
- Any romance or friction between Alex and Ada.
- The p1–10 frame tells the audience Alex lives and wins a lake, which drains suspense.
- The finale is a public hearing (p57–62). That plays as a bottle episode, not a season ender.

**Would they see a series in the first issue?** Probably yes, on p12–19 (debt, the hidden room, the father's hologram). They would doubt it on p1–10.

## Does it smell like school

**Pages that exist to explain, or state the lesson:**
1. **p31**: the Measure's collar is engraved "WHAT IS NOT MEASURED CAN BE MADE INVISIBLE." The theme is printed on a prop.
2. **p47**: Leona narrates the Covenant's founding and the vote for emergency authority. It is backstory delivered straight to the reader.
3. **p59**: a **whole page** where Alex defines each safeguard ("It shows what's happening. It doesn't choose what matters." ×3). This is the book's glossary, read aloud.
4. **p60**: Alex sets out the procedure: "The affected districts choose their reviewers… Anyone affected can challenge the evidence."
5. **p61**: a **whole page** of system displays stating the civic outcome, with no characters acting.

**Borderline, not counted:** p20–21 (Bi11bot's one-line definitions of Alpha and the safeguards, which students need), and p44 panel 1 (the contributor roll call).

**Count: 5.** p59 is a whole page that exists to explain. So the book smells like school, but only in the climax, which is where a student reading the night before will judge it.

**Pages that teach without smelling of it, because people need the thing and pay for it:**
- p23 (Tomas's loan; medicine first in the budget)
- p24 (the wage forfeited for helping)
- p32 and p34 (62% vs 18%, the same thirty days)
- p35 (the installment arithmetic is exact)
- p39 (the creator bonus vs ownership)
- p42–43 (iterating, and an honest "not a guarantee")
- p44 (the revenue split and warranty reserve)
- p62 (an audit catches a doctored baseline)
- p63 (the posted price and the outstanding note)

This is the book at its best.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **A scene in Issues 03–05 where Alex himself misses a payment** (to Tomas, or on the household note), walks in, says he can't pay, and works out terms. *Satisfies Bill Reynolds (his beer-hall story) and Young Americans (failing, owning it, keeping your word).*
2. **Cut p1–10 to no more than four pages, and pay off or drop the thieves and the blinking component**, so Alex's birthday and debt land by page 5–6. *Satisfies students and the comic writer, and removes the unanswered "he stole it first" frame a progressive teacher could misread.*
3. **Replace p59–61 with the safeguards tested in action, the way p62 already tests them**: a representative tries to use each one wrongly and is caught. *Brings the school count down, and satisfies Bill (no lecture) and the students.*
4. **One page showing where the 104,000 credits came from** (units sold over time, wages, the purifier venture) **and the neighbours' 500 being repaid.** *Satisfies Young Americans as a real bank, and Bill (no windfall, no charity).*
5. **One scene establishing the Halyard Houses as chartered by Alpha's administration**, with privileges an honest shop doesn't have, set against the small Keel shop. *Satisfies Young Americans: cronies are the villains, not business.*

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: partly; smells like school: yes; school pages: 5
