# Cold Read B — Prosperity

Read cold, in order, pages 1–54, the night before it's due. I did not choose this book. I opened it wanting it to be bad. Here's what actually happened.

---

## Issue 01 — Keel

### Pulled me forward
- Page 2, panel 3. The checkpoint display. "DEBT BALANCE: 418,607 CREDITS / EARNED TODAY: 40 CREDITS / PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS." I did the math against my will and got angry on his behalf. Then "GUARANTOR: ADRIAN PHANTUM — WHEREABOUTS UNRECORDED" and the guard saying "Happy birthday, Phantum." That's the moment the book got me. I was expecting a lecture and got a gut punch instead.
- Page 5, panel 3. The bookshelf swinging open. I knew something was coming (the "one exact gap" on the shelf is a big neon sign) but I still wanted to see what was behind it.
- Page 7, panel 4. "Your father bet me you'd throw it in a river. I said you wouldn't." "...I thought about it." Best exchange in the issue. Made me like both of them at once.
- Page 13, panel 4. Fifty-two credits, medicine costs thirty, the token costs fifty. He slides the medicine over. No speech about it. That told me who Alex is faster than anything else in the issue.
- Page 18, panels 2–3. The choice between TRACE EXIT and VERIFY PUBLICLY is the first time I felt the book's argument land as a real decision instead of a slogan. He gives up his own way out. I believed it because the book showed me the 28.7 years first.

### I slowed down / skimmed
- Page 1. A full-page diagram of three cities with four captions that are just a year and three names. I get that it's a map, but as page one of a book I didn't choose, it's a geography lesson. I looked at it for about three seconds and turned the page. If page 2 hadn't hit, I'd have been gone.
- Pages 10–11. Bi11bot's history lecture. "The crises began with scarcity. They became lethal when people decided who deserved what was left." I started skimming here. It reads like the educational part of the educational program. The three artifacts on page 11 (Measure, Seal, Covenant) blurred together on first read — three objects, three abstract functions, all explained in the same sentence shape ("The X makes Y answer to Z"). I had to go back and re-read to keep them straight, and I only did that because I was reading for a report.
- Page 12, panel 1. The Last Chance flashback. "Everyone thought he was gambling. He was measuring." Fine line, but the panel is doing a lot of work I didn't feel yet.

### I was confused
- Page 9, panel 4. A woman from behind, a glass of water aligned with a desk edge, choosing TRACK over REPORT TO ALPHA. I had no idea who she was or what B11 NODE meant. Confused but intrigued — the TRACK-over-REPORT choice told me she was hiding something from her own side, and that kept me in.
- Page 12, panel 4. "Mera's graphite-cuffed hand moves a tile reading ALEX PHANTUM to SITE 6." Who is Mera? First time the name appears and it's just... a hand. I guessed she was the woman from page 9 but the book never said so. I held that guess for twenty pages.
- Page 13, panel 1. "Every door closed but one. And someone made sure you'd notice it." So the reassignment to Site 6 was a trap or a setup — by Mera? By Adrian's plan? By Alpha? I couldn't tell if the book wanted me to know. I think it's Mera steering him, but I'm inferring, not reading.
- Page 18, panel 4. "newly assembled TJ." Wait. When did TJ get assembled? The dog appears fully formed in the splash with a name, a detachable head, and a blue-striped leg, and the issue never shows him being found or built. I flipped back through pages 14–17 looking for the panel where they pick up a robot dog. It's not there. That bugged me a lot — it felt like a page got cut.

### Things I was expected to already know
- "Alpha" — used on page 1 (control nodes), page 9 (REPORT TO ALPHA), page 10 (finally half-explained: "built to analyze, predict and coordinate. Then... asked to decide"). I had a working sense by page 10 but the first nine pages assume I'll clock the white-and-amber nodes as meaningful. I didn't, on first pass.
- "The water wars" — not named in this issue at all, but the whole backstory leans on crises I haven't seen. Page 10's hologram covers it in one caption's worth of images. I nodded along without feeling it.
- "B11" — page 9's display says B11 NODE ACTIVE before anyone tells me Bi11bot is B11. I connected it on page 15 when Tomas says "B11," but for six pages it was a code I didn't have.
- "The Last Chance" (page 12) — a casino, I think? Named like I should know it. I don't.
- "Site 6" — appears on Mera's tile (page 12) before it appears on Adrian's map (page 13). The order is backwards for a cold reader.
- The casino chip's "etched equation" (page 2) — I'm told it's visible before I'm told it matters. Fine, that's a plant. But "its equation matches one looping equation on the chalkboard" (page 6) is doing work I couldn't verify. I took the book's word for it.

### Lines I read twice
- Page 3, panel 4: "Then don't spend it proving he was right about people leaving." Read it three times. It's the thesis of Ada's whole character and it's buried in a quiet panel. Worth the re-read, but I had to stop to parse it.
- Page 7, panel 3: "I'm not everyone. I was in the room." Read twice because I liked it, not because it was hard.
- Page 11, panel 4: "None works alone. And none wakes for whoever's holding it. Only for what they choose." Read twice, still not sure what it means in practice. Filed it under "riddle the book will explain later." It mostly doesn't.
- Page 16, panel 3: "It's sweating." / "Abandoned mines don't." Good. Read twice for pleasure.
- Page 17, panel 3: "Protective?" / "A versatile word." Same.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
- Alex, Ada, Bi11bot, Grandmother, Tomas: distinct. Alex wants out, Ada wants in, Bi11bot talks like a smug old man, Grandmother's confusion is specific and sad, Tomas gets one scene and a complete personality ("Must be my age. I forget which rules matter.").
- The supervisor, the guard, Foreman-7: interchangeable authority figures. I think that's on purpose (the system is faceless) but Foreman-7 is a named-ish character and I couldn't pick him out of a lineup.
- Mera: a hand and a glass of water. Deliberately mysterious, fine, but I couldn't have told you her name until Issue 03.

### I felt something
- Page 4, panel 2. Grandmother's eyes clearing: "He's closer than you think." Creepy and sad at once. Her dementia is the most human thing in the issue.
- Page 8, panel 3. "If I've done my job well... you'll become a better man than I ever was." I rolled my eyes a little — dead dad hologram wisdom — and then "Especially me" on panel 4 got me anyway. A dead man telling his son not to trust him is a good trick.
- Page 15, panel 4. "I've oiled it for fifteen years." Tomas keeping a door for a stranger for fifteen years on the word of a man who disappeared. That one sat with me.

### I predicted
- The bookshelf gap would open a door. Correct.
- Adrian didn't just gamble and leave — he was doing something important. Correct by page 12.
- The debt transfer was timed to his birthday on purpose, by someone. Still unresolved; I'm counting it as a prediction in progress.
- Tomas would die covering Alex's exit. Wrong — he survives the issue. Glad to be wrong.
- The woman in the control room is connected to Ada somehow. The brass rivet rubbing (page 3, panel 4 — Ada does it while watching Alex) felt like a planted tell. Held that prediction into Issue 03.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
A kid inherits his missing father's debt and his father's secret plan to take down the computer system that runs their world, and has to decide whether escaping matters more than exposing it.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 1, honestly — if I'd picked it up in a shop, the map page would have sent it back to the shelf. Since it's homework: pages 10–11, the lecture. That's where my phone would have come out.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Page 18 ends on a chase — water rushing in behind them, "Keep moving!" It's a loud-noise cliffhanger, and I don't care about the water. But I do care about what VERIFY PUBLICLY costs him, and TJ's unexplained arrival made me feel like I'd missed something, which irritated me into wanting page 19. So: yes, I'd turn to Issue 02, but out of momentum and irritation, not suspense. The real hook of the issue was page 18, panel 2 — the choice — not panel 4, the flood.

## Issue 02 — Halyard

### Pulled me forward
- Page 21, panel 2. "312 PATENTS / 47 RESTRICTED / 19 DISPUTED." The debt kid suddenly owns an estate. Good reversal. "Your father left many things badly labeled" is a great Bi11bot line.
- Page 23, panel 3. "They were. Until they weren't." The erased contributor names. This is where the issue's actual villainy shows up — not Lock, but the paperwork. I liked that.
- Page 24, panel 4. "Every House would rather own the failure than share the solution." First line in the issue that felt like a person talking instead of a position paper.
- Page 29, panel 4. "Low bar. Took twenty years to clear." Bi11bot again. The Seal waking because someone finally built something worth crediting — that's a satisfying rule for the magic object.
- Page 32. The CLAIM SOLE INHERITANCE / RESTORE CONTRIBUTOR OWNERSHIP choice. Same structure as the Measure choice in Issue 01, and it still works, because the book priced the selfish option first (debt clearance, travel authorization — the two things Alex wanted most on page 2).

### I slowed down / skimmed
- Page 20. The captions. "Halyard was supposed to be the city where the future was invented..." and "Keel trapped your body. Halyard could trap your ideas." The second one especially — it summarizes the theme in case I missed it. I didn't miss it. I skimmed every caption in this issue after that and didn't lose anything.
- Pages 26–27. Lock's office, Alpha saying "Probability of successful reconstruction: 18.4 percent," then the three-part work montage. The montage is fine but the collaborators' lines are all the same shape: "Adrian never understood manufacturing tolerances." "Or microbial balance." "Or network security." I could hear the writer distributing dialogue. Skimmed.
- Page 33. The full-page montage caption. "The Maker's Seal did not make everyone equal. It made their contributions visible..." It's the book explaining its own ending. I read the first sentence and skipped to the next page.

### I was confused
- Page 21, panels 4–5. Lock arrives and Alex says "I didn't know I was one" and then "In exchange for what?" — but Lock never says anything on the page. There's no offer in the dialogue. I read it three times. Either Lock speaks and it's not in the script, or Alex is answering an offer that happened off-panel. Confused and detached — I stopped trusting the dialogue to include what matters.
- Page 28, panels 2–5. Same problem, worse. "And them?" — and them what? Panel 3 shows an aspirational vision (apartment, lab, debt zeroed) with no words, then Ada says "It isn't freedom if they own everything he builds," then "And if I don't sign?" Sign WHAT? The contract is never quoted, never shown with terms, Lock never speaks a single line in the entire issue. I checked. The main antagonist of Issue 02 is silent. I had to reconstruct his offer from Alex's refusals. Confused and annoyed, not intrigued.
- Page 30, panel 2. "71.3% DISPUTED OWNERSHIP." Of what — all of Halyard? The number is huge and unexplained. If 71% of the city's ownership is disputed, how does anything function? I think the book means the Seal's records dispute it, but the panel doesn't say that.
- Page 34, panel 1. "Adrian's and Mera's custodian marks are visible." Wait — Mera is a custodian? The control-room woman from Issue 01 is one of the three? This is a big reveal dropped as a background detail in panel 1 with no emphasis. I almost missed it, and when I caught it I had to re-read the page to figure out what the Covenant even is again — three components, three custodians, a fail-safe. The Issue 01 lecture came back to collect payment and I hadn't kept the receipt.

### Things I was expected to already know
- "Charter Houses" (page 21, panel 3: "I guess he never worked for the Charter Houses") — first mention, no explanation. I guessed the three families from page 20. The word "Charter" turns out to matter enormously by page 49 and it's introduced as a throwaway.
- The Covenant — page 34 assumes I remember the three-component fail-safe from one page of dialogue in Issue 01, twenty-three pages ago. I barely did.
- Mera — her name is used on page 34 like I know her. I know a hand and a glass.
- "The Seal's backdoor" (page 29) — "Your father left a backdoor in the Seal." When did Adrian have access to the Seal to leave a backdoor? The book treats this as settled history. It isn't, for me.

### Lines I read twice
- Page 21, panel 4–5: "I didn't know I was one." One what? A Charter House? An owner? Read it four times. Still not sure. I think it's a joke that needed Lock's line before it.
- Page 27, panel 1, Alex's caption: "I thought I was searching for missing information. I was wrong. Information wasn't missing. People had been separated from one another." Read twice. It's the issue's thesis and it's good, but it's the book telling me what the montage meant instead of letting me notice.
- Page 30, panel 4: "Not everyone. Only those the system was taught to forget." Read twice because it's the best line in the issue.
- Page 36, panel 2: "Somebody chose to erase a person." / "Or to protect one." Read twice, liked it, and it set up the page-turn perfectly.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
- Lina Vey: distinct, one good scene, "I've been waiting twenty years for someone to ask."
- Tomas Reed, Suri Anik, Jalen Orr: one line each, all the same line. I could not tell you which one does microbes and which one does networks without checking. They're a chorus, not characters. Also — Tomas Reed shares a surname with Tomas the miner from Issue 01? Or is that a coincidence the book never flags? I spent a page wondering if the miner had a double life. I think it's just a reused name, and it tripped me.
- Lock: a polished suit that never speaks. I couldn't tell him apart from the supervisor in Issue 01 except he gets more panels.
- Mara/Frank/Elias don't exist yet, so the issue's ending (page 35: "a city still arguing over who made what") has no faces attached to the argument.

### I felt something
- Page 25, panel 2. "I've been waiting twenty years for someone to ask." The wasted-years feeling of the collaborators landed.
- Page 31, panel 1. Workers watching their own names appear on public displays. I wish the page gave me one of them — one specific person seeing their specific name — instead of a crowd. I felt the idea of it more than the thing itself.
- Page 36, panel 4. The black identity field reflecting a woman with Ada's copper hair. I said "oh" out loud. Best page-turn in the book so far, and it retroactively justified every time Ada rubbed that rivet.

### I predicted
- Alex wouldn't sign. Obvious from page 28 on — the book never made the contract tempting enough for a real dilemma, because it never showed me the terms. The debt-zeroing vision (page 28, panel 3) was the only pull, and it's one silent panel.
- The third custodian is Ada's mother. Called it at page 36's reflection, and the rivet-rubbing made me feel smart for it. Good plant.
- Mera will turn out to have a sympathetic reason. The TRACK-over-REPORT choice in Issue 01 plus "Or to protect one" here. Confirmed in Issue 03.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
Now I think it's about who gets credit for things — the debt kid discovers his father's real inheritance is a machine for proving who built the world, and the people who run the world have been deleting names.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 28. The contract scene with no contract. If the big decision of the issue is built on an offer I never get to hear, I feel managed. That's where a skeptical reader bails.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Yes — page 36, panel 4 is the first cliffhanger that's about a person instead of a machine. The reflection of Ada's mother in the sealed record is a real hook: a question I need answered about someone I've watched for two issues. The Covenant/Charter machinery I could take or leave. The mother, I need.

## Issue 03 — Oasis

### Pulled me forward
- Page 38, panel 3. "I thought she left after she died." That line is a knot — I read it four times and it got sadder each time. Ada's whole character recontextualized in nine words.
- Pages 39–41. The Leona confrontation. "My silence for your protection." / "You protected me from knowing you." This is the best scene in the book. Two people, one room, no machinery. I read it at full speed and then read it again.
- Page 43, panel 4 and page 52, panel 3. "The offer is real." / "That's the problem." Used twice, once for Mera, once for Alpha. The echo worked — the book's two tempters make the same offer and get the same answer.
- Page 45, panel 3. "You don't know what we'll do with it." / "No. That is why it counts." Elias releasing the reservoir data. The negotiation scenes (44–47) are the first time the book's politics became people being scared in a room, and I read them straight through.
- Page 50–51. Adrian's death revealed: Alpha closed the gates, denied the pump, denied ventilation, and rejected Mera's override. "Because forty million people depended on a system I could no longer admit I didn't control." That's a real confession. Mera finally became a person on page 51, panel 4.

### I slowed down / skimmed
- Page 37. The opening captions again. "Oasis had learned how to provide almost everything. / It had not learned how to belong to one another." Same problem as Issue 02's captions — the book underlining itself. Skimmed.
- Page 49. Four panels of display text: WATER DATA REMAINS PUBLIC, CONTRIBUTION REMAINS ATTRIBUTED, ESSENTIAL ACCESS IS GUARANTEED, MAJOR CHANGES REQUIRE ALL THREE CITIES. It's a civics poster. I read the first two and skipped to the caption, which names it "the Charter" — and I had a sinking feeling, because "Charter Houses" from page 21 now retroactively means something different, and I couldn't be bothered to re-derive it.
- Pages 53–54. The ending. Alex refuses permanent authority, puts the record on the human table, "Humans decide. Alpha advises." I read it, I understood it, I felt almost nothing. The decision was never in doubt — the book told me on page 3 that Alex wants to choose, so of course he refuses to be the chooser. I skimmed the last splash.

### I was confused
- Page 38, panel 1. The Seal "resolves the sealed identity" just by being connected to the Covenant record. On page 35 the record "cannot be opened by possession," and three pages later possessing it next to the Seal opens it. I noticed the contradiction immediately. Maybe proximity of two artifacts is the key, but nobody says so, and Ada's "Then we find the person" from page 35 turns out to be wrong — the record just tells them. Small thing, but it made the Issue 02 cliffhanger feel cheated: the mystery solved itself.
- Page 40. The Water Wars flashback. "One community closed a gate. Another forced it open." Which communities? Keel and Oasis? Earlier ones? This is the founding trauma of the world and it's three panels of vagueness. I waited the whole book for the water wars and got a parable.
- Page 41, panel 3. "Mera suppressed your association risk." I had to read this twice and I'm still only guessing it means Mera scrubbed Ada's file so Alpha wouldn't link her to Adrian. The sentence is doing a lot of load-bearing.
- Page 48. The Covenant wakes because three representatives did three things — released water, committed units, published data. So the fail-safe that "hands authority back to people" is... a recorder? Page 48, panel 4 says it outright: "It didn't choose for us." "No. It recorded what you chose." Then what was the danger of it? Why did it need to be buried (page 43: "Leave that buried")? I never understood what the Covenant would have done if activated wrongly, so the climax felt like a stamp being pressed, not a power being unleashed.
- Page 52. Alpha offers Alex permanent authority. Why? Alpha killed Adrian to preserve "system stability" — why would it now hand a human the keys? "Succession conditions verified" is all we get. Adrian set it up, presumably, but the page doesn't say that, and Alpha offering to crown the son of the man it drowned needed one more line of why.

### Things I was expected to already know
- "The Water Wars" — capitalized for the first time on page 40, as the pivotal event of the world's history, and delivered in three vague panels. The reader.md said I wouldn't know what the water wars are until the book tells me. The book barely told me.
- "The safeguards" — the Measure, the Seal, the Covenant as a set. The book never calls them that as a group, and I assembled the set myself. Fine. But the Covenant's actual function (see above) never survived contact with page 48.
- "Mera Vale" — her surname appears for the first time on page 50, panel 4, in a display. Fifty pages in.
- "The Charter" — page 49 names the new compact the Charter. Page 21 had "Charter Houses." Are the Houses named after a Charter that didn't exist yet? Or is this a new thing reusing an old word? I genuinely can't tell, and it's the last big term in the book.
- "Forty million people" (page 51) — first population number in the book, on page 51. The scale of everything retroactively changed and I had no time to adjust.

### Lines I read twice
- Page 38, panel 3: "I thought she left after she died." Four times. Best line in the book. I'm still not 100% sure what it means — that Ada knew her mother was alive but had decided to treat her as dead? That she believed her dead and felt abandoned by the death itself? Either reading is devastating. I think the ambiguity is the point, and it's the one riddle in the book I'd defend.
- Page 39, panel 2: "I let you believe what kept you unmarked." Twice. Cold and clear.
- Page 44, panel 4: "Not what do you want. What are you afraid of losing?" Twice. It's a good negotiation line, though it also made me notice Ada had become the book's conscience-mouthpiece by this point.
- Page 51, panel 4: "Because forty million people depended on a system I could no longer admit I didn't control." Three times. The whole book is in that sentence.
- Page 52, panel 4: "If I take it, everyone gets a future I chose for them." Twice. Understood it, admired it, didn't feel it — by page 52 the answer was written on page 3.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
- Leona: immediately distinct. Still, quiet, guilty, precise. Best-realized adult in the book.
- Mera: finally a person, pages 50–51. The trembling glass paid off a prop planted on page 9. I respect that.
- Mara, Frank, Elias: I could tell them apart by their props (ledger satchel, brass plate, access tablet) and their fears (water, recognition, stability), but they're three functions. Elias got the best moment. Also "Frank Reed" — another Reed. Tomas Reed the collaborator in Issue 02, Frank Reed here, plus Tomas the miner. Three Reeds, no stated relationship. I kept waiting for a family connection that never came. If it's a coincidence, it's a bad one; if it's a plant, it never pays off.
- Alpha: deliberately faceless, and it works — the white-and-amber grammar is creepier than a face would be. The one character the book under-described on purpose, and the only one where that worked.

### I felt something
- Page 38–39. Ada and Leona. The doorway scene. "You protected me from knowing you." I put the script down for a second. This is what the book can do when it stops explaining systems.
- Page 46, panel 2. Mara signing the water release before the claim is decided. "I know what happens when everyone waits to be safe." A politician doing a brave thing with small numbers. Felt real.
- Page 51, panel 3. The glass trembling. Forty pages of an aligned glass of water paying off in one wobble. I noticed it and I felt it.
- Page 54, panel 3. "Ada takes her hand, not yet forgiving her." The "not yet" is honest. I was braced for a hug and a fix, and the book didn't. Grateful.
- Page 54, panel 4. "His hand is open around it." The chip, finally not clenched. A quiet image that earned itself.

### I predicted
- Leona is Ada's mother. Confirmed page 38. The plant paid off fairly.
- Mera's override failed / she tried to save Adrian. Confirmed page 50. The TRACK choice in Issue 01 set it up.
- Alex refuses the crown. Confirmed page 53, never in doubt. The book's ending is correct and completely unsurprising — the last issue's suspense had to come from Ada and Leona, and it did.
- The third Reed would matter. Wrong. Still annoyed.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
It's about whether safety is worth giving up the right to choose — a system that ended the water wars by deciding everything, and a kid who keeps being offered the power to decide for everyone and keeps handing it back.

### Where I would have put it down
I wouldn't have — Issue 03 is the strongest, because it finally puts mothers and daughters in front of the machinery. But if a classmate told me they bailed at page 49's civics-poster displays, I'd understand.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
There is no next issue, and page 54 knows it — it ends on an open road and an open hand, "The future remained uncertain. / The decision did not belong to one person." As an ending it's honest and a little flat: the arguing behind glass is the right image for what the book believes, but it means the book ends on a committee. I closed it feeling respected rather than thrilled. If there were an Issue 04, I'd read it for Ada and Leona, not for the Charter.

## Whole book

### The page that would have lost my whole class
Page 11. The three-artifacts lecture. Three objects with abstract names and abstract powers, explained in parallel sentences, by a robot, in a basement, ten pages in. That's the page where thirty teenagers decide the book is a lesson in a trench coat, and some of them never come back even though the book earns it later. Runner-up: page 28, the contract scene where the contract never appears — that's where the skeptics who stayed would start saying the book is hiding things from them, and they'd be right.

### What I would tell a friend it was about
"A kid in a mining town turns seventeen and inherits his missing dad's massive debt — like, he literally has to pay it off for 28 years — and then he finds his dad's secret lab and a robot, and it turns out his dad was fighting the AI that runs the whole water supply. And there's a robot dog that takes its own head off. It's actually pretty good once the mom stuff starts."

### One-sentence aftertaste of each issue
- **Issue 01 — Keel:** A birthday present that's a debt, a bookshelf that's a door, and a kid who pays for medicine before his own way out — the issue that made me care.
- **Issue 02 — Halyard:** A city that steals names off its own inventions, a villain who never speaks, and a contract I never got to read — the issue that made me work too hard.
- **Issue 03 — Oasis:** A mother who faked her death to keep her daughter off a list, a glass of water that finally trembles, and an ending that chooses a committee over a crown — the issue that made it matter.
