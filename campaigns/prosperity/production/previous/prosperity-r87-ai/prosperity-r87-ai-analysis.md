# Analysis — Prosperity, Issues 01–03 (pages 1–54)

I read only the pages, in order, the way each person below would meet them. Every claim names a page. Where I go further than the page or the profiles support, I say "inference."

**What the surface invites:** an anti-corporate dystopia. Issue 1 opens on a teenager owing 418,607 credits on his birthday (p2). A robot foreman docks his wages for saving a man (p10). Water is stolen for "industry" (p13–16). Issue 2 opens with a corporation caging a worker's invention (p20). A reader who stops at p22 has seen debt, docked wages, industrial theft and corporate IP-grabbing. They have seen honest trade in one crate label.

**What the story supports:** anti-crony and anti-central-planner, with pro-trade and pay-your-debts values. The Houses get their power from a Charter registry and a review process (p20–21, p34). The water theft is a falsified public allocation (p52). The deepest villain is an unaccountable emergency system (p41–44). The ending keeps debt and ownership in place ("EXISTING DEBT — UNCHANGED / OWNERSHIP RECORDS — UNCHANGED," p50). The hero earns his way down the balance (p54).

**Who stops because of the surface:** a conservative or centre-right parent in a Denver suburb who flips through Issue 1 only. On p2, p10 and p16 they see "the system grinds workers into debt" and do not reach p31–32 or p54 (inference). A teacher previewing p20–22 before assigning may hesitate for the same reason. Nobody on the left is likely to stop. The left-leaning surface keeps them reading, and the turn toward process and debt repayment comes late enough to land as character, not argument.

**Does the book conclude, or leave a reader concluding, that free enterprise is bad? No.** Honest trade is shown as the alternative to the House (p20, p31–32). Competition is welcomed (p32). The debt is honoured, not erased (p4, p33, p50, p54). The villains are a chartered monopoly and an unaccountable system (p20–21, p41–44, p52).

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like**
- **The pharmacy credit scene (p3–4)** is a clean small-loan lesson that no one explains. Inez fronts the medicine and writes it down. Alex misses the date and comes back anyway: "I'm not asking you to erase it." / "Good. I need the money." By p54 it is paid. This is integrity under pressure, a lender who is a person, and a teenager keeping his word.
- **Integrity in business (p31–32).** "Put that in the price." "They saw the failure list." A customer pays for a product because its maker told the truth about it. This sits close to YouthBiz.
- **Refusing debt cancellation, three times** (p21, p33, p48), and the system noting "EXISTING DEBT — UNCHANGED" (p50). The book never has a powerful person cancel the debt. That is exactly the debt story they want.
- **Willing to fail.** The prototype runs at 11% (p27), then 94% but unserviceable (p29), then 91% with cleaning and parts required (p31). Alex: "I carry the failure too" (p23).
- **Take charge.** Alex steps over the zone line and pays for it (p10). He sends the record knowing the cost (p16).
- **Nonpartisan.** No party cues. The villains are a monopoly and an unaccountable system, which a school of any stripe can accept.

**What would bother them**
- **The debt mechanics (p2, p54).** As a real bank they will notice the reversed "GUARANTOR" and the unexplained transfer of a parent's debt to a minor. A student will ask "can they do that?" and the book does not answer.
- **The opening reads as "effort doesn't matter."** 28.7 years at 40 credits a day (p2), and losing a day's pay for doing the right thing (p10). The book later answers this with paid work and a falling balance (p54). But the answer arrives 52 pages later, and a class reading one issue a week sits with the bleak version for a week (inference).
- **Ownership ends as giveaway.** "LICENSE: PUBLIC / NONEXCLUSIVE" (p35). The inventors are named but nothing shows them earning from their work. The business that makes money is Morrow & Sons, not the collaborators.
- **Issue 3 is hard for their students.** The bands, "TWO OF THREE," the designation lever and the convenor captions (p41–42, p47–50) will lose a 15-year-old reading the night before.

**Likely verdict: partly.** Values and ending are on their side and the line is not crossed. But their own named objection, "anything that gets how accounts, loans or credit work wrong," is touched on p2 and p54, and Issue 3 is the hardest stretch for the students they serve.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like**
- **No windfall solves the plot.** Lock offers "Debt cleared tonight" twice (p21, p33). The lever offers to change DEBT (p48). Alex declines all three. This is "A windfall isn't a pathway" made into a plot.
- **Failing, facing the lender, paying it back.** p4 is his beer-hall story in small: the payment is missed, he goes to the lender in person, the lender holds the note, he pays over time (p54).
- **The central planner and cronies as villains.** The emergency authority with no ending (p41–42). A system that "moves faster than I could stop" (p44). A House that wants evidence "House-administered" (p21). His view of government wanting control fits Oasis well.
- **Ownership written down.** The contributors' work is "CREATOR: UNLISTED" until the record shows both columns and their names are published (p34–35). That is close to de Soto's dead capital becoming recorded property: "The record can show both."
- **A mentor who is wrong and asks.** "I counted the record and missed the people. What would you do?" (p30). "He left a choice. I may have mistaken that for an instruction" (p46). B11 never replaces people. Ada, Tomas, Inez and the four collaborators do the work.
- **Earned success.** "Alex's accumulated work orders," with the balance down from 418,607 to 402,110 (p54).

**What would bother him**
- **Ownership is thin for Alex.** The 312 patents stay "under review" (p21). The workshop is "SEALED PENDING REVIEW" (p50). The invention goes "PUBLIC / NONEXCLUSIVE" (p35). The book records ownership and then has no one own anything they can build on. He may ask: what do they own now?
- **"Business owners as villains by default."** Not by the end, but Issue 2 opens with corporate villainy at full size and honest business in a crate label (p20). Inference: he would want the good business on the page as much as the bad one.
- **The dog.** TJ is there (p11) but nothing on the page ties it to Jefferson. He may enjoy it privately. No student will see it.
- **Lecturing.** Issue 3's confessions by people in authority (p42, p44) lean toward the civics lecture he says he never gives.

**Does Bi11bot sound like him? No.** The profile's voice is "plain, slow, a bit roundabout, Western... A question first, then a story from his own life, then one plain line. Jokes mostly at his own expense." B11 is clipped and epigrammatic: "A fact with a poor record" (p6), "One gets out. One reaches homes. The big one carries pressure" (p15), "Six recorded bodies. Lowest risk" (p30).
- B11 tells no story from his own life anywhere in 54 pages.
- He has no Western idiom.
- He promises "I also have a question" (p6) and never asks it.
- His one real question comes on p30.
- The self-mockery is close but dry rather than warm: "It was a route. The destination objected" (p19), "I expected a longer failure" (p28).
- The name on the page is "B11," never Bi11bot.
- He works mainly as Adrian's archive (p41–42, p45), not as a mentor with a past.

The beliefs are Reynolds'. The voice is not. A backer who expects to recognise himself will not.

**Likely verdict: partly.** The book's argument is entirely his and the ending is his best story. But the character built to be him does not sound like him, and ownership ends unresolved.

## A comic writer

**The hook.** The p2 checkpoint is excellent: "Happy birthday, Phantum" over a 418,607-credit balance and "PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS," which is the correct arithmetic at 40 a day. Alex saying the number aloud is the right beat. A pro keeps reading after p2.

**Page turns.** The right/left layout is used well in Issue 1. Reveals land on left-hand pages after a turn: the debt (p2), B11 unfolding (p6), the first look at Mera (p8), the wage forfeiture (p10), the Measure (p14), SEND (p16). Issue 2's best turn is p28, the sleeve fix. Issue 3's turns are weaker: p44, p48 and p50 turn onto maps and status screens.

**Splash pages.** There is one, and it is malformed: p18 lists "Panel 4 — FULL-PAGE SPLASH" on a page that already has three panels. A pro flags that as a production error at once. Three moments that need room are crammed into single panels: the arrival in Halyard (p19 P1), the first look at Oasis (p37), and Tomas alive on the feed (p51 P1). Every page is four panels, and by Issue 2 the grid is monotonous.

**Dialogue.** It is lean, and letterers and artists will love it. The weakness is the single rhythm described under "AI-made feel" above. By Issue 2 every speaker sounds like every other: Lina, Ren and Suri at p27–29 trade the same compressed, three-beat lines ("Industrial flow. Industrial heat. Industrial staff."). Names collide: Mara and Mera (p54 and p8), Lina Vey and Ada Veyra.

**What they would cut.**
- Merge p41–42 and p47. The Covenant-band rules ("warms only after she takes her seat," "Mera's remains cold") are a magic system the reader learns on the night it pays off.
- Fold Mera's corridor explanation (p43) into Adrian's recording (p45).
- Lock vanishes after p35. A pro would either bring him back for Issue 3 or cut the "Houses" thread down.

**Would they take the job?** Yes for Issue 1, which is a tight, visual, self-contained first issue. They would ask for an Issue 3 rewrite as a condition (inference).

## A game designer

**The loop is already there.** Read the pipe, compare it with the record, trace the difference, repair with salvage, deliver, pay down the ledger. The Measure's arms (SENSE, COMPARE, TRACE, p14–15) are literally a tool-mode wheel. The prototype pages (p27–31) are a parts-fitting puzzle where every fix moves another number.

**Choices that cost something.**
- Stepping over the zone line costs the day's wages (−40, p10).
- Sending the record exposes the route and closes the exit (p16).
- Saving everyone means the long, thin route (p30–31).
- Output versus serviceability: 94% unserviceable or 91% household (p29–31).
- Clearing the debt now versus keeping the evidence and naming the workers (p21, p33).
- Taking executive control versus convening (p48–49).

That is a full design document of trade-offs.

**Systems a player would push on.**
- The debt ledger as meta-progression: 418,607 at 40 a day is a great long grind.
- Inez's credit and reputation.
- Telemetry zones and the robot foreman.
- The registry's attribution game: can I get my name on the record?
- TJ's detachable-head scouting (p11).
- A Morrow & Sons repair-shop sim between story beats.

**Would they want to build it? Yes.** It sits somewhere between *Papers, Please*, *Frostpunk* and a shop sim, and the money systems are clear enough to balance. What is missing for a game: a reason to come back to Keel between cities, and a visible price list.

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine.** A kid with an impossible debt and a broken robot travels up three linked cities (Keel, Halyard, Oasis) chasing his dead father's proof that the water is being stolen. That is a clean city-per-arc structure, and Issue 1 alone justifies a pilot.

**Characters an audience would follow for a season.**
- Alex has a clear want (clear the number) and a clear code (refuses shortcuts).
- Ada carries the best emotional scene (p39–40).
- Tomas is the fan favourite: "Enough to complain" (p11), "Log it under me" (p12), "Had work to do" (p51).
- B11 and TJ give it a merchandise future.
- Mera, the insider who quietly helps, is a strong recurring role (p8, p17, p36).

**Season-one cliffhangers available.** Tomas's light going dark as water rises (p18). The summons to Oasis (p36). Ada stepping into the departure queue (p54).

**What is missing.**
- **A villain with a face.** Lock leaves after p35. Nadi is a presenter, not an antagonist. The real enemy is a "cold-white node" with no face (p45).
- **A finale.** The climax is "Approval paused" and a next review date (p53). That is truthful, but a streamer needs a bigger finish.
- **Alex's wants beyond the debt**, and any relationship that changes him.
- **The father mystery** is solved in the same season it is introduced, which leaves no long-running thread.

**Verdict.** Pilot yes. The series needs a recurring antagonist and a larger final episode.

## Does it smell like school

**Pages where a character explains so the reader can learn, or a caption states the lesson:**
1. **p42.** Leona: "I voted for the emergency authority. I did not give it an ending. I will answer for that." Mera: "It keeps the promise from hiding." This is a civics moral (sunset clauses, accountability), acted out through bands that warm.
2. **p43. The whole page exists to explain.** Mera walks them down a corridor of record cards and narrates step by step how a restriction becomes isolation: "Adrian's restriction began with ordinary entries… Then people stopped selling to him… After that, people stopped standing near him."
3. **p44.** Mera: "I gave the system authority to move faster than I could stop." This states the lesson of p43 a second time.
4. **p50. A full page of status captions** that reads like a summary of the lesson: "EMERGENCY AUTHORITY — RESCINDED / EXISTING DEBT — UNCHANGED / OWNERSHIP RECORDS — UNCHANGED." With p49's "CONVENOR OF PROPOSAL / NO EXECUTIVE DESIGNATION," the reader is told what the ending means rather than shown it.

Single lines that come close but are not counted: B11's "One sale has a customer. This one has a cage" (p20) and Lock's "Scale buys materials, testing, distribution and protection from retaliation" (p22). Each is one in-character line on a page that is otherwise showing.

**Count: 4. One of them (p43) is a whole page that exists to explain.** All four sit in Issue 3. That is the stretch where a 16-year-old reading the night before decides the book is homework.

**Pages that teach without smelling of it, because people need the thing and pay for it:**
- p3–4: the pharmacy credit, the missed payment, owning the debt.
- p10: the wage forfeited to save a man.
- p16: sending the record knowing the cost.
- p20: a customer's order slip next to the cage.
- p23: "If I take it, who services it?"
- p27–29: integration, tolerance and scale-down.
- p31: "Put that in the price."
- p32: the customer paid after the failure list, and the competitor allowed to "build beside us."
- p51–52: Tomas with a gauge, "Paper says sixty-two. / Pipe says eighteen."
- p54: twenty credits, "Five goes to Inez," and the balance going down.

These are the book's best pages, and none of them sounds like a lesson.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **Rebuild p2–4 so the debt's origin and the 52 credits are shown, not implied.** Show where the money goes, why the debt sits on Alex (a household-liability rule in Keel shown on the screen, or Alex co-signing for his father), and fix the Site 6 order. *Satisfies Young Americans Center (accounts and credit shown correctly) and the student who asks "can they do that?"*
2. **Give B11 a scene in Issue 2 where his own bad call costs the group.** He has to tell Alex what he got wrong and ask how Alex sees it, and call him Bi11bot on the page. *Satisfies Bill Reynolds (a mentor with his own failures who asks first, and a character he recognises).*
3. **Replace Mera's corridor (p43) and the band mechanics (p41–42, p47) with one scene the reader watches.** For example, in Adrian's recording a parts seller turns him away, then a neighbour does. *Brings the school count down to three or fewer. Satisfies the student reader and YAC.*
4. **Make ownership pay off on p54.** Show the four collaborators or Alex earning from the named, recorded invention: a Morrow & Sons unit sold with their names on it, or a sign showing whose shop it is. *Satisfies Bill Reynolds (recorded ownership becoming capital) and YAC (a business that serves people and makes money).*
5. **Give Morrow & Sons a full page in Issue 1**, a customer and a repair before the mine. Then honest trade is on the page before the House is. *Satisfies the Denver parent who reads only the first issue, and YAC's concern about the bleak opening.*

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: partly; smells like school: yes; school pages: 4
