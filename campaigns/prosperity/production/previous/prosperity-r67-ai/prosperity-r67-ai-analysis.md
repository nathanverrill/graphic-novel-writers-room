# Analysis: *Prosperity*, Issues 01–03 (pp. 1–54)

I read the pages only. Page numbers are the script's.

---

## The political read

| Reading | Signal | Pages that carry it |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | **Strong on the surface** | p2: a 17-year-old inherits his father's debt by automated rule ("PARENTAL HOUSEHOLD LIABILITY TRANSFER: COMPLETE"). pp14–15: water is diverted from homes to industry while the record says otherwise ("REPORTED TO KEEL: 62% — DELIVERED TO KEEL: 18%"). Anyone who knows Flint will think of it. p20: "She made it." / "The House owns it." pp21–29: a corporate director threatens workers' jobs to force a signature. pp50–53: a community review board challenges a corporation's "compliant" allocation. |
| **Socialist** | Some | p17: miners seize the residential regulator themselves. p20: the worker is cut off from what she makes ("CREATOR BONUS: 14 CREDITS"). p33: "No exclusive owner." p54: "CONTRIBUTOR SHARES POSTED", which reads as co-op ownership. |
| **Libertarian** | **Strong, underneath** | p20: the Houses are "selected for scale" by an Alpha charter, so the state picks winners. B11: "Morrow earns customers. The Houses were selected for scale." p41: emergency authority with "no expiration. No independent revocation… Alpha recorded 'later' as permission to continue." p44: the system cuts Adrian off from buying, travelling and finally from people. p13: "Protective?" / "A versatile word." pp22, 48: the hero turns down a debt-clearing deal and then executive power. |
| **Conservative** | Some | p9 and p54: keeping your word on a debt. p42: "Both choices were mine." Family duty runs through the book (Grandmother, the father's legacy, the mother-daughter reckoning). p22: he refuses a deal that would make things easy. Mistrust of technocracy runs throughout. |
| **Capitalist in a bad way** | **Strong** | The Houses (pp19–36), Lock's threats (pp22, 29: "I said protection was available."), p36 "HOUSE PRODUCTION AUTHORIZED", and p53, where the House rep calls the theft "compliant". |
| **Capitalist in a good way** | Some, and late | p3: Ada's fixed-installment loan. p20: Morrow & Sons ("price posted, worker wage and modest shop margin"), but it is **one panel**. p54: Morrow sells purifiers and cartridges at a posted price, Alex earns a maintenance credit, and his loan balance goes down. |

**What the surface invites:** an anti-corporate, anti-debt story. Poor miners have their water stolen by corporations and a machine government, and a kid is born into debt. A reader flipping through sees pp2, 14–15, 20 and 21–29, and that's the read they come away with.

**What the story supports:** something closer to classical-liberal or libertarian. The villain is a *chartered* monopoly propped up by emergency powers that never expired. The good business (Morrow) earns its customers. The hero turns down both the windfall (p22) and the throne (p48–49). The debt is never cancelled (p51: "EXISTING DEBT… UNCHANGED PENDING HUMAN PROCESS"; p54: 402,110 credits). The fix is a slow process that anyone can appeal, not a saviour.

**Who stops because of the surface:** a social-studies teacher in a conservative district, or a parent who picks up Issue 02 and flips pp20–29. They see "corporations as villains", may stop there, and never reach p41 or p54. Students won't stop over politics. They stop over confusion and AI-smell (see below). A progressive reader who finishes may feel let down that the debt stands and the House keeps producing (p36). That's a weaker reaction and unlikely to become a complaint.

## Young Americans Center

**What they'd like:**
- p1: a teenager making an unapproved repair that works ("Just notice it works."). That's take-charge in one splash.
- pp3, 9, 54: a loan made correctly. "Fixed installments. Same amount each time. No surprise fees." When he misses a payment, he says so: "Then tell me before I have to ask." / "I'm telling you." Fourteen months later: "PERSONAL LOAN BALANCE REDUCED" / "Same amount next week." Of all the money they lend to teenagers, this is the arc they'd most want in front of students.
- p2's maths holds up: 418,607 ÷ 40 a day ≈ 28.7 years. p54's balance (402,110 after 14 months) is consistent with roughly 40 a day. A bank will check this, and it passes.
- p22: Alex refuses "Your debt clears tonight." p51: the debt is not cancelled by the powerful. Their objection about debt that someone powerful cancels is avoided outright.
- p54: Morrow & Sons sells a serviceable product with replaceable cartridges at a posted price. Contributors get shares, and the teenager earns a maintenance credit. A business that serves people and makes money doing it.
- Gambling is shown as ruin, not glamour.
- The word "capitalism" never appears, and there are no party cues in the dialogue.

**What would bother them:**
- p2: inherited debt for a minor. It's the book's first money fact, and a real bank would call it untrue. Inside the fiction it's labelled an unjust rule ("KEEL SUCCESSOR RULE — ALPHA AUTHORIZED"), but a student isn't told it's unlike real life.
- p3: "Banks don't bring medicine up a hill."
- Issue 02 reads, page after page, as big business against workers. Morrow gets one panel (p20).
- The debt is never shown as anything but an injustice he's stuck with. At p54 he has paid down 4% in 14 months. A tired student can read that as "effort barely matters", which brushes against "the system is rigged".
- p9 is confusing. Alex's account shows −40 and Ada asks "The wage forfeiture did that?", but the forfeiture happens on p10. A bank reader will see account mechanics in the wrong order.

**Likely verdict:** satisfied in part. They'd be glad to hand out Issue 01 and the last page. Issue 02 makes them nervous in classrooms where the teacher leans either way.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he'd like:**
- **No windfall solves the plot.** He refuses the debt clearance (p22) and executive authority (p48–49: "Then the refusal has a price."). The 312-patent estate is never cashed in (p21). Lock even says it for him: "Rights under dispute are not a wallet."
- **Crony capitalism as the villain, not free markets.** "Morrow earns customers. The Houses were selected for scale." (p20). The charter award reads exactly like the corporate welfare he warns about, big companies going "up to the government".
- **Government power that never sunsets** (p41). "We thought we could take it back later." This is his worry about control in its plainest form.
- **Presence over technology.** Palms on pipes (p1, p14), touch tests on connectors (p28), "HUMAN ROLE REQUIRED" (p47), Tomas's physical gauge beating the record (p52). "Technology may scale intelligence. It cannot replace presence" is the book's theme, though nobody says it.
- **A mentor who is wrong and asks.** p28: "The fault is likely in the membrane chemistry." / "No. Feel the edge." p30: "I counted the record and missed the people. That was my error." / "What would you do?" That's the only page where B11 behaves the way he wants.
- **Failure in the open.** p26: "Now we can fail where the cameras can see us." p27: 11% before 94%.
- **Ownership made concrete.** p31: "If a household can't clean it, it doesn't own it." p35, p54: contributors and maintenance responsibility recorded, shares posted.
- **A lender faced and repaid**, in small form (pp9, 54).

**What would bother him:**
- **Alex doesn't really fail.** He's right at p13 (the bolts), p28 (the edges), p30 (the people) and p31 (the fold). His one failure, the missed installment, is caused by the system, not by him. Nothing on the page matches the beer hall: no "we borrowed too much", no "Paul, I can't pay you."
- **The lenders who matter have no face.** The 418,607 is owed to a screen. There's no banker to go and see.
- **Property, de Soto style, is ambiguous.** Adrian left 312 recorded patents, which is exactly the "written down, recorded, defensible" capital de Soto talks about, and the hero answers "No exclusive owner" with "PATENT STATUS: UNFILED" (p33). The contributor shares on p54 partly rescue it. Still, a de Soto reader may feel the book treats recorded title as the problem.
- **The ending is a committee** (pp50–53). He might count that as civic hands-on learning. He might also feel a process has replaced a person's initiative. That's inference.
- The dog. TJ is never said to be named for a Founder. If it's Jefferson, it isn't on the page, so he'd only know if someone told him.

**Does Bi11bot sound like him?** By the profile's own voice notes (plain, slow, a bit roundabout, Western; a question first, then a story from his own life, then one plain line; jokes at his own expense; never a lecture): **mostly no.**
- On the page the character is "B11", not "Bi11bot". The pun on his name is half there at best.
- B11 speaks in clipped, dry, machine epigrams: "Correct." "A versatile word." "Trace shows where. Not why." "That is generally what connectors are for." "Signing produces the lowest immediate loss." That's a butler-robot voice, not a slow Western storyteller.
- In 54 pages he tells **no story from his own life**. He asks **one** real question (p30). He uses no Western idiom.
- The self-deprecating humour is the closest match: "Well. That took longer than expected." (p6), "I expected a longer failure." (p28).
- Positives: B11 never lectures, never hands out the answer, is wrong twice and owns it, and never replaces people. Alex's people are Ada, Tomas and the five builders.

**Likely verdict:** satisfied in part, close to yes on values. The plot holds his line on windfalls, cronyism and presence better than most commissioned books would. It misses on the mentor's voice and on the young person failing and walking into the lender's office.

## A comic writer

**The hook:** strong. p1's splash (an unapproved repair that works) and p2's birthday debt screen ("Happy birthday, Phantum." / "I've had better surprises.") are a real pitch opening. You know who he is and what he's up against in two pages.

**Page turns:** placed well. Most reveals land on left-hand pages after a turn:
- p6: B11 wakes.
- p14: the hidden chamber.
- p16: the one-use choice.
- p18: the cliffhanger.
- p34: "No."
- p42: the bands link.
- p52: Tomas alive.
- p54: the close.

**Craft problems a pro would flag:**
- **p17 kills the p18 cliffhanger.** p17 shows "Tomas remains behind the workers, alive and limping." A page later the wristband says "TOMAS SITE 6 — SIGNAL LOST" and "the question is unresolved." The reader already has the answer. The same goes for the p52 "reveal".
- **p9 happens before p10.** The wage forfeiture is discussed before it occurs.
- **p17–18: is Mera saving them or drowning them?** "Give him a route that exists," and then she floods the conduit behind them. On a first read it's unclear.
- **Splash pages:** two, p1 and p37. p1 works. p37 is cluttered with two insets and a screen, so it isn't really a splash. The bands linking (p42) and the 94% prototype (p28) are bigger moments with less room.
- **Dialogue:** one voice for everyone. Nearly every exchange is a balanced two-beat aphorism:
  - "Paper says sixty-two." / "Pipe says eighteen."
  - "Watching is not knowing."
  - "It's slow." / "It's visible."
  - "Not a city." / "Not yet. A household."
  - "Thinking about it isn't doing it."

  Lock is the only distinct voice, and he's the best-written character on the page ("From being asked to choose while frightened.", p21). Ada, Tomas, Lina, Ren and B11 all speak alike.
- **Too much screen text.** Dozens of all-caps readouts carry plot and theme, worst on pp47–51.

**What they'd cut:**
- The Issue 02 fetch quest: pp23–25 are three errands for "a piece" each.
- The roll-call on p27 panel 1.
- At least half the screens in Issue 03.
- One of Mera and Leona's overlapping confessions (pp40–41, 44).
- Too many named people for 54 pages: four builders introduced in three pages and never given separate wants.

**Would they take the job?** Yes for Issue 01, which is tight, physical and well turned. They'd ask to re-break Issue 03, whose climax is a hearing among three unnamed functionaries, before signing.

## A game designer

**Is there a game?** Yes, and a clear one.

**The loop:** work a shift, earn 40, make installments, choose to risk pay to help someone (p10: "WAGE FORFEITED: −40 CR" / "That is today's pay." / "I know."), and find where the record and reality disagree.

**Choices that cost something:**
- p10: your pay or a stranger.
- p16: a one-use exit. Leave unseen, or broadcast and go back through Tomas's door.
- pp22/29: sign and save the visible group, or refuse and expose them.
- p48–49: take power, or refuse and lose the workshop and your clearance.

Each choice has a stated, permanent cost. That's rare and good.

**Systems a player would push on:**
- **The Measure** (pp14–15): sense, then compare record against physical condition. It's a *Papers, Please* detective verb, and it scales to every city.
- **The debt ledger** with fixed installments and a lender who remembers (pp3, 9, 54).
- **TJ**, the modular dog whose detached head scouts while his body works (pp11, 13). It's an instantly understood mechanic, and it'll sell.
- **The incompatible-standards build** (pp27–28, 31–32): three House standards that won't connect, and a sleeve, a fold and a heat loop to bridge them. A strong crafting puzzle.
- **The public appeal table** (pp50–53) works only if it's adversarial: the House rep plays cards, and you counter with physical evidence.

**Would they want to build it?** Yes. It sits close to Young AmeriTowne's simulated-town idea. They'd build Issue 01 first and treat Issue 03's governance as a late-game system, not a cutscene.

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine:** a debt-bound teenager in a three-tier world (Keel, Halyard, Oasis). Every episode, the official record lies, and someone has to put a hand on the pipe. That's a repeatable procedural on top of a serial mystery about the father.

**Characters an audience would follow for a season:**
- **Alex**, though he needs more flaws.
- **Ada**, the strongest arc: the lender with a mother in the capital, pp37–42.
- **Lock**, the best villain voice.
- **Mera**, ambiguous: she watches, and she can't undo it.
- **B11 and TJ**, the merch.

Tomas works in small doses. The four builders are interchangeable as written.

**Season-one cliffhanger:** the best one is buried. p35: "a separate dark metal band engraved **ADRIAN** is visible for one instant." Is B11 carrying Adrian, or is he Adrian? pp45–46 refuse to show "evacuation or death". That's the season hook. The book uses it and then lets it go: by p41 it's just "Adrian's band". The p18 conduit cliffhanger would have been a good mid-season break if p17 hadn't spoiled it.

**What's missing:**
- A face for Alpha.
- An Issue 03 climax with physical danger rather than a review that is "slow… visible".
- What Alex wants for himself. "You still thinking about leaving?" / "Every day." (p54) is set up from p3 and never pursued.
- A personal cost that lands on someone we love. The builders' "access review" stays offscreen.

**Verdict:** they'd option the premise off Issue 01 and rebuild the back third around Adrian.

## Does it smell like school

For these readers (made to read it the night before, quick to say "AI wrote this"), the bigger risk isn't one lesson line. It's the **house style**. Every character speaks in tidy, balanced epigrams, and the plot runs through bureaucratic screen text. To a 16-year-old looking for a reason to put it down, that reads as machine-made. Add Grandmother's riddle (p4: "He's closer than you think.") and B11's "Somewhere the records forgot." (p7), and the impatience starts early.

**Pages where a line or caption exists to teach** (11):
1. **p3:** "Fixed installments. Same amount each time. No surprise fees." It's in character for a lender stating terms, but it's the line a student will flag as the financial-literacy bit.
2. **p20:** "Morrow earns customers. The Houses were selected for scale." The thesis, stated, with a diagram panel ("price posted, worker wage and modest shop margin").
3. **p21:** "Your father left you an estate, not liquid money. Rights under dispute are not a wallet." A definition of liquidity. Lock's voice softens it.
4. **p31:** "If a household can't clean it, it doesn't own it." A good line, but it's the moral.
5. **p41:** Leona explains the Covenant mechanism: two of three custodians, no expiration, no revocation. Civics exposition about sunset clauses.
6. **p42:** "I will support a process where affected people can see what is promised and call it broken." A civic statement read into a recorder.
7. **p47:** "HUMAN ROLE REQUIRED: CONVENOR / AUTHENTICATOR" / "I convene it. I don't own it."
8. **p48:** "That's still two people deciding for everyone else."
9. **p50:** "TEMPORARY REVIEW PROCESS — THREE CITIES / PUBLIC EVIDENCE / APPEALABLE"
10. **p51:** "RESCINDED WITHIN DEFINED SCOPE" / "UNCHANGED PENDING HUMAN PROCESS"
11. **p53:** "It's slow." / "It's visible." The lesson as a closing caption.

Eight of the eleven fall in pp41–53. Issue 01 barely smells at all. Issue 03 smells like a civics unit.

**Pages that teach without smelling of it, because people need the thing and pay for it:**
- **p2:** the debt screen. A student can check the 28.7 years themselves, and it's correct.
- **p9:** the missed installment, owned before being asked.
- **p10:** a day's pay forfeited to save a man, with the price shown in credits.
- **pp14–15:** a hand on two pipes, one cold and one full. Record against reality, no explanation needed.
- **p16:** a one-use choice with a stated cost.
- **pp27–28:** 11%, a spark, a filed sleeve, 94%.
- **p32:** "FEEDWATER REQUIRED / CLEANING REQUIRED / REPLACEMENT PARTS REQUIRED", a product with real upkeep.
- **p49:** "Then the refusal has a price."
- **p52:** Tomas's "Paper says sixty-two." / "Pipe says eighteen."
- **p54:** a sale, a cartridge, a contributor share, a maintenance credit, a payment, a balance going down.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **A scene in Issue 02 where Alex's own mistake costs Ada money, and he goes to her before she finds out. Bill Reynolds, YAC.** Something he chose, not a system penalty. He would face the lender, the terms would be renegotiated, and p54 would show those payments. This is the beer-hall story the book doesn't yet have, and the "fails, owns it, keeps his word" arc YAC wants. Fix the p9 order while you're there.
2. **Rebuild p30, or p46, so B11 asks first and the error he admits is one of his own from the past, with a cost. Bill Reynolds.** Bi11bot should be the slow, roundabout, Western questioner of the profile, not a dry readout. If p30 is the template, the other B11 scenes can follow it.
3. **Give Morrow & Sons a full scene in Issue 02, for example as the rented maker room on p26. YAC, and conservative teachers.** A small business that earns customers would sit on the page next to the Houses, not as one panel. That breaks the surface read of "business is the villain" before p29.
4. **Rebuild pp47–51 so people with names and stakes do what the screens now announce. The students (school smell), the comic writer, the showrunner.** Tomas's gauge scene (pp52–53) shows the method already works. The readouts in between are where the book turns into a civics unit.
5. **p17: take Tomas out of frame. Comic writer, showrunner, students.** The p18 cliffhanger would then hold through Issue 02, and the p52 reveal would land.

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: partly; smells like school: yes
