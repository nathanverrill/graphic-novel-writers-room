# Analysis — *Prosperity*, pages 1–64

I read the script cold, pages only. The readers are 15 to 18, the book is homework, they're reading it the night before, and they distrust anything that looks AI-made. They'll judge it on the first ten pages and the last five. The partner will judge it against their October notes. The backer will judge it mostly on Bi11bot and on how the debt gets settled.

**What the surface invites:** Issues 01–03, at a glance, look like a dystopia of debt bondage and corporations stealing from workers: the birthday debt (p12), the forfeited wage (p24), the Caldera kiosk (p39). A teacher flipping to p39–40 sees a corporation stripping a worker's name off his work and an executive in a suit pressuring a teenager. That reads as anti-corporate.

**What the story supports:** The real villain is a central planner (Alpha) and the "temporary emergency authority" humans gave it (p47, p52). The Houses are cronies. Water reaches them through Alpha's falsified records (p30–32), and Lock can freeze commercial rights "pending review" (p40), which is state power, not market power. The hero's answer is ownership written down and defended (registry p39, Maker's Seal p44), measurement (p32), a licensed business with a warranty (p44), and repaying debt through work and royalties (p63).

**The weak spot in that defence:** The page never shows *how* the Houses connect to Alpha. "Industrial diversion… toward Halyard" (p30–32) is the only link, and it's two pages earlier and one city away. A reader can easily file the Houses under "big business" rather than "cronies."

**Who stops early because of the surface:**
- A conservative parent or school board member who flips to p34–35 ("communal table") and p39–40 and stops.
- A progressive student who loves Issues 01–03 as an anti-corporate story. At p44 the answer turns out to be a licence split and a warranty reserve, and they may feel cheated rather than persuaded. They won't stop reading, but they'll roll their eyes.

**The prologue leaves one question open that matters to both partners.** "Nobody gets water that clean by being polite." / "Then he took it first." (p2). "How did you get this lake?" / "I didn't." (p7). The book never comes back to how old Alex came to hold Lake Keel. A sceptical reader can leave with the thief's version of ownership unanswered.

**Does the book conclude, or leave a reader concluding, that free enterprise is bad? No.** The ending endorses it on p43–44 and p63, panel 4. The anti-House pages (p39–40, p62) are aimed at cronies, and in both cases the cure is records and competition, not taking away anyone's property.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like**
- A teenager who takes charge. He steps out of zone knowing it costs him: "You know that costs you." / "I noticed." (p24).
- He borrows 40 credits, kept "separate from your household account," and repays it (p23, p63, panel 3: "REPAYMENT IN PROGRESS"). That is their bank's lesson, done right and done small.
- "Medicine first. The rest can wait." (p23). Budgeting shown by doing.
- Failure built into the work: 11%, "Smaller failure," then 91% (p42–43). That is "willing to fail," on the page.
- A business that serves people and makes money doing it: the licence split, the warranty reserve, the competitor, the customer paying a posted price (p44, p63).
- Integrity under pressure: refusing the House buyout (p40) and refusing the executive debt clearance (p50, p58).
- Intellectual property protected and credited (p39, p44). This directly answers their October complaint.
- Nothing partisan. "Capitalism" never appears, and neither does any party cue.

**What would bother them**
- p12: a child inheriting parental debt, plus the misused "guarantor" label. They are a real bank, and this is the one page where the book gets account mechanics visibly wrong, with no correction anywhere.
- p35: neighbours pay his overdue installment and that payment opens the plot ("They bought the gate"). On p63 the 500 credits show up as a line on Alex's ledger, but never as money he owes back. A debt eased by other people's money, with no repayment shown, sits close to the thing they object to.
- p35 also says "CURRENT INSTALLMENT OVERDUE" on, apparently, the first days of the debt.
- Issues 04–05 are still hard to follow. The Covenant bands are tracked by colour (sage-gold, gold-and-burgundy, pearl-grey). What the linked bands *do* is never shown: on p56 "No command display activates," and on p57 the screen just waits for a human. "Two linked. One answer still missing." (p50) is the riddle style they complained about.
- p61 and the civic-review ending feel like civics class, not story.

**Likely verdict: partly.** This is clearly the book their notes asked for: water from page 1, a real business, IP and profit respected, a readable train. A teacher could put it in front of students. They will still ask for p12 and p35 to be right as banking before they do.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like**
- Every windfall is refused: Lock's estate-for-debt deal (p40), the "DEBT CLEARANCE: AVAILABLE" chamber (p50), and "EXECUTIVE CLEARANCE AVAILABLE" (p58). No giveaway solves the plot. The debt goes down by work, royalties and posted repayments, and it is still 314,159 at the end (p63). That's "No Free Lunch" plus an earned pi joke.
- Adrian's confession is close to his beer-hall story: "I borrowed too much. I gambled some of it… The mistakes were mine." (p19).
- Ownership made concrete: the registry (p39), the worker whose name disappears from the record (p39, which is de Soto's dead capital as a picture), and the Maker's Seal (p44). "Find out who gets to decide what stays ours." (p10).
- A central planner and its crony Houses as villains (p30–32, p40, p52), and a lone Director who admits "I could not stop a door from closing" (p52).
- "Then what are we missing?" in Ada's mouth (p14) is his own question.
- Free markets need failure: p42–43.
- The AI mentor doesn't replace people. Tomas, Ada, the four makers and the Keel workers solve every problem. Bi11bot carries things.

**What would bother him**
- **Bi11bot does not sound like him.** Measured against the voice notes (plain, slow, roundabout, Western, a question first, then a story from his own life, then one plain line, jokes at his own expense):
  - The good: one real question-first exchange, "My route assumed we left Tomas… That was wrong." / "Yes. What would you do?" (p25). Being wrong on the page: "I read that wrong." (p29). "Who carries the downside?" (p23) is very much him. A dry self-joke: "B11 if the room is on fire." (p18).
  - The missing: no story from his own past, anywhere. No Western plainness, no "damn hard." Mostly he states facts like a navigation system: "Lower distance. Lower risk." "Private route remains open." "The recording ends there."
  - He also has no lines from p38 to p64 except one action on p48. The character built on Bill disappears from the second half.
  - Verdict on the voice: it has his questions and none of his stories.
- **The p35 collection.** "We don't believe in charity." Neighbours' money is what opens the rail gate. He'd accept it as community, but he'd want to see it paid back.
- **Nobody faces the lender.** The 418,607-credit debt has no person behind it. His best story is "I went to the bankers… Paul, I can't pay you." The book has no Paul.
- **TJ, the dog (p1–10), only appears in 2099.** Nothing on the page says TJ is named for Jefferson. If that's the intent, it's an inference only he will make.
- A careless reader could take the Houses as "business owners as villains by default." That's an inference, but it's the risk on p39–40.

**Likely verdict: partly.** He'd agree with the book's argument from start to finish. He'd be disappointed that the mentor carrying his name has none of his stories and is gone after Issue 02.

## A comic writer

**The hook.** The cold open is visual and immediate: a robot dog throws its head and legs across a lake to jam a thief's siphon (p2–6). It works. The problem is that it opens with old Alex and two thieves (Nera, Voss) who never come back. The salvaged component and "the records will tell us" (p7–8) are also never paid off. A pitch editor will ask what p8–9 are for.

**Page turns.** On this right/left layout, a right-hand page should end on a hook and the next left-hand page should open with the reveal.
- Working: p17 ends on the amber eye ("TICK"), and the turn brings Bi11bot rising (p18), the best turn in the book. p11 ends on "DEBT TRANSFER DAY," and the turn brings the debt figure (p12). p31's Measure, then the turn to the 62%/18% readout on p32. p55 "The recording ends there," then the turn to p56.
- Fumbled: the bookcase swings open at the bottom of a *left* page (p16), so the reveal lands mid-spread. p57 is labelled "FULL-PAGE CIVIC HALL" and then given three more panels. That's a production contradiction an editor will flag immediately. The real splashes are p10 and p64. Neither is the big climactic image, because the climax is a meeting.

**Dialogue.** It's quick and often funny: "I'm generous with containers." (p13), "Badly, eventually." (p16), "Adrian's jaw. His poorer timing." (p18), "Foreman! Your safety report is on fire." (p27). But everyone gets the same clipped, aphoristic comeback, so the voices merge. The "for whom?" template repeats three times. Ada has the most distinct voice.

**What they would cut.** p8–9, or else pay them off. Half of p51–52 (Mera's archive repeats her point). p60–61 squeezed into one page. They'd also merge the two Oasis chapters so the Covenant gets explained once, clearly.

**Would they take the job?** Yes, as a rewrite. Issues 01–03 are a strong, well-plotted run with a clear goal and real costs. They'd want a free hand on Issues 04–06, where the plot becomes symbolic objects clicking together.

## A game designer

**Yes, there's a game. It's closer to *Papers, Please* or *Frostpunk* than to an action game, and it overlaps neatly with Young AmeriTowne.**

- **The loop:** repair, earn, budget (medicine vs. food vs. parts, p23), pay the installment, unlock travel (p35). Then measure, find the lie, challenge it (p32, p62).
- **Choices that cost something:**
  - Leave your zone to save Tomas for −40 credits (p24).
  - The private route against the public broadcast (p33).
  - The House buyout against a frozen estate (p40).
  - Executive debt clearance against staying a person among people (p50, p58).
  - Every one has a number attached. That's rare and valuable.
- **Systems a player would want to push on:**
  - The reported vs. delivered gap (62/18). Players would want to audit every pipe.
  - Crafting from incompatible parts, with failure rates (11% to 91%, p42–43).
  - The licence split and warranty reserve (p44): set your own split and live with it.
  - House competition (p44, "COMPETING INDUSTRIAL UNIT").
  - Evidence challenges against doctored records (p62).
  - TJ's detachable-limb mechanic (p2–6) is a ready-made puzzle tool.
- **What's thin:** the Oasis chapters have no system to play. Linking the bands is a cutscene, not a decision.

**Would they want to build it?** Yes. The economy is already designed, and it's the book's best asset.

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine:** a debt clock (418,607 credits, 28.7 years) plus a quest up through three stacked cities for three safeguards, pursued by a faceless system. Issue 01 is a real pilot: birthday debt, best friend, ailing grandmother, a hidden workshop, a robot, a dead father's message, a locked door, and "TRACKING ACTIVE" (p11–22). Issue 00 works as a cold open but promises a future-frame story (thieves, the lake) the season never delivers.

**Characters an audience would follow:**
- Alex, though he has almost no flaw. He's right every time after p29.
- Ada, the best character: sharp tongue, a mother wound, the broadcast.
- Tomas, the comic, loyal working man.
- Mera, the authority figure who turns.
- Bi11bot, who has the merch potential and gets benched.

**Season-one cliffhanger options:** the bulkhead closes on Adrian with "I do not know what happened after" (p55–56), and the House competitor (p44). Both are on the page and both are left hanging. That's good.

**What's missing:**
- A villain with a face who stays. Lock is gone after p44, and Alpha is a screen.
- A finale that isn't a hearing (p57–63).
- An interior flaw for Alex.
- Any reason for the 2099 frame.

They'd option it for the world and the debt-clock premise and rebuild the back half.

## Does it smell like school

**Pages where the book explains so the reader can learn, or a scene exists to state procedure: 4**

1. **p40, panel 2.** A display labels the three Houses by sector (water/chemistry, energy/compute, extraction/materials). It's a legend for the reader, not a plot event.
2. **p60.** Representatives place baselines and records, and "mark the first temporary review docket." It's procedure staged as action.
3. **p61, the whole page.** Four panels of displays: "EMERGENCY AUTHORITY: RESCINDED," the nodes changing colour, "MEASUREMENT — ROUTING — PAYROLL — MAINTENANCE — RECORD PUBLICATION," "EXISTING RECORDS: OPEN TO HUMAN REVIEW." This page exists to explain the new governance arrangement, and it's the page a skimming student will remember as "the civics part."
4. **p63, panel 2.** An itemised repayment ledger with six categories. It reads like a worksheet answer key.

**Single lines that state a moral (not counted; one each won't lose a reader):** the Measure's collar, "WHAT IS NOT MEASURED CAN BE MADE INVISIBLE" (p31), "If we hold all the keys, everyone waits for our hands" (p50), "Nobody gets the whole room" (p59).

**World-building exposition that reads as necessary rather than school:** "Alpha routes water…" (p20), the safeguards (p21), the Leona flashback (p47).

**Where to look:** the school smell is concentrated in the last five pages, exactly where the night-before reader decides what the book was.

**Pages that teach without smelling of it, because people need the thing and pay for it:**
- The debt figure and projected clearance as a gut punch (p12).
- Tomas's 40-credit loan, kept separate from the household account (p23).
- The −40 wage forfeit for doing the right thing (p24).
- 62% reported vs. 18% delivered (p32).
- Neighbours bringing the installment current (p35).
- The 11%-to-91% build (p42–43).
- The 38-credit split with a warranty reserve (p44).
- The Tomas note still being repaid (p63, panel 3).
- A customer agreeing to a posted repair price (p63, panel 4).

That's nine pages of financial literacy done the right way, by people who need the money.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **A lender scene (Issue 05 or Issue 06, one page).** Alex faces the actual holder of the 418,607-credit debt, a person, says plainly what he can and can't pay, and they agree on terms he then keeps. *For Bill Reynolds and for Young Americans Center:* their founder story and his beer-hall story, and the book's best chance to get credit mechanics right.
2. **p35 → p63: make the neighbours' 500 credits a loan.** Put names and amounts on the communal table and show it being paid back on the final ledger. *For Bill ("we don't believe in charity") and the partner ("borrowing and paying back").*
3. **A Bi11bot scene in Halyard (around p42).** As the build fails, he asks the question first, tells a short failure from his own past, and lands one plain line, then steps back while the makers solve it. *For Bill:* it gives the mentor his voice and keeps him in the back half without replacing people.
4. **Replace p61's display page with one dramatised scene.** For example, a Keel family reading their own delivered water on a public gauge for the first time. *For the students:* it takes the school smell out of the ending.
5. **Expand p41 into one page per joining decision**, with each maker staking something real (a job, a House contract, an old loyalty to Adrian). *For Young Americans Center:* their Chapter 3 request for professional judgment, ambition, loyalty and risk.

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: partly; smells like school: yes; school pages: 4
