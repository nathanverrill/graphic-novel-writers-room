# Analysis: *Prosperity*, Issues 01–03 (pages 1–54)

I read the pages cold. I had no brief, plan or notes. The book is 54 pages in three issues of 18: Keel (1–18), Halyard (19–36) and Oasis (37–54).

---

## The political read

| **Progressive** | Strong in Issue 1, some after that | p10: Alex loses a day's pay ("WAGE FORFEITED: −40 CR") for saving a trapped miner. pp13–16: an industrial line takes a poor district's water while reporting 62% and delivering 18%. pp20, 22, 24: kiosks strip workers' names from their work ("CREATOR: UNLISTED"). p53: public review and "the affected participants." **What the surface invites.** On the surface the book reads as a left-leaning labor and environmental dystopia. Issue 1 is a seventeen-year-old in debt bondage, a company mine that docks pay for a rescue, and industry stealing a poor district's water. Issue 2 opens with a big House taking a worker's invention behind glass. The words "industrial" and "House" carry the villainy, and the word "Charter" is the only hint that the Houses are chartered by the state rather than simply big.

**What the story supports.** Underneath, the villains are fraud, cronyism and unaccountable authority, not trade:
- Reported versus delivered water is fraud (p16, p51).
- An industrial interest gets a public board to approve a diversion before anyone checks the pipe ("DECLARED INTEREST," pp37, 52). That is a crony, not a market.
- The deepest villain is an emergency authority with no end date (pp41–44).

The heroes, meanwhile, are a pharmacist who lends, a repair firm that takes orders, and a kid who answers a bigger company with competition (p32). By p54 the honest business is the happy ending.

**Who stops reading because of the surface.** Most at risk: a Young Americans Center staffer, or a conservative Denver parent, who flips through Issue 1 only. Eighteen pages of inherited debt clocks, docked wages and diverted water read as "the system is rigged." The correction mostly arrives in Issue 3. A progressive teacher is less likely to stop, because Issue 1 suits them. Issue 3's emergency-powers material may then read to them as a right-coded cue, though it is written generally enough to survive that.

**Does the book conclude, or leave a reader concluding, that free enterprise is bad?** No. Honest lending and service businesses win (pp3–4, 20, 31–32, 54). The villains are a chartered House that buries evidence (pp21–22, 33), a crony diversion (pp37, 52) and emergency authority without limit (pp41–44, 50).

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like**
- **Pages 3–4 are their programme in two panels.** A lender fronts medicine and writes it down, the teen misses the date and owns it ("I'm not asking you to erase it"), and on p54 the line reads "INEZ QUISPE — MEDICINE ADVANCE: PAID" with "Five goes to Inez." That is credit used as a tool, missed and repaid.
- **Initiative and failure.** Page 1 is initiative. Pages 27–28 show a business failing at 11%, then iterating to 94%.
- **Honest product, paying customers.** The unit lists its own failures (p31), the customer paid "before seeing it" because "She saw the failure list" (p32), and Alex answers the House with "Then it can build beside us." That is a business that serves people and makes money.
- **No one cancels the debt.** Alex refuses the debt-clearing deal three times (pp21–22, 23, 33), and p50 states "EXISTING DEBT — UNCHANGED."
- **Gambling is a family wound,** never cool.

**What would bother them**
- **Page 2 gets credit wrong for a teenage audience.** Liability transfers to a 17-year-old automatically, against a gambling-account collateral. A real bank whose mission is teaching credit will not like a teen walking away thinking a parent's debt lands on them at their birthday.
- **The debt is the purest "done to you" debt in the book, and its maths stays bleak.** It is 402,110 of 418,607 after fourteen months.
- **Issue 1 alone reads as "rigged."** A staffer who previews only the first issue may stop there.
- **Issue 3 is hard for a student to follow on one reading.** Covenant bands, "TWO OF THREE," a designation lever, "CONVENOR OF PROPOSAL": a student on homework night will lose the thread.
- **"LICENSE: PUBLIC / NONEXCLUSIVE" (p35) leaves ownership blurry.** They may wonder whether the collaborators own anything.

**Likely verdict:** partly satisfied. The line is never crossed, and the Inez and Morrow threads are exactly what they would hand a classroom. The way the central debt works, and Issue 1 read on its own, keep it from a clean yes.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like**
- **The no-windfall ending is honoured,** almost to the letter. Lock's "Debt cleared tonight" is refused (p33), and "EXISTING DEBT — UNCHANGED" (p50).
- **His beer-hall story in miniature.** Alex faces his lender with empty hands (p4) and pays her back (p54).
- **The central planner as villain.** An emergency authority with "EXPIRATION: NONE" (p41) and an official who says "I gave the system authority to move faster than I could stop" (p44) is his view of government on the page. He would also see corporate welfare in the "Orbital Industrial Branch" winning a diversion before the pipe is checked (pp37, 52).
- **A hero who refuses power.** Alex refuses the lever (p49) and Ada refuses it too ("Don't make two people the answer for everybody," p48).
- **Earned success, with failure first** (pp27–28).
- **A mentor who is wrong.** On p30 B11 says "I counted the record and missed the people. What would you do?", and on p46 "I may have mistaken that for an instruction." That is exactly the AI he describes: it scales the count and misses the presence.
- **A dog.** TJ is a magnetic scrap dog, and if he reads "TJ" as Jefferson he will be pleased (inference: the page never says so).
- **Vocabulary.** "Capitalism" never appears.

**What would bother him**
- **The lecturing in Issue 3.** pp42–44 and p50 tell rather than ask (see the school section).
- **Ownership is only half concrete.** Pages 33–35 record the names, which is de Soto's point, but then license the work publicly and "PROPERTY TRANSFER: NONE." His "ownership ends arguments" is not quite shown: nobody ends up holding a recorded title they can build on.
- **The debt is compelled, not chosen.** Daniels repaid debts that had been discharged, and Reynolds went to Paul himself. Alex never chooses to take his debt on; it is imposed on him on p2.

**Does B11 sound like him?** No, not by the profile's own voice notes. The profile asks for plain, slow, roundabout and Western speech: a question first, then a story from his own life, then one plain line, with jokes at his own expense.
- **B11 is clipped and machine-precise.** "Stored. Sleep is more comfortable." "A fact with a poor record." "Six recorded bodies. Lowest risk."
- **He asks one real question in 54 pages** ("What would you do?", p30). He announces another ("I also have a question," p6) and never asks it.
- **He tells no story from his own past,** no "damn," nothing Western.
- **There is dry self-mockery:** "The destination objected" (p19), "I expected a longer failure" (p28). That is the nearest he gets.
- **He delivers answers and morals,** including the book's most school-like line (p20).
- **He passes the most important test:** he never replaces people, and he is wrong in exactly the right place.

**Likely verdict:** yes, satisfied, with a clear note that the robot doesn't talk like him and that Issue 3 preaches.

## A comic writer

**The hook.** Page 2 is a pro-grade hook. A teenager's birthday reads out as a debt of 418,607 with "PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS," and the guard says "Happy birthday, Phantum." Page 1 is a tight cold open with a working character beat ("Then don't approve it. Just notice it works."). They would keep reading past ten pages.

**Page turns.** The right/left layout is used well in Issue 1:
- the debt readout lands on a left page after the first turn (p2)
- the key on p5 turns over to B11 unfolding (p6)
- the forfeited wage closes p10, and TJ assembles after the turn (p11)

The Halyard opening (p19) and Leona's door (p38 to p39) also turn cleanly. Issue 3 turns mostly onto readouts rather than reveals.

**Splash pages.** Page 18 calls itself "Panel 4 — FULL-PAGE SPLASH." A page can't be both a fourth panel and a full-page splash; an artist will ask which. The image itself (four of them climbing as the water rises) is the right issue ending. Halyard's arrival (p19) and the Oasis chamber (p47) are the splash moments the book doesn't take.

**Dialogue.**
- Strong:
  - "Good. I need the money." (p4)
  - "Enough to complain." (p11)
  - "Log it under me." (p12)
  - "Then it can build beside us." (p32)
  - "Paper says sixty-two. / Pipe says eighteen." (p51)
- Weak: the mannered three-part exchanges on pp27–29, and the riddles. "He built and bet with the same hands" (p5), "A promise to be seen" (p42) and "Let them see who called him" (p36) a teen won't decode. Mera speaks almost entirely in riddles, which hurts, because she is the book's swing character.

**What they would cut or fix**
- **The Issue 3 machinery.** The Covenant bands, "two of three," the cold band versus the warm band, the designation lever.
- **Prop overload.** There are two brass keys, a chip, a token, a Measure, a Seal, three bands and a cartridge.
- **Look-alike names.** Mera/Mara and Vey/Veyra. Mara arrives on p54 as if we know her.
- **The introduction dump.** Four collaborators are introduced by name tag at once (p22).
- **The antagonist handoff.** Lock vanishes after p35, and Issue 3's opponent is Nadi, a newcomer on p37. The series loses its face.

**Would they take the job?** Yes, on the strength of Issues 1–2, on condition they could rebuild Issue 3 around people rather than mechanisms.

## A game designer

**Is there a game?** Yes, and a clear one: a repair-and-measure management sim in the line of *Papers, Please* and *Frostpunk*, with a debt ledger as the clock.

**The loop:** take a shift, earn credits, pay the ledger, find a discrepancy, measure it, decide what to expose and what it costs, then build and sell.

**Choices that cost something**
- Step over the line and lose a day's wage (p10).
- Send the record and lose the escape route (p16).
- Sign and clear the debt but bury the collaborators, or refuse and keep 28 years of debt (pp21, 23, 33).
- Take the planner's route for six recorded bodies, or the long route for everyone (p30).
- Build at industrial scale or at household scale (p29).
- Take executive power or convene the room (pp48–49).

Every one of them has a number attached.

**Systems a player would push on**
- The reported-versus-physical reading (62/18). It is a ready-made detective mechanic.
- Assembling components to a tolerance (pp27–28).
- TJ's detachable head as a scouting tool (p11).
- Customer orders with spare-parts follow-up revenue (p31).
- Inez's ledger as a credit-trust meter.

This lines up closely with a simulated-town-with-loans programme.

**Weak spot.** Issue 3 is a cutscene. The lever is the only player action, and the right move is not to pull it.

**Would they build it?** Yes. Issues 1–2 are practically a design document.

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine.** A debt clock on a seventeen-year-old, plus a stolen water supply, across three stacked cities: underclass Keel, maker Halyard, governing Oasis. It is *Snowpiercer*/*Arcane* territory, and the first issue proves it. They would keep going.

**Characters an audience would follow for a season**
- Alex: competent hands, stubborn word-keeping.
- Ada: the route-knower with a hidden mother.
- B11 and TJ: the merch and the comic relief.
- Tomas: the tough miner presumed dead, then back on p51. That is a crowd moment.
- Inez: the lender everyone respects.
- Lock: an articulate antagonist ("Scale buys materials, testing, distribution and protection from retaliation") worth casting.

**Season-one cliffhangers.** p18 (Tomas's light goes dark as the water rises) is the episode-one ending. The season-one finale is the Adrian recording (p45) or the summons (p36).

**What's missing**
- A sustained villain with a face. The node has none, Lock is dropped, and Mera is a riddle.
- Interpersonal heat between Alex and Ada, or anyone.
- A want for Alex beyond "somewhere that isn't here."
- A slower burn on Adrian. The father mystery is solved in about eight pages.
- An ending with more drive. "Next review date" (p53) is honest but low-voltage for a finale.

## Does it smell like school

**Pages where the book explains or states the lesson:**
1. **p20, Panel 4.** B11: "One customer paid for a thing. One worker got paid for losing hers." A caption-style moral, spoken by the robot.
2. **p42, Panel 1.** Leona: "I voted for the emergency authority. I did not give it an ending. I will answer for that." This is a civics lesson on sunset clauses, delivered as a confession to camera.
3. **p43 (whole page).** Mera walks the heroes through how Adrian's restriction escalated, card by card ("PURCHASE ACCESS: LIMITED... Then people stopped selling to him"). The scene exists to explain how emergency control spreads.
4. **p50 (whole page).** Readout screens state the moral: "EMERGENCY AUTHORITY — RESCINDED / EXISTING DEBT — UNCHANGED / OWNERSHIP RECORDS — UNCHANGED." The lesson arrives as text, not as a scene.

Borderline, not counted: p44 ("I gave the system authority to move faster than I could stop"), p49 ("CONVENOR OF PROPOSAL / NO EXECUTIVE DESIGNATION") and p53 (process civics). These pages are where a student reading on homework night feels the lesson plan. The bigger risk in Issue 3 is not the lecturing, though. It is that the band-and-lever mechanism can't be followed on a first read.

**Count: 4, of which p43 and p50 are whole pages that exist to explain.**

**Pages that teach without smelling of it**, because someone needs the thing and pays for it:
- pp3–4: medicine on credit, a missed payment, owned.
- p10: rescue costs exactly a day's pay.
- p16: sending the truth costs the exit.
- pp27–29: prototype fails, iterate, then find out that what scales isn't what a family can run.
- pp31–32: a paid order, spare cartridges, a customer who trusted the failure list.
- p51: "Paper says sixty-two. Pipe says eighteen."
- p54: "Twenty credits." / "Five goes to Inez."

This is the book at its best. These pages teach more than the counted pages do, and no student will feel taught.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **Page 2 and a short later scene: make the debt something Alex chooses to take on.** For example, he signs to assume Adrian's loan to keep the grandmother's apartment, at a real lender's counter, instead of an automatic "liability transfer" on his birthday. *Satisfies Young Americans Center* (credit shown correctly) *and Bill Reynolds* (a person taking charge, not a victim; the Daniels repayment).
2. **One B11 scene in Issue 2 (around pp26–28): B11 asks Alex a question first, then tells a story of his own failure with Adrian, a venture or a loan that went bad.** *Satisfies Bill Reynolds* (Bi11bot in his voice: question, own failure, plain line) *and the comic writer*.
3. **Replace the band mechanics on pp41–42 and the walkthrough on p43 with one scene of people.** A Keel resident or Tomas confronts Leona and Mera face to face over what the restriction did. *Satisfies the student reader and Bill Reynolds* (no lecture), and brings the school count down.
4. **Page 50: show the result in a scene instead of readouts.** Alex goes back to a lender, Inez or a bank, and borrows to expand the Morrow shop against the record of p54's repair slips. *Satisfies Young Americans Center* (borrowing as a tool a teenager can use) *and Bill Reynolds* (facing the banker; ownership made concrete).
5. **Carry Lock into Issue 3 as the House behind the Orbital diversion, shown working the Charter board.** *Satisfies the showrunner and comic writer* (a villain with a face), *and Young Americans Center*: it makes it plain from the surface on that the villain is a crony working the state, not business itself.

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: yes; smells like school: yes; school pages: 4
