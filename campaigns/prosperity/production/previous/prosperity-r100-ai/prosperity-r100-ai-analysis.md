# Analysis: *Prosperity*, Issues 00–03 (pages 1–64)

I read the pages only, in order, as each of these readers would meet them.

---

## The political read

| Reading | Signal | Pages that carry it |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | Strong on the surface | p12 (a 17-year-old inherits his father's debt through a "PARENTAL HOUSEHOLD LIABILITY TRANSFER"); p28 (an industrial pipe gets the water a town is told it receives); p34 (`OWNERSHIP: CALDERA HOUSE / CREATOR: UNLISTED / CREATOR BONUS: 14 CREDITS` – "My correction is in there"); p29 (workers close ranks against a foreman); p51–52, 60–61 ("affected people", representatives for each affected place, public challenge); p62 ("Owned units aren't delivered water"). |
| **Libertarian** | Strong in the story | p57–59 (Alex refuses `EXECUTIVE DESIGNATION: SINGLE AUTHORITY`, which would have cleared his own debt); p60 (`EMERGENCY AUTHORITY: RESCINDED`); p12, 20, 53 (travel and financial restriction by a central system, Alpha); p15/23/63 (a private loan between neighbours, missed, renegotiated and repaid with no authority involved); p35–36 (refuses a debt-clearing deal); p43–44 (creators' names written into a public registry, which is de Soto's point about property). |
| **Conservative** | Some | Keeping your word (p15 "Make it real", p23 "Then show me", p63 `WEEKLY TERMS: COMPLETE`); family duty (medicine for Grandmother, p15–16); no handout (debt still `402,110` on p63). There is little tradition, faith or community institution beyond the shop. |
| **Socialist** | Some | p63 `EQUAL PROFIT SHARES`; p44 `DESIGN: PUBLIC / NONEXCLUSIVE`; p29 worker solidarity; p61 community allocation tables. Nothing is nationalised, and the civic authority in Oasis is part of the problem (p53). |
| **Capitalist in a bad way** | Strong | The Houses (Caldera, Vector, Orbital): they divert water (p27–28), take workers' inventions (p34), cut a lab for "efficiency" (p38), offer a minor his debt cleared in exchange for his father's patents (p35), keep purifiers in storage while the town's gauge reads dry (p62). Director Lock is the only villain with a face. |
| **Capitalist in a good way** | Some, late | p40 Ren: "build it so a customer can repair it"; p42 Alex takes away the misleading output cup and writes an honest warning label; p63 Morrow & Sons: a customer brings in a purifier casing, and the ledger shows `CUSTOMER PAYMENT / MATERIALS / MAINTENANCE / WAGES`; the debt goes down because Alex earns. All of this is a few panels, mostly on one page. |

**What the surface invites:** an anti-corporate water dystopia. Inherited debt, a company town, corporations taking water and workers' patents. A reader who stops after Issue 02 has seen businesses only as predators.

**What the story supports:** something closer to libertarian or anti-crony. Look at what the Houses actually do on the page: checkpoints (p33), shift reassignments (p22), shutters and forced removal (p45–46), "House Administration" that clears debts by decree (p35). They are a guild-state, not businesses competing for customers. The book's hero refuses a windfall twice, refuses to rule, pays his neighbour back, and ends running a repair shop that charges customers.

**Who stops reading because of the surface:** a free-market parent or a teacher in a conservative district who flips through p27–38 and files it as "another corporations-are-evil book." Also a Bill Reynolds reader who lands on p62 and reads "Owned units aren't delivered water" on its own. A progressive teacher will read straight through, happy, and may teach the ending as a win for community governance over ownership. The pages allow that reading, though they don't require it.

**Does the book conclude, or leave a reader concluding, that free enterprise is bad?** **No.** The villains are a monopoly fused with administration (p33–35, 45–46) and a central authority (p53, 57). The ending is an honest business paying debts (p63). Two lines lean the other way: p62 "Owned units aren't delivered water" and p44's public release. Neither tips it.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like:**
- The Tomas loan is exactly what they want students to see. Alex borrows 45 credits for medicine and writes the terms down himself (p15). He misses a payment and shows up anyway: "You brought your face." / "Lost the money." Then he renegotiates in writing, `20 CREDITS NEXT FRIDAY` (p23), and pays in full: `MEDICINE ADVANCE: PAID / WEEKLY TERMS: COMPLETE` (p63). A teenager uses credit, fails, owns it and keeps his word. That is the Bill Daniels story at teen scale.
- Integrity under pressure, three times: he refuses Lock's deal (p35–36), writes the honest warning label (p42) and refuses the lever (p59).
- The large debt is not cancelled. It is lower because he works (`418,607` on p12, `402,110` on p63).
- "Willing to fail" is shown in the build: "Then we give it a smaller failure" (p41).

**What would bother them:**
- The only businesses on the page for 50 pages are predators, and the honest one is one page long. A teacher in a conservative district may read it as partisan.
- The casino chip as cherished key (p17) and keepsake (p9).
- Debt as something done to you (p12). They would want it clear that the inherited balance is the dystopia's injustice and not how real accounts work. The page doesn't say so, but it doesn't misstate how a loan works either. The Tomas loan is accurate.
- Clarity for students. Alpha, the Houses, the Covenant bands, Leona's relationship to Ada, who Mera is and who Morrow is are never made clear on the page (see the comic writer's section). Their staff would have to explain the book, which is what they don't want.
- The civic language on p51–61 ("affected people", representatives) may sound left-coded to some districts and civics-heavy to all of them.

**Likely verdict:** **Partly.** The line is not crossed. The loan arc and the honest label are exactly what they would choose. But the business that serves people and makes money is a one-page coda, and the surface read of Issues 01–02 is a risk for a carefully nonpartisan partner working in schools.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like:**
- The p23 scene is his beer-hall story: "I went to the bankers… I said, Paul, I can't pay you." Alex walks up empty-handed, says so, writes a new note and pays it over time.
- No windfall, twice. Lock offers `DEBT: CLEARED` (p35). The lever offers `DEBT: CLEARANCE AVAILABLE` (p58). He refuses both and is still in debt on p63. "A windfall isn't a pathway."
- The central planner is refused, and `EMERGENCY AUTHORITY: RESCINDED` (p60).
- Ownership made concrete: the creators' names go into a public record that blocks the exclusive claim (p43–44). That is de Soto's "written down, recorded and defensible." He may or may not like that they then release the design publicly (p44). It is their choice as owners, but the page doesn't present it as a business decision.
- The mentor is wrong and admits it: "You were wrong." / "Yes. What would you do?" (p25). The mentor asks: "Do you want answers or an exit?" (p19). B11 never hands Alex the solution and never replaces people. Tomas, Ada and the makers do the work.
- The dog TJ may be named for Jefferson. That is inference; the page never says so.

**What would bother him:**
- **Bi11bot does not sound like him.** Measured against the profile's voice notes (plain, slow, roundabout, Western; a question, then a story from his own life, then one plain line; jokes at his own expense), B11 is clipped and aphoristic:
  - "A fact with a poor record." (p18)
  - "That is rarely the cheaper order." (p19)
  - "It is a route. The destination objected." (p30)
  - "The summons has teeth." (p47)

  There is no story from his own life anywhere. There is no "damn hard" and no Western rhythm. The nearest thing to self-mockery is "I am carrying less than I appear to be" (p31) and "I may have mistaken that for an instruction" (p54). The questions and the willingness to be wrong are his. The voice is a dry robot's.
- B11 is the one who proposes leaving a pinned man behind (p25). It is framed as a prediction that turns out wrong, but the bot built on his personality makes the cold calculation.
- p62 "Owned units aren't delivered water" and p63 `EQUAL PROFIT SHARES` will make him pause. Neither is a dirty word, and the book never says "capitalism."

**Likely verdict:** **Yes.** He would finish it satisfied: the kid fails, faces the lender, pays it back, turns down the giveaway and turns down the crown. The one thing he would mark is that the bot carrying his name doesn't talk like him.

## A comic writer

**The hook:** a robot dog that comes apart into a swimming head and walking legs to sabotage water thieves (p1–7). That is a strong, visual, wordless cold open. But it is ten pages of a frame that never returns. "Then they'll come back" (p10) is never paid off, the drone component is never mentioned again, and the thieves' question ("The old man with everything… Or water made him rich," p3) is left hanging. A pro would see it as a promise the book breaks.

**Page turns:** the right/left layout is used well where it counts:
- p11 → p12: the birthday debt reveal, `418,607 CREDITS`.
- p17 → p18: B11 unfolds.
- p23 → p24: Tomas pinned.
- p27 → p28: `REPORTED 62% / DELIVERED 18%`.
- p45 → p46: the barrier seals.
- p57 → p58: the lever unlocks every record.

**Dialogue:** best when it's people talking like people:
- "You brought your face." (p23)
- "Go. I'm about to become paperwork." (p26)
- "Worse. Now I'm in it." (p39)
- "Took you long enough." / "You kept counting." (p63)

It is worst when everyone talks in the same epigram register:
- "He always sees the part that costs." (p6)
- "I chose the days I could prevent. I lost the years I couldn't see." (p50)
- "Don't hold it like a crown." (p44)

The audience is students with short patience for riddle-style lines. Leona and Mera both speak almost only in these.

**Splash pages:** there are none. Every one of 64 pages is a four-panel grid. The reveals that want a full page get a single panel:
- Halyard's glass towers (p33)
- the purifier running for the first time (p42)
- Oasis above the clouds (p48)
- the lever (p57)

**What they would cut or compress:**
- The 2099 frame, unless it comes back at the end.
- The four recruitment pages (p37–40). They are a fetch quest: each maker hands over one part and says one line.
- Two of the three procedure pages (p60–61).
- Mera's introduction. She first appears on p53 in mid-confession, and the reader doesn't know who she is.

**What a cold reader can't follow:**
- What Alpha is (named p21, explained p57).
- What the Houses are.
- What the Covenant bands do (p52, 56). The word "Covenant" appears only in a stage direction.
- That Leona is Ada's mother. It is implied, never said.
- What a Convenor is (p46).
- Who Morrow is (p63).

**Would they take the job?** Inference: yes, for Issue 01. It is a tight, complete pilot with a debt clock, a mentor, a dog and a verified lie. They would ask for a rewrite of Issue 03 before signing their name to it.

## A game designer

There is a game in it, and the book is close to a design document already.

- **Loop:** work a shift → earn credits → meet the weekly payment → spend on survival (lamp fuel *or* medicine, p15) → watch the debt clock (`28.7 YEARS`).
- **Choices that cost something:**
  - Leaving your zone to save Tomas costs `−40 CR` and makes you miss the loan payment (p24, p23).
  - Refusing Lock's deal keeps a 418k debt (p35–36).
  - Refusing the lever leaves `DEBT: ACTIVE` (p59).
  - Publishing the design trades exclusivity for safety (p44).

  These are real trade-offs, and the book lets them hurt.
- **Systems a player would push on:**
  - Water flow, with the Measure as a verification tool that catches reported against delivered.
  - A registry and patent economy (`312 PATENTS / 47 RESTRICTED / 19 DISPUTED`).
  - Building the purifier from four makers' parts, with tuning against overload (p41).
  - TJ's modular body, where the head, legs and magnets do separate jobs. That is an ideal puzzle mechanic.
  - Reputation with neighbours (Tomas keeps counting).
- **What's missing for them:** the debt number appears only twice, so the player never sees choices move it. And the shop economy on p63 is a ledger with no numbers.

**Would they build it?** Inference: yes. It is a resource-management and puzzle game with a companion who asks questions rather than giving hints.

## A streaming showrunner

- **The engine:**
  - A teenager born into a debt he didn't make, in a town being lied to about its water.
  - A dead (`PRESUMED DEAD / LEGAL STATUS: MISSING`, p55) gambler father who left behind a lab, a robot and 312 patents.
  - The debt clock is the series' spine.
- **Characters an audience would follow for a season:**
  - Alex.
  - Tomas, the most alive character in the book: dry, fair, keeps his word.
  - Ada, if she gets her own want.
  - TJ, the merchandise.
  - B11.

  Lock is a good villain who disappears after p43 and is never confronted. Alpha has no face (p57), which is honest but gives a season no one to hate. Leona and Mera arrive too late to carry an arc.
- **The first issue:** strong. The pilot is p11–28, and p12 (`Happy birthday, Phantum.` / `Twenty-eight point seven.`) is a cold-open-quality line. Issue 00 works as a teaser only if the series is built to arrive at 2099.
- **Season-one cliffhanger:** the natural midseason break is p46 (Ada and Alex split by the barrier). The finale, p64, is quiet and unresolved rather than a cliffhanger: Ada holds two papers, the debt stands, Caldera's crates are challenged but not connected. The cliffhanger they would want is already in the book and unused: is Adrian alive ("MISSING", p55), and who becomes "the old man with everything" (p3)?
- **What's missing:**
  - A face on the antagonist.
  - Ada's own goal.
  - A payoff for 2099.
  - One big set piece in Issue 03. Right now it is a hearing.

## Does it smell like school

**Pages where the scene exists to show how something works:**

1. **p60.** Alex lays out the Measure, the Seal and the band, residents feed in instruction strips, and the screen reads `THREE TEMPORARY REPRESENTATIVES / EACH AFFECTED PLACE / PUBLIC RECORD / PUBLIC CHALLENGE`. This is the climax, but it plays as a procedure diagram.
2. **p61.** Three labelled tables, three representatives each, "physical mandate strips." A resident's instruction ("Don't approve what we can't check") states the principle. This is the nearest thing to a whole page that exists to explain. It is saved by setting up p62's challenge, which pays off with a dry gauge.
3. **p63, panel 2.** The shop ledger separates `CUSTOMER PAYMENT / MATERIALS / MAINTENANCE / WAGES / EQUAL PROFIT SHARES`. One panel, plainly there for the reader.

**Count: 3.** None is a whole page with no other job, but p60–61 together are the stretch a skeptical 16-year-old will skim. The moral lines are single panels and won't lose anyone:
- Ada's "Don't make two people the answer for everybody" (p58)
- Mera's "I preserved order… It was not enough" (p53)
- Ren's "build it so a customer can repair it" (p40)

**Pages that teach without smelling of it, because people need the thing and pay for it:**
- **p15:** 45 credits for antibiotics, not enough cash, a neighbour's loan with written terms.
- **p23:** a missed payment, owned to his face and renegotiated in writing.
- **p12:** a debt balance and the years it will take to clear.
- **p24:** what leaving your post costs, in credits.
- **p28:** reported against delivered, caught by measuring.
- **p34:** who is listed as owner and who as creator, and what a 14-credit bonus means.
- **p42:** an honest label that limits your own sales claim.
- **p63, panel 3:** the receipt, `PAID`.

These are the book's best teaching, and none of it reads as a lesson.

The bigger risk for this audience is not school smell but confusion. There are too many unexplained terms and riddle lines in Issue 03 for a reader doing this the night before.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **Give B11 one scene where he tells a failure of his own, Bill's way: a question, then the story, then a plain line.** Put it in the flood conduit (p30–32) or in place of his "leave him" line on p25. *For Bill Reynolds.*
2. **Bring the 2099 frame back on the last page or two and show what "everything" (p3) is: what old Alex built and earned.** That answers the thieves' "Or water made him rich" and pays off "they'll come back." *For Young Americans Center and Bill (earned success on the page), the showrunner (the hook pays off) and teen readers (the opening means something).*
3. **Make Morrow & Sons a scene, not a sign.** A customer with a broken unit, a price, a repair, a paying return visit, and a rival or a House product the customer passed over. *For Young Americans Center: a business that serves people and makes money, on the page and not only in a ledger.*
4. **Fold the procedure on p60–61 into one page and spend the freed page on a confrontation with Lock.** *For teen readers and the school-smell count; for the showrunner and the comic writer, who want a villain with a face paid off; for Young Americans Center, who would rather see a crony beaten than a committee formed.*
5. **Establish early, in scene, who Leona is to Ada and what Alpha and the Houses are (Issue 01, around p14 and p20–21).** *For the students, who get only the pages, and for Young Americans Center, whose staff should not have to explain the book.*

`VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: yes; smells like school: no; school pages: 3`
