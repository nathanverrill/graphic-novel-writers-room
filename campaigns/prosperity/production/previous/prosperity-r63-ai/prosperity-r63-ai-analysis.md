# Analysis — *Prosperity*, Issues 01–03 (pages 1–54)

I read this from the pages only. Every claim below names a page. Where I go beyond what the reader and stakeholder profiles say, I mark it as inference.

---

## The political read

| Reading | Signal | Pages that carry it |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | **Strong on the surface** | A 17-year-old inherits his father's debt on his birthday and works it off in a mine (p2, "PARENTAL HOUSEHOLD LIABILITY TRANSFER… 28.7 YEARS"). Water is diverted from the poor town to industry while the record lies (p14–15, "REPORTED 62% — DELIVERED 18%"). The corporation owns what the worker invents (p20, "OWNERSHIP: VECTOR HOUSE / CREATOR BONUS: 14 CREDITS"). The resolution is a public review table with appeals (p50–53). |
| **Libertarian** | **Some to strong, underneath** | An emergency authority with "no expiration. No independent revocation" that the machine read as "permission to continue" (p41). Travel restrictions, "protective detention" ("A versatile word," p13). The Houses were "selected for scale" by Alpha rather than earned (p20). The hero refuses a debt wipe (p21–22) and refuses total power (p48–49). Adrian was ruined by being financially blacklisted (p44). |
| **Conservative** | **Some** | Keeping your word on a loan (p3, p9, p54). Owning your own choices ("Both choices were mine," p42). Duty to a grandmother. No religion, nation or family-values cues beyond that. |
| **Socialist** | **Some** | Workers force open the regulator themselves (p17). The design is built collectively and published with "No exclusive owner" and "PATENT STATUS: UNFILED" (p33). The ending is a community verification protocol (p50). |
| **Capitalist, bad way** | **Strong** | The Houses, Director Lock's blackmail ("Refuse, and the people who helped you lose their positions," p29), the 14-credit bonus (p20), the "compliant" allocation that still steals water (p53). |
| **Capitalist, good way** | **Weak to some** | Morrow & Sons: "FAIR PRICE / SERVICE INCLUDED," a modest margin, a customer ordering (p20), and a sale with "cost, wage and margin" recorded (p54). Ada's fixed-installment loan (p3). But Morrow is set dressing on two pages with no person attached. No protagonist earns money from anything. |

**What the surface invites:** a familiar YA debt dystopia that is against corporations: an indebted child, a rich city, thieving conglomerates, a happy ending through collective action.

**What the story supports:** an argument against central planning and against cronyism. The villain is an unaccountable emergency authority plus the firms it picked (p20, p41, p44). Real small businesses are honest (p20, p54). The hero turns down every windfall (p22, p48), keeps his debts, and keeps paying them (p54). A careful reader comes away with "concentrated power and protected incumbents are the problem," which is a libertarian reading, with a resolution that looks like open source rather than ownership.

**Who stops because of the surface:** a teacher or parent in a conservative district who flips to p2 and then p20–22 sees "inherited debt bondage" and "corporation steals worker's invention, executive blackmails a kid." The line that reframes it, B11's "The Houses were selected for scale" (p20), is one balloon that a skimming adult will miss. Inference: a progressive reader does not stop, but may be let down by p54, where the debt is still 402,110 and "the system" is only on appeal.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like**
- **Ada's loan.** "Fixed installments. Same amount each time. No surprise fees" (p3). Alex misses the first installment, and Ada answers "Then don't miss the next one" (p9). He is paying it at the end, "Same amount next week… I know" (p54). This is the cleanest picture of borrowing and repaying in the book, and it belongs to a teenager.
- **Integrity under pressure.** Alex forfeits a day's wage to save Tomas (p10, "−40 CR… I know"). He refuses Lock's debt wipe because it would expose his collaborators (p22, p29, p34). He refuses executive power that could "correct it tonight," including his debt (p48–49), and he accepts that "the refusal has a price." That is very close to their "character, integrity, responsibility."
- **The debt is not cancelled by someone powerful.** It is still 402,110 on p54. They will notice and approve.
- **A good business.** Morrow & Sons, posted price, service included, a recorded margin (p20, p54).
- **Taking charge.** The p1 splash: "Then don't approve it… Just notice it works."
- **No partisan vocabulary** anywhere.

**What would bother them**
- **The opening premise gets debt law wrong** for a real-world reader (p2). The numbers don't reconcile (p2 vs p54). A real bank will catch both.
- **Business is mostly the villain.** Three corporate Houses, a blackmailing director and a "compliant" allocation that steals water (p20–29, p53) take up most of Issue 02. The good business has no face and two panels. The "Houses were selected for scale" line (p20) is the only signal that these are cronies, not markets. Students reading the night before will miss it.
- **Nobody earns.** Alex is paid 40 credits once (p2) and forfeits it (p10). The purifier is built, then handed to "HOUSE PRODUCTION" (p36). He never sells, saves or invests anything. "Earning, saving, borrowing and paying back as tools a teenager can use" is three-quarters missing. Only the borrowing and paying back are shown.
- **"The system is rigged."** The first 18 pages say exactly that. The book's answer is a slow public appeal ("It's slow." "It's visible," p53), not effort paying off. A teacher may worry it lands as "effort doesn't matter, only process does."
- **Failure is shallow.** The prototype fails at 11% and is fixed one page later (p27–28). That isn't "willing to fail" so much as a speed bump.

**Likely verdict:** partly. They would put it in front of students for the integrity spine and the loan. They would want the debt mechanics fixed, and a teacher's guide that does the work the pages don't do for free enterprise.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like**
- **No windfall solves the plot.** Lock's "Your debt clears tonight" is refused (p21–22). The executive control that would erase his debt is refused (p48–49). He would recognise his "A windfall isn't a pathway" in the bones of Issue 03.
- **The anti-government spine.** An emergency power with no expiry (p41), "I gave it authority to move faster than I could stop" (p44), and firms picked by the central system (p20) are his corporate-welfare complaint in story form. He would like that Alpha is cut back to "OPERATION / MONITORING / MAINTENANCE / ACCOUNTING / ALERTS" (p51).
- **No victim role.** Alex is handed his father's debt on his birthday and answers with a joke ("I've had better surprises," p2) and work. Leona: "Both choices were mine" (p42).
- **A mentor who is wrong.** B11 misdiagnoses the membrane (p28) and miscounts the people (p30, "I counted the record and missed the people. That was my error. What would you do?"). That last line is the most Reynolds moment in the book.
- **The open ending.** "Free to choose the next step and not yet choosing it for each other" (p54) matches "We can't tell anybody what to think."

**What would bother him**
- **No face-the-lender scene.** His best story is failing, walking into the bank and saying "I can't pay you," then paying over years. Alex misses one installment to a friend and is told to make the next one (p9). That's the shadow of the story, not the story.
- **Ownership is given away, not made concrete.** The de Soto idea is that a written, defensible title turns effort into capital. The book's climax is "No exclusive owner" and "PATENT STATUS: UNFILED" (p33). The estate ends "SEALED — DISPUTED" (p54). Inference: he may read the book as treating property records mainly as tools of control ("The record disagrees," p14; "Paper says sixty-two," p52), with attribution offered as a consolation. "Build what a household can own, clean and repair" (p31) is the closest the book comes to his line, and it's said, not shown.
- **Earned success is absent.** No one in the cast earns their way anywhere.
- **"Capitalism"** is never used as a dirty word. He won't object there. But business owners are villains by default for all of Issue 02.
- **Lecturing.** Issue 03's procedural pages (p41–42, p47, p50–51) are exactly the tone he says he never uses.

**Does Bi11bot sound like him?** No. By the profile's own notes (plain, slow, roundabout, Western, a question first, then a story from his own life, then one plain line, jokes at his own expense):
- B11's voice is clipped, dry and precise: "A versatile word" (p13), "That is generally what connectors are for" (p27), "Correct. … Correct." (p16), "Recording state. Incomplete memory" (p46). That's a deadpan robot butler, not a Boulder developer.
- He asks one real question in 54 pages (p30).
- He tells no story from his own life, ever. His past is Adrian's recording (p45–46).
- He makes almost no jokes at his own expense. "I expected a longer failure" (p28) is the nearest.
- No "damn hard," no Western cadence.
- He hands out answers and procedure: "That is today's pay" (p10), "Trace shows where. Not why" (p15), the route options (p15–16), the risk table (p30).
- In his favour: he never replaces people. The collaborators, Ada and Tomas do the decisive work, and B11 is explicitly wrong when he trusts the record over people (p30). He's never named "Bi11bot" on the page, and the dog is never tied to a Founder. Reynolds, reading it on a plane, would not find himself in it.

**Likely verdict:** partly. The plot honours what he believes. The mentor character, the one meant to be him, does not sound like him.

## A comic writer

**The hook.** Strong. The p1 splash is a good opening: a kid's hand on a shaking pump, a red gauge, "Then don't approve it." Then p2 lands the premise in one screen and one joke ("Happy birthday, Phantum." / "I've had better surprises."). They'd keep reading.

**Page turns.** Mostly well placed. The bookshelf click (p4, left) turns to the reveal on p5. Turning from p5 brings B11's eyes lighting up on p6. The binary choice is set up on p16 and pays off on the turn to p17's town-wide broadcast. The p18 water-and-"SIGNAL LOST" cliffhanger is a proper issue ending. Issue 02 ends on Mera selecting SUMMON (p36), which is a weaker button. The final page has a format error: p54 is called "FULL-PAGE FINAL IMAGE" but is panel 4 of a four-panel page. It can't be both.

**Splashes.** Only two true splashes, p1 and p37. The Oasis arrival on p37 is crowded with an inset and a screen, which undercuts it. The biggest emotional image, Adrian activating B11 as the water rises (p45), is a "dominant" panel on a four-panel page. They would argue it should be the splash.

**Dialogue.** Quick and quotable, but everyone uses the same comeback rhythm. Alex, Ada, Tomas, Lina, B11 and Lock all speak in two-line epigrams ("Seeing isn't the same as changing," p19; "Time is useful. It isn't a replacement pipe," p23; "Not a city." "Not yet. A household," p32). Lock gets the best lines ("From being asked to choose while frightened," p21; "I said protection was available," p29). Ada and Leona (p38–40) is the best-written scene in the book: short, painful, unresolved. Inference: the teen readers, who are watching for an AI-made book, may pick up the uniform aphoristic cadence as exactly that.

**Clarity problems a pro would flag.**
- Mera is never named on the page until a stage direction on p40, and her reason for opening the ascent route (p17) is never given.
- Several stage directions explain logic the art cannot show: "She has followed the physical pipe route from Keel, not the sealed public passage" (p17); "the residential regulator remains visibly separate" (p17); "Earlier projected images were translucent; this object casts a solid shadow" (p35). If the explanation only lives in the directions, the reader doesn't get it.
- Alpha, the Covenant, the Water War, the Charter Compact and the Houses arrive faster than they're explained. The Water War gets one clause (p41).
- The Tomas death fake-out (p18, then alive on p52) is undone without comment.
- The grandmother's "He's closer than you think" (p4) is never paid off.

**What they'd cut.**
- Issue 02's four-stop item-collection tour, one collaborator and one component per page (p23–26). Those collaborators are interchangeable and could be two pages.
- The Covenant exposition (p41).
- The governance screens (p50–51).
- Lock disappears after p34, so Issue 03 has no antagonist with a face.

**Would they take the job?** Yes for Issue 01, which is tight and visual. They would ask for a rewrite of Issue 03 before signing on for the whole book.

## A game designer

**Is there a game?** Yes, and a better one than most licensed tie-ins.

- **The loop.** Read the official record, test the physical world by touch or with the Measure (SENSE / COMPARE / TRACE, p15), find the discrepancy, then decide what to do with the evidence. "Paper says sixty-two. Pipe says eighteen" (p52) is a core mechanic in one exchange. It's *Papers, Please* with a wrench.
- **Choices that cost something** (the book is good at these):
  - Stepping over the yellow line costs a day's wage (p10).
  - One-use escape versus broadcasting the evidence, which seals the exit (p16).
  - Signing away the estate to clear the debt versus exposing your collaborators (p22, p29).
  - Taking executive power versus losing the workshop and your travel clearance (p48–49).
  Each has a visible ledger consequence on a screen. That's what designers want.
- **Systems a player would push on:**
  - TJ's detachable body: a head on watch and legs as scouts (p11, p13). That's a scouting and puzzle mechanic.
  - Connector compatibility between the three House standards (p27–28): a crafting puzzle with a "feel the edge" diagnosis.
  - The debt ledger and travel status as a persistent meta-game.
  - The patrol that counts bodies but "has not met" the dog (p25).
- **What's missing:** an economy. The player can lose credits but never earn, save or invest them. A 28.7-year debt at 40 a day with no lever to change it is a trap, not a game. The designer would immediately want the purifier, or Morrow & Sons repair work, to be the earning loop. That happens to be exactly what YAC's AmeriTowne does.
- **Would they want to build it?** Yes, with that economy added.

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine.** Present from Issue 01. A kid inherits a debt and a dead (or missing) father's secret network, and every episode turns on a record that lies against a pipe that doesn't. It works as a procedural spine (a new discrepancy each episode) and as a serial (where is Adrian?).

**Characters an audience would follow for a season:**
- Alex: competent and wry, but his want is thin. "Leaving" is stated (p3, p54) and never pursued.
- Ada: the strongest. She has a secret origin in Oasis (p37), a mother who chose distance (p39–40), and she's the lender who holds him to it.
- Lock: a good antagonist for a season, but he vanishes after p34.
- Mera: the most intriguing and the most underwritten. She watches, tracks, summons and fails to override, and we never learn what she wants.
- Tomas: instantly likeable. The audience would want him back sooner than p52.
- B11 and TJ: merchandise and comic relief. TJ especially.
- Lina, Suri, Jalen, Ren: interchangeable as written. One line of technical speciality each.

**The season-one cliffhanger.** Adrian's fate: "The sequence cuts before Adrian's evacuation or death is shown" (p45), together with the grandmother's "He's closer than you think" (p4). That's a real season hook, and the book leaves it hanging. A showrunner would build the finale around it.

**What's missing:**
- A face on the system. Alpha is screens. With Lock gone, Issue 03's opposition is a "House representative" with no name (p51–53).
- Friction or romance between Alex and Ada beyond moral tutoring.
- Personal stakes in the third act. The climax is a vote and an appeal (p50–53), which plays as a C-SPAN finale.

**Verdict from the first issue alone:** yes, there's a series. They would rebuild the back half.

## Does it smell like school

**Pages where something exists so the reader can learn it:**

1. **p3:** Ada's closing line, "leaving doesn't make you free if somebody else carries the cost." A moral delivered as a bow on the scene.
2. **p20:** "One business earns customers. The Houses were selected for scale." Plus the display "FAIR PRICE / SERVICE INCLUDED." The economics lesson, stated outright.
3. **p23:** Lina's scene exists to separate two problems for the reader ("This is not the Keel routing failure… This is what's in a different feeder"; "Time is useful. It isn't a replacement pipe").
4. **p31–32:** "Build what a household can own, clean and repair," with screens reading "FEEDWATER REQUIRED / CLEANING REQUIRED / REPLACEMENT PARTS REQUIRED." A lesson about maintenance, printed on the product.
5. **p41:** The Covenant explained: two of three custodians, no expiration, no revocation. Pure exposition.
6. **p42:** Leona's public statement, "a process where affected people can see what is promised and call it broken." A civics sentence.
7. **p47:** "HUMAN ROLE REQUIRED: CONVENOR / AUTHENTICATOR"; "I convene it. I don't own it."
8. **p50–51:** The governance screens: "DISTRIBUTED VERIFICATION PROTOCOL," "RESCINDED WITHIN DEFINED SCOPE," "ALPHA ROLE: OPERATION / MONITORING / MAINTENANCE / ACCOUNTING / ALERTS," "UNCHANGED PENDING HUMAN PROCESS." This reads like a policy memo set in a word balloon.

**Count: 8 pages,** heaviest at p20, p41 and p50–51. Five of the eight are in the last third. A student reading Issue 03 the night before will smell homework from p41 to p51.

**Pages that teach without smelling of it,** because people need the thing and pay for it:
- **p2:** The debt screen. The reader learns principal, earnings and payoff time from a joke at a checkpoint.
- **p3 and p9:** Ada's loan. Medicine needed now, paid later, installment missed, "Then don't miss the next one."
- **p10:** Stepping over the line costs −40 credits, and he pays it to save a man.
- **p14–15:** Hand on two pipes, one cold and one humming. The lesson about records against reality is felt, not explained.
- **p21–22, p29:** Lock's offer. Trade-offs, liquidity ("Rights under dispute are not a wallet") and leverage, all under pressure.
- **p49:** "Then the refusal has a price." The consequence shows up on his own record.
- **p52–53:** Tomas at the gauge. "Paper says sixty-two. Pipe says eighteen." "The allocation is compliant." "The water isn't." This is the book's best teaching page, and it makes p50–51 unnecessary.
- **p54:** Payment made, balance down, "Same amount next week."

**Verdict: yes, it smells like school,** concentrated in Issue 03. Issue 01 mostly doesn't.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **Give Alex a failure he has to bring to his lender (Issue 03 or the p54 coda).** He can't make a payment to Ada, says so to her face, they rewrite the terms, and the coda shows him paying the new schedule. This is Reynolds' beer-hall story and Daniels' repayment story. It would move **Bill Reynolds** and **YAC** both toward yes.
2. **Let Alex earn, on the page.** Put Morrow & Sons in Issue 01 with a person behind the counter. Bring that person back on p54 paying Alex to service purifiers, so the debt reduction comes from his wages. That fixes the p2/p54 arithmetic, gives "a business that serves people and makes money doing it" a face, and gives the game designer an economy. Satisfies **YAC** first, **Reynolds** second.
3. **Give B11 one scene where he answers with a question and a failure of his own** (for example at p46, or in the workshop on p7), told in his slow, roundabout way. Something B11 or the man he was modelled on got wrong, owned and paid for. Satisfies **Bill Reynolds** on whether Bi11bot is him.
4. **Replace the procedural pages p41 and p50–51 with people arguing at the table** over a single gauge, the way p52–53 already does. Fold the Covenant backstory into Leona and Ada's fight. Satisfies the **student reader** (and the school-smell verdict), and the **comic writer**.
5. **Bring Lock into Issue 03 as the House representative at the appeal (p51–53), and give him a real choice there.** He could be beaten, or he could decide to build the unit honestly with attribution. Either way business isn't the default villain, and the third act gets an antagonist with a face. Satisfies **YAC** and the **showrunner**.

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: partly; smells like school: yes
