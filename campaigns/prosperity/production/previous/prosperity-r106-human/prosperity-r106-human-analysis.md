# Analysis: *Prosperity*, pages 1–64

I read the pages only, in order, the way a 16-year-old reads them the night before they're due. Page numbers are the script's. Where I go past what the profiles say, I mark it **inference**.

---

## The political read

| Reading | Signal | Pages that carry it |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | Strong on the surface, some underneath | Debt passed to a 17-year-old by "PARENTAL LIABILITY TRANSFER" (p12). Pay docked for leaving your zone to save a man (p24, "WAGE FORFEITED: −40 CR"). A corporation owns a worker's fix and pays him "CREATOR BONUS: 14 CREDITS" (p34, "My correction is in there"). A civic hall with "affected residents" (p52). |
| **Libertarian** | Strong in the story | Alex turns down Lock's offer to wipe his debt (p35–36). He turns down the single-authority lever and the "DEBT: CLEARANCE AVAILABLE" that comes with it (p58–59, "No executive designation"). The villain is a planning machine, Alpha, that nobody can switch off (p53, "SCOPE EXCEEDED"). Lina says "Don't sign anything" (p46). Recording who made something is treated as protection (p44). |
| **Conservative** | Some | Keeping your word on a loan (p15, 23, 63). Family duty: medicine for Grandmother (p15–16). An estranged mother who owns what she did (p50, "I made the safer choice. It hurt you anyway"). |
| **Socialist** | Some | "Workers close ranks… This is just where we stand" (p29). The design is released "PUBLIC / NONEXCLUSIVE" (p44). Three tables of temporary representatives allocating "COMMUNITY SUPPLY" (p61). A shop co-owned by five makers (p63). |
| **Capitalist in a bad way** | Strong on the surface | Keel works like a company town: shift assignment, travel restriction, wage forfeiture (p11–12, 22, 24). Caldera House owns the work and leaves the maker "UNLISTED" (p34). Lock offers a deal that buys out the contributors (p35). Caldera keeps "420 UNITS" in a yard, "NOT CONNECTED" (p62). In the prologue a thief says of old Alex, "Or he made water pay for it" (p3). |
| **Capitalist in a good way** | Some, and it lands late | Ren: "Customers should be able to fix it" (p40). The display is honest about what the machine can't do, "NOT A POTABLE-OUTPUT GUARANTEE", and Alex takes the cup away from a customer (p42). On p63 a customer brings in a broken casing, Ren quotes a price, the customer "counts credits and agrees," and the shop has a sign with the makers' names on it. |

**What the surface invites:** an anti-corporate dystopia. For about 40 pages the institutions with power over Alex's life are an employer that docks pay and restricts travel, and Houses that take credit for workers' inventions. A reader flipping through Issue 01 sees debt bondage in a mining town.

**What the story supports:** a book against monopoly and central planning that is on the side of honest business. The real villains are Alpha (a planner no human can overrule, p53, 57), the Houses' grip on other people's work and on inventory (p34, 62), and Lock's buyout, which comes with "COLLABORATOR PROTECTION" (p35). The heroes refuse every shortcut and every giveaway. They build a product, label it honestly, open a repair shop and pay off a loan. Nobody in the book says "capitalism," and no one in a market is drawn as a villain just for being in it.

**Who stops reading because of the surface:**
- A conservative parent in a district, or a centre-right teacher, who flips through pp. 11–24 and sees "company town, inherited debt, docked wages." They may file it as an anti-business book before reaching p35.
- A progressive teacher who reads to the end may be puzzled, not pleased. The refusal of debt relief (p59, "DEBT: ACTIVE") reads as the book siding with the debt.

**Does the book conclude, or leave a reader concluding, that free enterprise is bad?** **No.** The ending is a customer paying for a repair at a privately run shop (p63) and a loan repaid on its terms (p63). The power that gets turned down is a single executive authority (p59–60), not a market. The risk is p44, where the makers release the design public. A reader could take that as "owning the design was the problem." The page itself frames it as stopping Caldera's "EXCLUSIVE CLAIM," not as a rejection of ownership.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like**
- **Tomas's loan (p15, 23, 63).** It's exactly what they teach. Terms are written on the receipt ("Friday." / "Make it real."). Alex misses a payment and shows up anyway with nothing ("You brought your face." / "Lost the money."). He rewrites the terms himself ("20 CREDITS NEXT FRIDAY"). Months later he pays in full ("MEDICINE ADVANCE: PAID / WEEKLY TERMS: COMPLETE"). This is integrity under pressure and borrowing shown as a tool, and it's the best sequence in the book for them.
- **Refusing the bailout, twice (p35–36, 58–59).** The debt is not cancelled by someone powerful. On p63 the balance has fallen from 418,607 to 402,110 because he paid it down. They'd like that the big debt is still there.
- **A business that serves people (p40–42, 63).** Repairable design, honest labelling, a customer agreeing to a price. Close to YouthBiz in spirit.
- **Initiative and failure.** Alex's prototype fails before it works (p38, 41: "Smaller failure."). That's their "willing to fail."

**What would bother them**
- **The 418,607-credit debt is something done to a child by a machine (p12).** "Debt as something done to you" is on their objection list. The book doesn't let anyone cancel it, which helps. But it never says whether the debt is legitimate, so a student can't tell whether paying it is integrity or submission.
- **Gambling as the key to the plot (p16–17).**
- **How Keel looks in Issue 01.** A teacher in a conservative district who flips through it sees a company-town dystopia, which is close to "the system is rigged." The book says effort matters (p63), but that point arrives 50 pages later.
- **Money mechanics are thin after p23.** We're told the shop earns (p63) but never see how the five owners split it, or how a 16,000-credit paydown happened. As a bank they'd want one page that gets this right on screen.
- **Issue 03 is mostly committee procedure** ("CONVENOR PASS," "DESIGNATED STRUCTURE REGISTERED"). Neutral politically, but not their world.

**Likely verdict:** satisfied in part. They'd use Issue 01 and the Tomas pages in a classroom tomorrow. They'd hesitate over the inherited-debt premise and the casino chip, and Issue 03 gives them little.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like**
- **His beer-hall story, more or less (p15, 23, 63).** A young man borrows, fails ("Lost the money."), goes back to the lender's face, renegotiates, and pays it off over time. He would recognise himself.
- **No windfall solves the plot.** Lock's assignment (p35) and Alpha's clearance (p58) are both turned down. "A windfall isn't a pathway" is acted out, never said.
- **A mentor who is wrong and asks a question.** B11 on p25: "I predicted we could leave him unseen." / "You were wrong." / "Yes. What would you do?" That's the line he'd underline. On p31 B11 tells a failure story about himself: "I once mapped a flood route backward for six hours… It taught me to stop calling the wall a door." B11 doesn't stand in for people. Alex overrules him and the people around Alex carry the plot.
- **Ownership written down.** The registry naming the five contributors (p44), "It records who made this," is de Soto's "written down, recorded and defensible."
- **Rejecting the single authority (p57–59).** Mera's machine is a planner nobody can stop. That fits what he says about government that wants "to control you."
- **Old Alex in the prologue owns "everything" (p3).** Read kindly, that's earned success from a boy who started 418,000 in debt (**inference**: the book never shows how he got there).

**What would bother him**
- **B11 doesn't sound like him.** By his own voice notes (plain, slow, a bit roundabout, Western, a question first, then a story from his own life, then one plain line, jokes at his own expense), B11 gets the structure right once (p31) and the question right once (p25). Elsewhere B11 is clipped and dry: "Stored. Sleep is more comfortable." / "A fact with a poor record." / "That costs more." It's machine wit, not a Boulder rancher's drawl. Nothing is "damn hard." Nothing is slow. The page never calls him Bi11bot, only "B11." And B11 is close to silent in Issue 03's climax (p54–55 only). The moral voice at the turning point is Ada's.
- **The dog.** TJ may be named for a Founder (Thomas Jefferson, **inference**), but the page never says so. A reader won't catch it, so the beat he asked for doesn't land.
- **The replacement is committees and queues.** After the planner falls, Alex's freedom to travel is "ELIGIBLE FOR PUBLIC REVIEW" with "REVIEW QUEUE 18" (p63–64). A libertarian may ask why getting out of Keel still needs permission.
- **The design released public (p44).** He'd want ownership to be what lets effort become capital. Giving it away as "NONEXCLUSIVE" cuts against that, even though it beats a House monopoly.
- **Ada's lines on p58–59** ("If you hold all the keys, everyone waits for your hand." / "And who checks what you miss?") are short, but they're the book telling, not asking.

**Likely verdict:** satisfied in part. The spine of the book is his life story, and he'd feel it. He would not recognise his own voice in the robot carrying his name.

## A comic writer

**The hook.** Mixed. Issue 01 has a great one: p12's checkpoint screen, "AGE VERIFIED: SEVENTEEN… DEBT: 418,607 CREDITS… PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS," and "Happy birthday, Phantum." It's a left-page reveal after a turn, and that's the book's best page. But a pitch reader meets Issue 00 first: ten mostly silent pages of a robot dog coming apart across a lake (p1–10). It looks good, but it's hard to follow on a first read. The head, torso and legs travel separately (p2–3, 7). On p1 "TJ's brass ring circles his right eye" and on p18 the same ring is B11's, so a reader can't tell which robot is which. On p9 the component and the casino chip blur together. The returning barge on p10 never comes back in the remaining 54 pages. A pro would ask why the pitch doesn't open on p11.

**Page turns.** Left-page reveals are mostly well placed: p12 debt screen, p18 B11 wakes, p28 verification goes public, p46 Lina taken, p58 the lever. Right-page cliffs on p27 ("That's not a rounding error") and p45 work.

**Splash pages.** None. Every one of 64 pages runs four panels. The storm drain opening into the glass towers (p33), Oasis above the clouds (p48), and the public displays flipping across Keel (p28) are all begging for a splash. The even rhythm flattens the big moments.

**Dialogue.** Lean, and often sharp: "You brought your face." (p23), "Go. I'm about to become paperwork." (p26), "We're already dispersed." (p29), "It is. The destination objected." (p30), "Inventory isn't delivery." (p62). The weakness is that everyone talks the same: terse, wry, three to six words. Tomas, Ada, B11 and Lina could swap lines. A pro would also flag undefined jargon: "convenor," "Houses," Alpha (first seen only as a readout on p20), "designated structure."

**What they'd cut.** Squeeze the prologue to about four pages, or move it to the end. Fold the four one-page contributor visits (p37–40) into two. Strip Issue 03's procedure (p52, 60–61) down to the one human beat on each page. They'd also want an introduction for Leona and Mera: Leona's link to Ada (mother? "You're taller," p49) is never stated, and "Lina Vey" against "Leona Veyra" suggests a relation that is never confirmed.

**Would they take the job?** Yes, on Issue 01 alone, and they'd want a rewrite of Issue 03, whose climax is people placing documents on a table (p60).

## A game designer

**Is there a game?** Yes, and a clean one. It's closer to the partner's simulated-town programs than to anything else in the book.

- **The loop:** take a shift, earn credits, pay your weekly terms, fix things. Each Friday Tomas's due date arrives (p15, 23), and the 28.7-year clock ticks in the background (p12).
- **Choices that cost something:** leaving your zone to save Tomas at −40 CR (p24). Answers or an exit (p19, and B11 says answers "cost more"). Signing Lock's assignment to wipe the debt or keeping your contributors (p35). Taking the lever and clearing everything, or not (p58–59). The book already prices its choices.
- **Systems to push on:**
  - The Measure, comparing what's reported with what's delivered (p27, 62: "62% / 18%," "Inventory isn't delivery"). That's a natural audit mechanic.
  - Modular, repairable builds where each contributor adds a part and the goal is "smaller failure" (p39, 41–42).
  - TJ's detachable body as a puzzle tool (p2–7, 25).
  - The ownership registry, where attribution and exclusive claims are a resource (p34, 44).
- **What a player would want and won't find:** a way to grow the shop. Hire, set prices, reinvest. p63 shows the business but gives it no levers to pull.

**Would they build it?** Yes. A designer would see a debt-and-repair management game with an audit tool, and the Tomas loan is a ready-made tutorial.

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine.** A 17-year-old handed his missing gambler father's debt, in a company town where the water is being skimmed, with the father's hidden workshop and two robots as the inheritance. It works. The 2099 frame, where old Alex is "the old man with everything" (p3), adds a how-did-he-get-there question across seasons (a *This Is Us* / *Better Call Saul* shape, **inference**).

**Characters an audience would follow for a season:** Alex. Ada, the strongest presence and the one with a family wound (p49–51). Tomas, the lender who keeps a door shut for the father and stays behind as "paperwork" (p26), the book's best supporting role. B11, if he gets a personality bigger than his quips. TJ will sell merchandise.

**Season-one cliffhanger.** p28: Alex makes the theft public and the gate slams shut on Tomas as the enforcement beams close in. Or p46: Lina dragged off, Ada behind glass. Either ends an episode six well.

**What's missing**
- **An antagonist with a face.** Lock vanishes after p43 with nothing settled. Alpha "has no face or body" (p57). The climax has no opponent.
- **Adrian's fate.** "PRESUMED DEAD / LEGAL STATUS: MISSING" (p55). Good for a series, but the book does nothing with it.
- **The prologue's threat**, the returning barge (p10), is left hanging.
- **What Alex wants.** He says "I'm getting out" (p14), and then the book ends on "And you?" (p64) with Ada. Is there a romance or not? A season needs that tension written in, not left blank.

**Verdict:** there's a series in Issues 00–01. Issue 03 plays like a season finale of committee procedure, and a showrunner would rebuild it.

## Does it smell like school

Pages where a character explains something so the reader can learn it, a line states the lesson, or a scene exists to show a civic procedure:

1. **p44.** Lina: "It records who made this. It doesn't make you owner of us." A definition delivered as dialogue: attribution versus ownership.
2. **p58.** Ada: "If you hold all the keys, everyone waits for your hand." The moral, stated outright at the climax.
3. **p59.** Ada: "And who checks what you miss?", then the cancelled preview and "No executive designation." The checks-and-balances lesson, said aloud. The temptation is real (his debt is on the screen), which helps, but a teen hears civics.
4. **p60.** A whole page of documents placed on a table and a "process field" signed, ending "DESIGNATED STRUCTURE REGISTERED / EMERGENCY AUTHORITY RESCINDED." No conflict on the page. It exists to show a governing structure being set up.

Borderline, not counted: p52 (the clerk marks "PUBLIC REVIEW ACCEPTED" before the band links, mostly procedure) and p61 (three tables of representatives; saved by Tomas's challenge).

**Count: 4.** Pages 58–60 are three pages of civics in a row at the climax, and p60 is a whole page that exists to show a process. A skeptical 16-year-old will spot it.

**Pages that teach without smelling of it,** because people need the thing and pay for it:
- **p12:** the debt screen. Debt, term and restriction are shown as a threat to one boy.
- **p15:** Grandmother's 45-credit antibiotic, "medicine or lamp fuel," a loan with written terms.
- **p23:** missing a payment and renegotiating face to face.
- **p24:** the price of doing the right thing, −40 CR.
- **p27:** reported versus delivered, as a mystery reveal.
- **p42:** honest labelling. Alex takes the cup away from a customer.
- **p62:** "Inventory isn't delivery," with the dry gauge as proof.
- **p63:** a customer counts credits and agrees to a price; the loan receipt is paid; the debt is lower.

These carry more economics than pages 58–60 and nobody will call them homework.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **Rebuild pages 58–60 so the choice happens in front of someone who'd pay for it** (Bill Reynolds; the school-smell count). For example, Tomas or a Keel resident is in the room and stands to gain if Alex takes the lever. The refusal costs a person, not an argument. Ada's two moral lines and the page of document-placing go.
2. **Give the 418,607-credit debt one face-to-face scene with whoever holds it** (Young Americans Center; Bill). For instance, Alex sits down with the creditor and splits or restructures the note, the way Tomas's loan works. The big debt becomes something he negotiates and owns, not something a scanner did to him.
3. **One B11 scene in Bill's shape** (Bill). B11 asks a question first, tells a slow, slightly roundabout story of his own failure with a joke at his own expense, and lands on one plain line. Put it where Alex has to decide something himself, ideally Issue 03. The same scene can say TJ's full name.
4. **One page where Morrow & Sons earns and splits money** (Young Americans Center). A customer pays, the five owners divide it, and a share goes to the debt. That would show how the 418,607 became 402,110 on p63. It also needs a fix for the "& Sons" sign or an explanation of Morrow.
5. **Cut the prologue to a few pages or move it to the end, and keep TJ's and B11's eye rings distinct** (the student reader; the comic writer; the showrunner). The book would open on p12's debt screen within the first few page turns, and the dangling barge either pays off or goes.

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: partly; smells like school: yes; school pages: 4
