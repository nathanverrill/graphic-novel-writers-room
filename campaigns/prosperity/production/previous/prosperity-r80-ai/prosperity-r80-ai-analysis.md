# Analysis: Prosperity, Issues 01–03 (pp. 1–54)

I read the script cold, pages only. Every claim below cites a page.

---

## First, the reader the book is assigned to

This is a 16-year-old reading it the night before it's due, already suspicious that a machine wrote it.

- **Issue 1 holds them.** Page 1 is a kid fixing a pipe against orders. Page 2 is a birthday that hands him **418,607 CREDITS** of his father's debt and **PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS**. That is a hook a teenager understands without help. From page 3 on, the deal with Inez and the missed payment on page 4 have stakes a reader feels.
- **They will trip over the names.** Several terms are never introduced:
  - "Alpha" appears on p.14 ("Alpha's data line") and p.15 ("Alpha's node") and is never explained.
  - "Fitch" (p.3, "Five fitch each shift") is a second currency in a book that otherwise counts in credits.
  - "Water Wars" first appears on p.41.
  - The Covenant bands (pp.36, 39–42, 47) run Issue 3, and their rules are never shown before they matter.
  - **Mera** (the Oasis official) and **Mara** (the Morrow & Sons shopkeeper) are one letter apart. Ada **Veyra** and Leona **Veyra** share a surname. A first-time reader will mix them up.
- **The lines read like riddles.** "A fact with a poor record" (p.6), "Thin still carries" (p.30) and "Then I become a pattern" (p.25) are the kind of riddle-style prose this reader has no patience for.
- **The AI smell is real.** The same sentence shapes keep coming back:
  - "Same X. Different Y." three times: p.5 "Same hand. Different damage."; p.20 "Same part. Different downside."; p.23 "Same shortage. Different problem."
  - "Not X. Y." again and again: p.3 "Not because you bought a token. Because you don't know one."; p.17 "Open the segment. Not the homes."; p.19 "Not dead. Not safe."; p.24 "Not a heroic one."
  - Nearly every character talks in the same dry, clipped way (Tomas, Ren, Lina, Ada, B11).

  A reader hunting for signs of AI will find these patterns. They are what gets the book called "AI slop" in class the next morning.
- **Where they quit:** Issue 3. Pages 37–53 are mostly a hearing: allocation requests, declared interests, a clerk, an approval stamp. A reader who made it through Halyard's chases is likely to stop around pp.41–43.

**What the surface suggests:** "The system is rigged against a poor kid, and big corporations steal from workers." That comes from page 2's debt, the 28.7-year projection, the wage taken on p.10, and Issue 2 opening on corporate Houses.

**What the story actually backs:** the villains are a central system with no expiry date (Issue 1, Issue 3) and cronies who use charters and registries to own other people's work (Issue 2). Against them stand recorded ownership and attribution (pp.33–35), honest small business (Inez, Morrow), refusing a windfall (p.33) and refusing unearned power (p.49). Nobody cancels the debt. It gets paid down by work (p.54).

**Who stops because of the surface:**
- A YAC staffer or a conservative parent who reads only Issue 1 sees a kid crushed by inherited debt and a wage system that punishes decency. Nothing in Issue 1 yet shows a market that works, apart from Inez. That reader may stop before Morrow appears on p.20.
- A progressive teacher is not driven off by the surface. They may notice the p.22 "They built it" exchange, but they keep reading.

**Does the book conclude, or leave a reader concluding, that free enterprise is bad? No.** Honest business and recorded ownership are the positive poles (pp.3–4, 20, 31–32, 33–35, 54). The villains are monopolists and planners (pp.21–22, 41, 44). The ending leaves debt and ownership standing (p.50) and shows the debt being repaid (p.54).

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like**
- **Pages 3–4 and 54 are their story.** A young person borrows, misses the date, says so himself ("I'm not asking you to erase it"), and later the ledger reads **INEZ QUISPE — MEDICINE ADVANCE: PAID**. The lender is decent and also needs the money ("Good. I need the money"). This is the Bill Daniels story done properly.
- **Morrow & Sons is the business they would invent.** It serves people and makes money doing it: "We fix what we sold" and **SHOP MARGIN — POSTED** on p.32, and the plain trade-off "A House builds faster. I can tell you what it costs."
- **Integrity under pressure on p.33.** Debt cleared tonight, or the collaborators keep their names. He says "No."
- **No windfall, no forgiveness.** p.50 says **EXISTING DEBT — UNCHANGED**, and the debt is paid down by work on p.54.
- **The p.31 trade-off.** "Lower output. Maintainable." / **REPLACEMENT PARTS REQUIRED.** That is product thinking a YouthBiz kid would recognise.
- **No bank is a villain.** The only lender in the book is honest.

**What would bother them**
- **The premise on p.2 is debt as something done to you.** It isn't cancelled, which helps, but the opening image is still a rigged system. A teacher in a progressive district gets "inherited debt crushes the poor" from Issue 1.
- **The finance doesn't add up.** Inherited liability doesn't work this way. The balance on p.54 has no visible timeline. "Fitch" versus credits is a second currency in one scene (p.3). They are a real bank and will notice.
- **Ren's "theft" line on p.25.**
- **Issue 3 is civics, not financial literacy.** Emergency powers and allocation hearings are not their curriculum. Earning, saving and borrowing drop out of the book between p.4 and p.54.
- **The p.22 "They built it" exchange** is the kind of partisan wink they keep out of public schools.

**Likely verdict: partly.** They would put pp.3–4, 32 and 54 in front of students happily and would want the debt premise and its arithmetic fixed before signing off. The line is not crossed.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like**
- **No windfall solves the plot.** He refuses 312 patents turned into instant money (p.21: "I can make it spendable tonight"; p.33: "No"). The debt stays. He pays.
- **His beer-hall story is in it.** Alex fails, faces the lender, doesn't play the victim, and pays over time (pp.4, 54).
- **Ownership made concrete, de Soto-style.** Work that isn't recorded is lost. Once it is recorded under the right name, it can be defended (p.24: **CREATOR: UNLISTED**; p.34, attribution corrected to **LINA VEY**; p.35: **PROPERTY TRANSFER: NONE**).
- **Cronies as villains.** The Houses get "Protection from retaliation" (p.22) and use charter reviews to own other people's work. That's his "going up to the government, getting money" complaint, in fiction.
- **Government that wants control.** Emergency authority with no ending (p.41), and the hero who won't pull the lever (p.49).
- **A mentor who is sometimes wrong and asks.** p.29: "I counted the record and missed the people. What would you do?" p.46: "I may have mistaken that for an instruction." B11 doesn't replace people: on p.49 Alex turns to the people in the room, not the robot.
- **A dog**, TJ (p.11). If he reads TJ as Thomas Jefferson, he'll be pleased. The book never says so, so that's inference.
- **The word "capitalism" never appears.**

**What would bother them**
- **Bi11bot doesn't sound like him.** His voice notes: plain, slow, roundabout, Western, a question, then a story from his own life, then one plain line, jokes at his own expense.
  - B11 does have the question (p.6: "Would you like the route, or the question first?"; p.29: "What would you do?") and the self-mockery (p.28: "I expected a longer failure").
  - B11 does not have the stories. He never tells one from his own past.
  - B11 does not have the cadence. He speaks in clipped one-liners ("A fact with a poor record," p.6; "The destination objected," p.19). That is a dry English-butler wit, not a slow Boulder developer saying "damn hard."
  - B11 sometimes explains (p.15: "Three routes. One is private. One is local. One carries pressure."; p.20: "Morrow sells the thing...").
  - He's called "B11" on the page, never Bi11bot.
  - In Issue 3 he's mostly a filing cabinet (pp.41–42, 52).

  Bill would see a well-behaved robot and not recognise himself in it.
- **The public, nonexclusive license (p.34) reads ambiguously.** A libertarian may like owners freely choosing to license. Another reader may see the invention handed to the commons.
- **The ending is a committee.** "Convenor of proposal" (p.49) and a hand-written next review date (p.53). He'd ask where the market is in the water question. Keel still gets 18%, and the fix is a better hearing, not ownership or trade.
- **Issue 3 lectures.** pp.41–43 and 49 have characters stating the civic principle.

**Likely verdict: partly.** The theme is his and the line is respected. The mentor written to be him isn't him yet.

## A comic writer

**The hook** is excellent and arrives on p.2: a birthday, a debt, **28.7 YEARS**. Page 1 is a confident cold open. The writer would read on.

**Page turns.** Page 1 is a right page, so every odd page ends on a turn.
- **Working:** p.3 "I will." turns to p.4 "You missed the date." p.5's key turns to p.6, B11 waking. p.7 "Someone noticed." turns to p.8, Mera. p.15's three routes turn to the p.16 choice. p.45's bulkhead turns to p.46's aftermath.
- **Wasted:** the bookcase opens at the bottom of p.4, a left page, and the workshop appears on p.5 in the same spread, so the reveal is already visible. Pages 37–53 have almost no turn pressure.

**The splash.** p.18 lists the "FULL-PAGE SPLASH" as Panel 4 of a four-panel page. An artist can't draw that as written. It's also the book's only splash. Issues 2 and 3 have no big image: no Halyard reveal on p.19, no Oasis reservoir, no flood at Adrian's station.

**Dialogue.** It's tight, sometimes very good:
- p.11 "Enough to complain."
- p.51 "Paper says sixty-two." / "Pipe says eighteen."
- p.4 "The shift took your wages. You still missed the date."

But everyone speaks in the same short, epigrammatic way, and the "Same X. Different Y." / "Not X. Y." patterns repeat (see the reader section). An editor would mark it as all one voice.

**Continuity.**
- p.32 puts Alex in Keel watching Mara sell a purifier in the middle of the Halyard arc, before the license exists (p.34). The next page has him back in Halyard facing Lock.
- p.37 has Alex and Ada entering the review room before p.38 has them arriving at the gate.

**What they'd cut or merge**
- Four collaborators introduced across two pages (pp.23–25). Lina and Ren carry it; Suri and Jalen are tasks, not people.
- The Mera/Mara name clash.
- Leona's whole relationship with Ada crammed into pp.39–40, then turned straight into procedure.
- The Covenant band rules (pp.41–42, 47), which explain a magic system in the final act.
- Most of the hearing pages.

**Would they take the job?** Yes, on the strength of Issue 1 and the purifier-building sequence (pp.27–31). They'd ask to rebuild Issue 3.

## A game designer

**There is a game here, and a good one.** It fits the ground YAC's Young AmeriTowne already covers.

- **The loop:** take a shift, earn 40, pay Inez, pay down the debt, watch travel status. The core screen is already designed on p.2.
- **Choices that cost something:**
  - Step over the yellow line and lose the day's pay to save a man (p.10).
  - Send the record and lose the hidden exit (p.16).
  - Take Lock's debt clearance or keep the collaborators' names (p.33).
  - Pull the lever or give up control (pp.48–50).
  - Each of these has a stated price on screen. That is rare, and it is the designer's dream.
- **Systems a player would want to push on:**
  - Reported against physical flow, measured with the Measure (pp.14–15, 51). That's an auditing mechanic in the style of Papers, Please.
  - Building the purifier from parts with mismatched edges, then trading output against how easy it is to maintain: 94% that a family can't carry, or 91% they can own (pp.27–31). A crafting and trade-off system.
  - Routes that log "recorded bodies" and miss the unrecorded people (p.29).
  - Attribution and licensing in the registry (pp.24, 34).
  - Morrow's shop as the sell-side economy (p.32).
- **What's thin:** Issue 3's hearing is a dialogue tree, not a system.

**Would they build it?** Yes. They'd prototype a "Keel shift" vertical slice from pp.1–16 and the p.31 purifier configuration first.

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine:** a debt-bound kid climbs a city in three tiers (Keel below, Halyard in the middle, Oasis above) to find out what his vanished father built. It's the vertical-world structure of *Snowpiercer* and *Arcane*. There's a clear season shape: one issue per tier.

**Who an audience follows for a season**
- **Alex:** competent, stubborn, decent.
- **Ada:** the strongest secondary. She has a secret (p.38) and an exit (p.54).
- **B11 and TJ:** merchandise and charm. TJ's head coming off on p.11 is a viral image.
- **Tomas:** the everyman who keeps turning up. "Had work to do" on p.51 is a crowd-pleaser.
- **Lock:** a good antagonist who vanishes after p.35.

**Season-one cliffhanger:** it's there.
- Adrian behind a closing bulkhead with "No evacuation route appears" (p.45) is the hook for season two.
- Tomas's telemetry going dark (p.18), resolved on p.51, is a good mid-season payoff.
- Ada's two routes (p.54) is a quiet, workable finale.

**What's missing**
- **A face on the system.** "Alpha" is never personified. The p.45 node "pulses without a face."
- **A want for Alex beyond "somewhere that isn't here"** (p.3).
- **Romance or any warmth between Alex and Ada.** Not required, but a streamer will ask.
- **Humour past B11's one-liners.**
- **An Issue 3 villain.** Nadi is a clerk with a deadline.
- **Lock's return.**

In Issue 1, yes, there's a series. By Issue 3 the showrunner sees a courtroom episode.

## Does it smell like school

**Pages where a character explains so the reader learns, a caption states the lesson, or a scene exists to define a term:**

1. **p.20.** The Morrow order sheet as a checklist (**PARTS PAID / WORKER PAID / CUSTOMER PAYMENT RECEIVED**), B11 explaining "Morrow sells the thing. The House controls the permission to make it," and a slogan board. A compare-and-contrast exercise.
2. **p.32.** Mara's shop. The page has no plot function: it's out of sequence and nobody in it wants anything. It exists to show a model business, down to the label **SHOP MARGIN — POSTED**. It is dramatised (a customer asks, "If it fails?"), but it's a demonstration page.
3. **p.41.** Mera explains the Water Wars agreement, and a panel prints **EXPIRATION: NONE / INDEPENDENT REVOCATION: NONE**. A sunset-clause lesson.
4. **p.42.** Leona: "I voted for emergency authority. I did not give it an ending. I'll answer for that." Olani: "Nothing here approves an allocation." Accountability, stated.
5. **p.43.** Mera walks Alex through how exclusion builds up, card by card ("First people stopped selling to him... Then people stopped standing near him"). An explanatory tour.
6. **p.49.** "Put the interests on the table. Put the gauges beside the record. Let the affected people answer." / "I can bring it together. I can't own the answer." The book's thesis, spoken aloud.

**Borderline, not counted:**
- p.14's inscription **WHAT IS NOT MEASURED CAN BE MADE INVISIBLE** (a single line).
- p.35's **PROPERTY TRANSFER: NONE**.
- p.50's "It costs you control."
- p.53's procedural "Challenge accepted. Approval paused."

**Count: 6.** Four of them sit together in Issue 3 (pp.41, 42, 43, 49), which is where an unwilling reader feels homework. p.32 comes closest to a whole page built to explain.

**Pages that teach without the school smell, because people need the thing and pay for it:**
- **pp.3–4:** a medicine advance with fixed terms, a missed date, and owning it.
- **p.10:** the cost of doing the right thing, shown in credits.
- **pp.13–16:** paper against pipe, and sending the record costs the exit.
- **pp.21–22:** what a locked asset is ("value with a lock on it"), taught by a villain's temptation.
- **pp.27–31:** engineering trade-offs that end in something a family can own.
- **p.33:** turning down a windfall.
- **p.51:** "Paper says sixty-two. Pipe says eighteen."
- **p.54:** paid for a repair, five to Inez, the balance down.

These are the book at its best.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **Pages 41–43:** cut the civics walk-through. Show Adrian being shut out instead: a shopkeeper refusing him a part, a door closing. Let the reader work out the emergency-authority problem from the action. *Satisfies: the student reader; brings the school count down to three or fewer; Bill ("I've never believed in lecturing").*
2. **A B11 scene (Issue 1 or 2) showing one of his own past failures**, for example a repair he and Adrian botched, seen in Adrian's records. He owns it with a joke at his own expense, then asks Alex what he'd do. *Satisfies: Bill. This is what would make Bi11bot sound like him.*
3. **Page 2, or a page early in Issue 1:** show where the debt came from. Adrian borrowed for parts and bet on his work, from a real lender. That makes it a debt with a source, not a liability the system imposed. And make the time passing before p.54 visible, so the balance adds up. *Satisfies: YAC, as a bank.*
4. **Page 25:** make it plain on the panel that the module Ren takes is his own unrecorded work, reclaimed, not something taken from a business. *Satisfies: YAC and Bill.*
5. **Page 32:** fold Morrow & Sons into the plot. Mara is the first to build the licensed household unit after p.34, and pays the named inventors, so honest business drives the ending instead of sitting beside it. *Satisfies: YAC; Bill (ownership and earned success); removes a school page.*

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: partly; smells like school: yes; school pages: 6
