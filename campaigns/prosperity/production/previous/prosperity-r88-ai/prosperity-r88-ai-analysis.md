# Analysis: *Prosperity*, Issues 01–03 (pages 1–54)

I read the pages only, in order, in the order a student meets them: right page 1, then the turns.

---

## The political read

| Reading | Signal | Pages that carry it |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | **Strong on the surface, weak underneath** | p2 (a gambler's debt passes to his son by law on the son's 17th birthday: "HOUSEHOLD LIABILITY TRANSFER: COMPLETE"); p10 (Alex loses a day's pay for saving a trapped man); p13–16 (the poor district's water goes to "UPLINK INDUSTRIAL"); p20, 22, 24 (a "House" takes workers' inventions: "CREATOR: UNLISTED / CREATOR BONUS: 14 CREDITS"); p37, 52 ("DECLARED INTEREST: ORBITAL INDUSTRIAL BRANCH"). | **Socialist** | **Weak** | p23–28 (five workers build the purifier together); p35 ("LICENSE: PUBLIC / NONEXCLUSIVE"); p12–13 (miners block the tunnel for him). The book never collectivises anything. p53 refuses it outright: "No instant redistribution occurs." p50: "OWNERSHIP RECORDS — UNCHANGED." |
| **Libertarian** | **Strong** | p41 (an emergency authority with "EXPIRATION: NONE / INDEPENDENT REVOCATION: NONE"); p42 (Leona: "I voted for the emergency authority. | **Conservative** | **Some** | p3–4 and p54 (his word kept, his debts paid); the grandmother and household (p4, p54); Ada and her mother (p39–40); hard work with no handout. There are no partisan cues either way. |
| **Capitalist in a bad way** | **Some to strong** | Caldera House and Director Lock (p20–35); the industrial branch's lobbying interest (p37, 52); the stolen-patent kiosk (p20). The House works under a "Charter" and runs the public registry. That makes it a chartered crony, not a firm with competitors, but a 16-year-old will see "a corporation stealing workers' work." |
| **Capitalist in a good way** | **Some to strong** | Inez's pharmacy and her ledger (p3–4, p54: "Good. I need the money."); Morrow & Sons, an owner-run firm with paying customers (p20, 32, 54); costs priced in openly (p31: "Put that in the price"; p32: "They saw the failure list"); a product cut down until a family can maintain it (p29); competition (p32: "Then it can build beside us"); a repair business that is paying off the debt (p54). |

**What the surface invites:** "the system is rigged and corporations steal water from the poor." Issue 01 opens on a debt the state hands to a child and a 28.7-year payoff clock (p2), then fines the hero for doing the decent thing (p10). **What the story supports:** a central allocation authority with an emergency power that never ends, a chartered House that controls the property registry, and an industrial interest that changed the official model. Against them stand honest shopkeepers, written records, measurement and refusing power. That is a story about crony capture and central planning, with small business on the good side.

**Who stops because of the surface:** a Denver parent or a centre-right teacher who flips through Issue 01 alone. They get a dystopia of debt and telemetry and quit before Morrow & Sons (p20) or the refusal (p49). A progressive teacher who reads to the end gets the opposite surprise: no redistribution, debts honoured, ownership left alone. Neither is likely to file a complaint. The first is more likely to put the book down.

**Does the book conclude, or leave a reader concluding, that free enterprise is bad? No.** The pages: p3–4, 20, 29, 31–32, 49–50 and 54. Customers pay, a business serves them, and the hero turns down central power and a debt wipe-out.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like.**
- **p3–4 is their founder's story in small.** The pharmacist fronts the medicine with "Thirty by next shift." Alex misses the date and comes back empty-handed. He says "I'm not asking you to erase it," and she answers "Good. I need the money." On p54 the ledger reads "INEZ QUISPE — MEDICINE ADVANCE: PAID," and five of every twenty credits goes to her. Borrowing, owning a failure and paying back, shown as tools a teenager can use.
- **A business that serves people and makes money doing it:** Morrow & Sons, with "CUSTOMER PAYMENT RECEIVED," the failure list priced in, and "Someone paid before seeing it" / "They saw the failure list" (p32). A purifier cut down until a household can maintain it (p29).
- **Integrity under pressure:** Alex turns down "Debt cleared tonight" twice (p21, p33) and keeps the collaborators' names on the record (p33).
- **Initiative and willingness to fail:** the unapproved repair (p1), stepping over the line (p10), the 11% prototype (p27).
- **Nonpartisan:** no cues either way. A teacher in any school can hand it out.

**What would bother them.**
- **The central debt is something done to you, and it is wrong about credit.** A child inheriting a parent's casino debt by law (p2) is not how accounts or liability work, and they run a real bank. To its credit, nobody powerful cancels it; he refuses that. But the premise could teach a false lesson, and the book never shows the lender, the terms or a way to renegotiate.
- **The 28.7-year clock** (p2) and a balance still over 400,000 at the end (p54) could leave a weaker reader with "effort barely moves it." A stronger reader sees about 16,500 paid in 14 months by a working 17-year-old.
- **A loose end in the accounts:** the departure token is marked "PAYMENT PENDING" (p3) and never shown paid. On p54 it sits beside the ledger with no line of its own. A bank will notice.
- **Saving and investing are absent.** Earning and repaying are on the page; growing anything is not.
- **Issue 01 alone reads as a rigged system.** Many teachers will skim only the first issue.
- **Issue 03 is hard to follow** (p41–42, 47–50). Their students will not get through the band and lever mechanics on a first read.

**Likely verdict: partly.** Nothing crosses their line. Business, ownership and repayment are on the right side of the story, and p3–4 and p54 are the scenes they would show in a classroom. The inherited-debt premise and the first issue's dystopian surface keep it from being a clean yes.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like.**
- **"A windfall isn't a pathway":** Lock's offer is exactly the windfall-for-control deal he hates, and Alex turns it down twice (p21, p33). The ending makes the point on screen: "EXISTING DEBT — UNCHANGED / OWNERSHIP RECORDS — UNCHANGED" (p50).
- **Facing the lender and paying back:** p4 and p54, close to his beer-hall story in spirit.
- **Government and cronies:** an emergency power with no expiration (p41); a declared industrial interest getting public water through the allocation board (p37, 52), which is his "going up to the government, getting money"; a hero who refuses to become the central decider (p48–49). Ada's line on p48, "Don't make two people the answer for everybody," is the most Reynolds sentiment in the book, said by a teenage girl rather than the mentor.
- **De Soto:** the plot turns on getting work written down, recorded and defensible: "Their work stays named. Mine does too" (p33); the Seal's "MISMATCH FOUND" (p34); the House taking property by controlling the registry (p20, 24). He would see his property argument in this.
- **Not a victim:** Alex never complains about the debt. "Twenty-eight point seven" (p2) is all he says.
- **Never "capitalism" as a dirty word:** the word never appears.
- **A mentor who is sometimes wrong:** B11 on p30 ("I counted the record and missed the people. What would you do?") and p46 ("I may have mistaken that for an instruction").
- **The AI doesn't replace people:** people do the decisive work: Tomas with the gauge (p51), the five makers (p27–31), Ada (p9, 48). B11 is a minor presence, which is right by his rule.

**What would bother him.**
- **p35, "LICENSE: PUBLIC / NONEXCLUSIVE."** He would ask: how do these five get paid? Recorded ownership that is then given away is not quite his orchard. (Inference: he would ask the question rather than object.)
- **The dog** is named "TJ" (p11) and the Founder is never confirmed on the page. He might catch the wink; students won't.
- **Little earned success.** Fourteen months of repair slips (p54) is honest but small, and there is no beer-hall moment where the boy borrows to build something of his own.

**Does B11 sound like him? Partly. The behaviour is his; the voice is not.** By the profile's own notes (plain, slow, a bit roundabout, Western; a question first, then a story from his own life, then one plain line; jokes at his own expense; never a lecture):
- **Never a lecture: yes.** B11 never explains a principle.
- **Jokes at his own expense: some.** "Stored. Sleep is more comfortable" (p6); "I expected a longer failure" (p28).
- **Asks instead of tells: once.** "What would you do?" (p30).
- **Story from his own life: never.** B11 tells no story at all.
- **Roundabout, slow, Western: no.** B11 is clipped and epigrammatic, the opposite of roundabout. Nothing in him would sound like Bill read aloud.

The name on the page is "B11," not Bi11bot.

**Likely verdict: yes.** The whole ending is a refusal of the windfall, the bailout and central power, and business is on the right side. He would want B11 to sound more like a person telling a story, but nothing here argues against him.

## A comic writer

**The hook:** **page 2** is a real hook, and it lands on a left page after the turn: "AGE VERIFIED: SEVENTEEN / HOUSEHOLD LIABILITY TRANSFER: COMPLETE… DEBT BALANCE: 418,607," then "Happy birthday, Phantum." It is clean and cruel, and you know the book from it.

**Page turns:** the reveals mostly land on left pages, as they should: B11 waking (p6), Mera in her control room (p8), SEND (p16), the climb (p18), the lever (p48). One problem: **Tomas is revealed alive in p51's first panel, on the right page facing p50**, so the reader sees him while still reading the fallout on p50. **p18 calls panel 4 a "FULL-PAGE SPLASH" on a page that has three other panels.** An artist will send that back.

**Dialogue:** many lines a writer would keep: "Enough to complain" (p11), "Log it under me" (p12), "The bolts say somebody turned" (p13), "The pipe isn't reading your paperwork" (p52), "Had work to do" (p51). "You're taller." / "That's what you have?" (p39) is the best exchange in the book. The problem is that **everyone talks in the same register**: miners, bureaucrats, a mother and a robot all speak in two-beat lines. Over 54 pages the voices blur.

**Names on the page:** Ada's name is never spoken until **p38** ("Ada?"); before that she exists only in panel descriptions. Mera's name appears only on a screen, on p44. The grandmother and the Oasis node never get names. A reader can't learn a name from a panel description.

**Too much is on screens.** A large share of panels are wristband or registry readouts, and the story is told through UI. That is fine in issue one; by issue three it is a wall of capital letters (p41, 47–50).

**What they would cut or merge:** Mera's override thread on p43–44, which repeats p8 and p36; the band mechanics on p41–42 and p47, which they would merge into one readable scene; one of the two escape sequences on p24–26.

**Would they take the job?** Yes, on Issues 01–02. Strong premise, clear three-city structure, a real emotional core in Ada and her mother, and in Adrian (p45–46). They would ask to rebuild Issue 03's machinery first.

## A game designer

**Is there a game? Yes, an unusually good one.** This book is built out of costed choices:
- p10: save Tomas and lose **−40 CR**.
- p16: send the record and lose the hidden route (**"ROUTE EXPOSURE / ACCESS CLOSURE"**).
- p21, 33: sign and the debt is gone, or refuse and keep the names.
- p29: output against serviceability.
- p30: the recorded route against the people who aren't on the record.

**The loop:** work a shift, earn, repair, measure what's reported against what's real, decide, pay down the ledger.

**Systems a player would push on:**
- The debt ledger (can I pay faster? can I renegotiate?).
- The Measure's compare, reported against physical (p15), as a detective mechanic.
- The modular purifier build: membrane, culture, node logic and heat, each one starving another (p27–28).
- Zones and telemetry (p9–10).
- TJ, the magnetic modular dog with a detachable head, which is a toy waiting to be made.

**What the player would demand:** a fail state for signing Lock's paper, and a way to start their own shop like Morrow & Sons. Both are missing, and both would make the game Young AmeriTowne in a dystopia.

**Would they build it?** Yes. It is a natural management sim with a puzzle layer, in the vein of *Papers, Please* or *Frostpunk*, and a better fit for a financial-education partner than most licensed comics.

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine:** a debt-bound kid follows his dead father's work up a three-tier world (Keel, Halyard, Oasis) and exposes who is taking the water. It is a clean season shape, one city per act. Issue 01 alone shows there's a series.

**Characters an audience would follow for a season:**
- **Ada**, the strongest: competent and guarded, with a mother who chose not to come for her (p39–40).
- **Tomas**, a fan favourite from "Enough to complain" to "Had work to do."
- **Alex**, solid but quiet. The audience gets his actions, not his inner life.
- **B11 and TJ**, for merchandise.
- **Mera**, interesting but unclear in motive.

**Season-one cliffhanger:** the real one is p18 (water rising, Tomas's light going dark), and it comes at the end of episode two or three. The finale (p54: Ada takes the departure, Alex stays with the token) is a quiet goodbye, not a cliffhanger. Nobody asks "what next?" except "will Alex leave?"

**What's missing:**
- **A villain with a face.** Lock disappears after p35. Nadi is a functionary. The cold-white system that keeps denying overrides is never explained.
- **Who altered the model** (p52, "ALTERED MODEL CONTRIBUTION: EXPOSED") is never answered.
- A peer world for Alex beyond Ada.
- One scene of Keel living with low water, so the stakes are felt and not only read off a gauge.

## Does it smell like school

**The pages where the book teaches the reader rather than the characters:**
1. **p20.** The Charter kiosk is a demonstration scene: a nameless worker drops a part in, "OWNERSHIP: CALDERA HOUSE / CREATOR: UNLISTED / CREATOR BONUS: 14 CREDITS," then "My correction is in there." It exists to define the House's theft for the reader. (Half a page.)
2. **p42.** Leona's "I voted for the emergency authority. I did not give it an ending. I will answer for that," together with p41's on-screen "EXPIRATION: NONE / INDEPENDENT REVOCATION: NONE." That is a civics lesson on emergency powers that never expire, and it reads like one.
3. **p50.** A page of status notices that sum up the lesson: "EMERGENCY AUTHORITY — RESCINDED / EXISTING DEBT — UNCHANGED / OWNERSHIP RECORDS — UNCHANGED." It is consequence, but it is also the book reading its own moral aloud.

**Borderline, not counted:** p40 ("I will remain where the affected people can answer me" is civics phrasing in a mother–daughter fight); p53 (the panel description says "No instant redistribution occurs" — not lettered, but if the art stages it as a point, it becomes one). **No page exists purely to explain.** Issue 03's problem is less that it smells of school and more that it is opaque: the reader doesn't learn the mechanics, they get lost in them.

**Count: 3.**

**Pages that teach without the smell, because people need the thing and pay for it:**
- **p3–4:** credit, terms, a missed payment, a creditor who needs her money.
- **p10:** the price of a choice, shown in wages.
- **p14–15:** what was reported against what actually flowed.
- **p27–29:** iterating, failing and designing for the buyer who has to maintain it.
- **p31–32:** price in the failures, earn trust, take payment.
- **p51–52:** a worker with a gauge beats a compliance claim.
- **p54:** a ledger going down.

These are the best pages in the book.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **A scene where Alex faces the holder of the 418,607 debt** (Issue 01 or the start of Issue 03). A person, not a screen, sets terms: separate notes, a schedule. It gets credit right, turns the lender into a person, and is Bill's beer-hall story told through a teenager. *Satisfies Young Americans Center (credit shown accurately, debt as something you manage) and Bill Reynolds.*
2. **Rebuild p41–42 and p47–49 into one readable scene** where the offer of central power and Alex's refusal of it are plain at first read. *Satisfies the student reader and Bill Reynolds, whose strongest moment is currently the hardest to follow.*
3. **Put Mara Morrow on the page as a person in Issue 01 or 02**: a customer at her counter, a price, a repair she stands behind. Then honest business is a character before it is a crate label. *Satisfies Young Americans Center, and stops the Issue-01-only reader from leaving with the "corporations are evil" reading.*
4. **Give the theft a face**: a scene showing who altered the model and how the industrial interest got its hands on the allocation board. *Satisfies the showrunner (a villain), and makes plain to both partners that the villain is a crony and not the market.*
5. **A scene where B11 gets something wrong with Adrian, shown and not told** (a route, a bet he didn't stop), which Alex then asks him about. *Satisfies Bill Reynolds: the mentor's own failure, then a question, his order of things.*

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: yes; smells like school: no; school pages: 3
