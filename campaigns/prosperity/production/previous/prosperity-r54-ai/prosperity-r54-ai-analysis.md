# Prosperity, Issues 01–03 (pages 1–54): Analysis

I read the script cold: the pages, `rules/reader.md` and `rules/stakeholders.md`, nothing else. Page numbers refer to the script.

## The political read

| Reading | Signal | Where it shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | **Strong** | Issue 1 is about a rigged system. A 17-year-old inherits his father's debt and his travel is restricted on his birthday (p2). The water records lie, "REPORTED 62% / DELIVERED 18%", and the book calls it "a maintained choice" (p18). Issue 3's answer is "representatives chosen by the communities affected" (p44, p51), and Ada's speech says "The people affected should see what was promised…" (p43). Public release of the design (p34) and a crowd of residents giving testimony (p52) also fit. |
| **Socialist** | **Some** | Halyard's Houses own what workers make: "She made it." / "The House owns it." with a 14-credit "creator bonus" (p20). A team is cut under "efficiency" (p24). | **Capitalist in a bad way** | **Strong** | The three Houses (Aqualis, Helion, Astra) and Director Lock are the corporate face of the problem (p21, p30, p34). Aqualis "copied it" / "scaled it" (p36). Contracts stay put while workers get reassigned as punishment (p30). |
| **Libertarian** | **Some, and it gets stronger late** | The real antagonist is a central authority: Alpha, an emergency power that never handed control back. | **Conservative** | **Some** | Family duty: the grandmother, the father's name, "Mine now" (p54). Personal responsibility: Alex takes his debt rather than having it erased (p53–54). He stays home by choice (p54). Fixes are shown as physical, local work (p1, p15). |
| **Capitalist in a good way** | **Weak** | Nobody in the book serves customers and makes money doing it. The nearest things are Alex's unapproved but working repairs (p2, p15), Ren's "Scrap is allowed to be useful" (p29), and Lina's "They scaled it" (p36). That last one is offered as a fair point but not developed. |

**What the surface invites:** a left-of-centre dystopia. The rich live in the sky, corporations take what workers make, the poor kid's debt is a life sentence, and the cure is community representatives. All of that is in Issues 1 and 2 (p1–36), which is two-thirds of the book.

**What the story supports:** something more mixed and closer to classical-liberal. The villain is a centralised emergency authority, Alpha, plus officials who traded consent for safety (p42, p47). The climax is a refusal of a windfall (p49–51). The ending says outright that the debt, the scarcity and the arguments remain (p53–54). The moral is about transparency and attribution ("a record that can't pretend you weren't here," p36), not about redistribution.

**Who stops reading because of the surface:** a conservative-leaning parent or teacher, or a student from a family that runs a business, who reads Issue 1 and the House pages of Issue 2 (p20–22: "The House owns it," "A clean owner also makes them disappear cleanly") and decides they know where this is going. The libertarian and responsibility material does not start until p47. A reader who stops at p36 never reaches it.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like**
- Alex works for a living and fixes things with his hands (p1, p2: "Just notice it works").
- He loses 40 credits to save a stranger and says "I know" (p14). That is integrity at a visible cost, priced on the page.
- "Medicine first" (p13) is budgeting under scarcity, shown without being explained.
- The debt is never cancelled by someone powerful. He refuses exactly that offer (p49–51).
- The debt is still there at the end, and he claims it: "Mine now" (p54). That fits "take charge" and "responsibility" closely, and it echoes Bill Daniels repaying debts he no longer legally owed.
- The tone is nonpartisan in vocabulary: no parties and no real-world slogans. The word "capitalism" never appears.

**What would bother them**
- **Debt is something done to you.** The book's one financial instrument is a parental liability imposed on a minor by the system (p2). No one ever borrows, saves, earns interest or repays a loan by choice. There is no bank, no lender and no loan officer anywhere in 54 pages.
- **Accuracy.** As a real bank, they will catch the inherited-debt premise (p2) and the unexplained 16,497-credit drop (p53).
- **Business as villain.** The only companies are the Houses, which own workers' output, punish with reassignment and copy the design (p20, p30, p36). No business serves people and profits doing it.
- **"The system is rigged" is literally the plot** (p18). The book does say effort matters (Alex's fixes work, p15, p29), which partly offsets this.
- The community-representatives ending (p44, p51–52) could read as a left-leaning civics lesson in a conservative district. Arguably it's the opposite in a progressive one. Either way it is a politics lesson, not a money lesson.

**Likely verdict: partly.** They can defend Alex's character in front of any school board. They would hesitate over the financial world he lives in, because it teaches teenagers that debt is inherited and inescapable, and that is what a bank teaching financial literacy exists to correct.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like**
- No windfall. On p49–50 the "debt screens falling to zero" are shown and refused. "Nobody promised a clean slate" (p53).
- The villain is centralised control: surveillance, financial restriction, travel restriction, "people stop helping a risk" (p47). The emergency authority is rescinded (p51). This is close to his own words about a government that wants "desperately to control you."
- Alex does not play the victim. He goes from "I can't spend twenty-eight years paying for what he did" (p3) to "Mine now" (p54).
- Records that make people's work defensible: "Put every name where the House record left a blank" (p34), "a record that can't pretend you weren't here" (p36). This is very close to de Soto's dead capital, though the book never calls it ownership.
- People are not replaced by the AI. Tomas, the four contributors and Ada do the essential work. Bi11bot rides in a tool bag.
- Failure shown: the prototype runs at 11% (p28), and the fix comes from scrap (p29).

**What would bother him**
- **Ownership is treated as the problem.** "Mine fails if I call it mine" (p27), "The estate isn't mine to transfer" (p34), "PATENT STATUS: UNFILED" (p33). His line is "Ownership ends a surprising number of arguments." This book says ownership is how people get erased.
- **No lender scene.** His best story is facing the banker ("Paul, I can't pay you"). Alex never faces anyone he owes. The creditor has no face.
- **Business owners are villains by default** (the Houses, Lock).
- **The solution is a committee shares out water** (p52–53), not property rights or exchange. A libertarian will notice that the fix for central planning is more planning, just better witnessed.
- **Lecturing:** Ada's speech (p43), Alex's three principles (p51) and the closing captions (p54).

**Does Bi11bot sound like him? No.** Against the profile's own voice notes:
- *Question first:* Bi11bot asks one question in the whole book, "shall we say hello to your father?" (p9), and it is a stage cue. The real questions come from Mara ("What are you afraid of losing?" p52) and Alex ("And if I'm wrong?" p49).
- *A story from his own life:* none. The closest is the river bet with Adrian (p9), which is about Alex.
- *Jokes at his own expense:* one, "That took longer than expected" (p8). Otherwise his wit is dry and aimed at others: "A versatile word" (p17), "Nearly continuously" (p23).
- *Plain, slow, roundabout, Western:* he is clipped and aphoristic instead: "Keel restricts bodies. Halyard is more ambitious" (p20), "Carrying is not honoring" (p44). Only "Well now" (p29) sounds like Bill.
- *Sometimes wrong:* never. On p11 he explains the tools like a docent, and he answers "Correct" (p11).
- The dog named for a Founder: TJ's name is never spoken or lettered on the page. Readers will not learn the dog has a name, let alone whose.

**Likely verdict: partly.** The plot honours his core beliefs: no windfall, no victimhood, central control as the enemy. But the mentor named for him does not talk like him, never fails, never tells a story, and lives in a book that is suspicious of ownership and business.

## A comic writer

- **Hook:** strong. The p2 checkpoint screen, a debt balance that "dwarfs every other number" followed by "Happy birthday, Phantum." / "I've had better surprises.", is a pitch in one panel.
- **Page turns:** Issue 1 uses the left-hand pages well. The bookshelf swings open on p6, Bi11bot ignites on p8, and Adrian's hologram appears on p10, all landing on the turn. The p18 flood is a good issue-ender. The p36 reflection of a woman with Ada's hair is a good second-issue cliff.
- **Dialogue:** the banter is sharp early ("You look expensive." / "It came with a twenty-eight-year warranty," p3). It goes epigrammatic by Issue 2. Everyone speaks in balanced aphorisms, and the four-line "Mine fails without…" round on p27 is the clearest case. The characters start to sound like one voice.
- **Visible problems:**
  - Director Lock has **no lines in the whole book**. On p21 Alex answers dialogue that isn't there ("You want the estate." / "I'll think about it."), and p30 has the same gap ("Quiet isn't the same as safe" answers nothing). A pro would read this as a cut-and-paste error.
  - Similar names collide: Mera Vale / Mara Doss, Lina Vey / Leona Veyra / Ada Veyra, Ren Okafor / Elias Renn.
  - Alpha appears from p7 ("pre-Alpha pipes") and p12 ("REPORT TO ALPHA") with no explanation until about p41. The water wars get one panel (p42).
  - "The fourth choice is yours" (p51) points at a count of choices that was never set up.
  - The casino chip's equation is never paid off.
- **Splashes:** p1, p19 and p37 are well chosen as one per city, and p54 echoes p1. The p54 captions spell out what the image already shows.
- **What they'd cut:** the one-contributor-per-page fetch run on p23–26 ("Find Suri." "Find Ren."), which could fold into two pages. The procedural hearing on p52–53. The closing captions on p54.
- **Would they take the job?** Yes, for the world, the hook and the visual design. They would ask for a speaking antagonist and a less procedural Issue 3, and they would expect to rewrite much of the Issue 2–3 dialogue.

## A game designer

- **Is there a game?** Clearly. It's a three-tier vertical world with a visible debt clock (p2), a daily wage, and a spend choice between medicine and food (p13).
- **Choices that cost something:**
  - Crossing the yellow line to save Tomas costs 40 credits (p14).
  - TRACE (a quiet exit) versus VERIFY (public, but loses the route) (p18).
  - Sign Lock's tablet or don't (p21–34).
  - Take authority or build a process (p49–51).
  These are textbook, Frostpunk- or Papers-Please-style trade-offs.
- **Loop:** work a shift, earn, spend, investigate with the Measure (scan, then compare the reported figure with what was delivered), recruit contributors, assemble, and choose what to publish.
- **Systems a player would push on:** the water allocation numbers, House ownership claims, a surveillance "repetition" meter (Jalen: "They watch repetitions," p25), the prototype's compatibility (11% to 91%, p28–29), and the debt itself.
- **What's missing for a game:** the debt never moves through the player's own action. Nothing the player can do pays it down, borrows against it or renegotiates it. That is the obvious missing system.
- **Would they want to build it?** Yes. This is the format the book translates into most naturally.

## A streaming showrunner

- **Engine:** three stacked cities, a debt clock, a hidden father and a lying water system. That is a good season-one engine.
- **Characters an audience would follow:** Alex (competent, wry, grounded), Ada (her mother storyline on p38–45 is the best dramatic material in the book), Bi11bot as a charm asset, Tomas (whose sacrifice on p16 is strong), and Mera (a compromised official with a buried crime).
- **Season-one cliffhanger:** either the p18 flood or the p36 reflection. Both work.
- **What's missing:**
  - A face for the antagonist. Lock is silent and Alpha "has no face" (p51).
  - Stakes in Issue 3: the climax is a protocol being registered and a committee drafting an order (p51–53).
  - Personal jeopardy after p18.
  - A reason for season two. The father mystery is closed (p48) and the system has been reformed (p51).
- **Would they buy it?** They'd option it on Issue 1 and rebuild Issues 2–3.

## Does it smell like school

**Yes.** These are the pages where someone explains for the reader's benefit, a caption states the lesson, or a scene exists to define a term:

1. **p11:** Bi11bot explains the three tools ("They make it harder to hide who saw it, who made it, and who owes a response").
2. **p22:** the five water components are listed in turn.
3. **p23:** Lina: "One problem was hidden accounting. This one is physical contamination."
4. **p27:** "Mine fails without…" ×4, then "mine fails if I call it mine."
5. **p42:** caption: "The Covenant failed when fear made promises smaller than survival."
6. **p43:** Ada's three-sentence principle of accountability.
7. **p49:** a scene that defines "convenor" ("Convenor of what?").
8. **p51:** Alex points at each object in turn and states a civic principle for it.
9. **p52:** a negotiation-technique moment ("Not what do you want. What are you afraid of losing?").
10. **p54:** the captions state the moral, ending on a line labelled "FINAL HUMAN QUESTION". If that is lettered, it reads as a discussion prompt.

On top of these, there are aphorisms that close scenes like a moral: p24 ("don't confuse relief with ownership"), p36 ("It's a record that can't pretend you weren't here") and p53 ("Nobody promised a clean slate").

**Count: 10 pages that smell of school, plus 3 aphorism pages.** For readers who are already wary of anything that looks AI-made, the evenly balanced, epigrammatic lines (p27, p32, p36) will also smell machine-written.

**Pages that teach without smelling of it**, because people need the thing and pay for it:
- **p2:** the debt screen. The numbers alone teach the cost.
- **p13:** "Medicine first," a few credits pushed across the table.
- **p14:** −40 CR to save Tomas.
- **p18:** measuring 62% against 18% with your own hands, then giving up the quiet route to publish it.
- **p28–29:** the prototype failing at 11%, and the scrap sleeve.
- **p33:** a screen listing everything the unit still requires: "useful, not miraculous."
- **p53:** the debt is still on the screen.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **A lender scene in Issue 3 (around p53–54).** Alex goes to a named person at the Keel credit office, says he can't pay on these terms, and comes away with the note split into pieces with a real schedule. This makes the drop from 418,607 to 402,110 a payment he made. It would satisfy **Bill Reynolds** (it is his beer-hall story) and **Young Americans Center** (debt as a tool you manage, with accurate mechanics).
2. **A business that serves people and profits (Issue 2, around p32–36 or p54).** For example, Ren or a Keel workshop selling and maintaining the compact units, and making money doing it. Aqualis's scaled version could then be both a copy and a service. This satisfies **Young Americans Center** and **Bill**, and it gives the "capitalist in a good way" reading something to stand on.
3. **Make Bi11bot wrong once, on the page, and give him a story of his own.** For example, he calls the p17 route wrong, and he tells one failure of his own or Adrian's in place of the p11 explanation. This would satisfy **Bill** (his voice notes, and a mentor who is sometimes wrong) and the **students** (it removes the worst explaining page).
4. **Make ownership concrete at the climax (p51–53).** Keel workers' repairs (p2, p15) become registered, defensible claims through the Seal, instead of the design going unfiled. This satisfies **Bill** (de Soto) and moves the ending from committee to property, which also helps **Young Americans Center**'s nonpartisan footing.
5. **Give the antagonist a face and a voice in Issue 2 (p21, p30).** Lock speaks and makes his case in person, and the p51–52 civics demonstration shrinks to make room for that conflict. This satisfies the **comic writer** and the **showrunner**, and it cuts two of the pages that smell most of school.

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: partly; smells like school: yes
