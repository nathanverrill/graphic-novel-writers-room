# Prosperity: Analysis (Issues 01–03, pages 1–54)

I read the pages only, the way each person below would meet them.

---

## The political read

| Reading | Signal | Pages that carry it |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | **Strong (on the surface)** | p2: a 17-year-old inherits 418,607 credits of household liability on his birthday. p14–15: water is quietly diverted from the poor residential line to industry ("REPORTED TO KEEL: 62% — DELIVERED TO KEEL: 18%"), which reads like Flint. p20: a young worker's invention becomes Vector House property for a "CREATOR BONUS: 14 CREDITS." p26, p29: a corporate director threatens the workers who helped. p52–53: a community challenge to a "compliant" allocation. |
| **Libertarian** | **Some to strong (underneath)** | p41: emergency authority with "no expiration. No independent revocation. | **Conservative** | **Some** | Keeping your word on a debt (p3, p9, p54). Duty to family (Grandmother, p4). Owning your choices: Leona says "Both choices were mine" (p42). | **Socialist** | **Some** | p27, p31: production by a collective, with five contributors around one machine. p33: "PATENT STATUS: UNFILED," "No exclusive owner." p54: "ALLOCATION REVIEW: COMMUNITY-PENDING." Water is handed out by a public table, never priced. |
| **Capitalist, bad way** | **Strong** | Caldera, Vector and Orbital Houses (p19–36). Cassian Lock's offer: debt cleared if Alex signs over the estate, and the collaborators named if he refuses (p21–22, p29). The kiosk (p20). "HOUSE PRODUCTION AUTHORIZED / CONTRIBUTOR ACCESS REVIEW PENDING" (p36). |
| **Capitalist, good way** | **Weak to some** | Ada's honest loan: "Fixed installments. Same amount each time. No surprise fees" (p3). Morrow & Sons, which posts its price, wage and margin (p20, p54). Alex in paid service work (p54). These add up to about three panels of positive enterprise against seventeen pages of the Houses. |

**What the surface invites:** an anti-corporate dystopia, where the rich up the mountain steal water from the poor below and big companies own your work. A teenager flipping through Issue 02 will read it that way.

**What the story supports:** a more libertarian and personal-responsibility book. The Houses are creatures of Alpha, a state system that picked them. The real villain is emergency power nobody took back (p41) and a record trusted over the physical world (p52: "Paper says sixty-two. / Pipe says eighteen."). The hero refuses a buyout (p34) and refuses absolute power (p49), and he keeps paying (p54). No debt is forgiven.

**Who stops reading because of the surface:** a business-owning parent or a centre-right teacher who skims pp. 20–29. They see a corporation buying a dead man's patents, threatening workers, and a hero who answers "No exclusive owner" (p33). The line that tells them the Houses were state-picked (p20) is hedged ("That is what the record says"), so they don't catch it. A Reynolds-minded reader is most at risk of quitting in Issue 02 and never reaching the payoffs on pp. 41–51. At the other end, a progressive student may finish annoyed that the hero calmly keeps paying an imposed debt (p54). That reader keeps going but argues with it, which is not a complaint.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like**
- **Ada's loan is a clean, accurate model of personal credit.** Medicine first, terms stated, "Fixed installments. Same amount each time. No surprise fees" (p3).
- **Alex owns a missed payment.** He misses the first installment and tells her before she asks ("Then tell me before I have to ask." / "I'm telling you." p9).
- **The book ends on repayment, not rescue.** Fourteen months later he is still paying: "LOAN BALANCE REDUCED," "Same amount next week" (p54). That is exactly the Daniels story: keeping your word after it gets hard.
- **No one cancels the debt.** It goes from 418,607 to 402,110 (p2 → p54), and the numbers add up: 40 credits a day for about 14 months. Alex turns down the buyout that would have cleared it (p21–22, p34). He also turns down the executive power that would have erased it (p48–49). The system leaves debts "UNCHANGED PENDING HUMAN PROCESS" (p51). This is the opposite of "someone powerful cancels it," and YAC will notice.
- **He takes initiative.** The improvised repair on p1: "Then don't approve it. … Just notice it works."
- **Integrity costs him something.** He gives up a day's wage to save Tomas (p10), and his refusal "has a price" (p49).
- **Morrow & Sons is a business that serves people and makes money.** It posts price, wage and margin (p20, p54).

**What would bother them**
- The p2 debt mechanics. A minor automatically takes on a parent's liability, and a teenager can't reliably tell a dystopia rule from how credit works. They are a real bank, and this is the item they are most likely to raise.
- The only line about banks is a dig (p3).
- Issue 02 casts large firms as villains for seventeen pages. Morrow & Sons gets two panels, and the "FAIR PRICE" label (p20) reads like a moral stamp, not a price.
- The climax is a civic hearing, not an enterprise. Pages 47–53 are governance. The free-enterprise moments are crowded into p54, panel 1.
- After "No exclusive owner" (p33) the book never shows who gets paid for the purifier. A YouthBiz coach will ask what the business model is.
- Partisan cues are roughly balanced: the water-justice signal leans left, the emergency-power signal leans right. That suits "carefully nonpartisan" better than most books manage.

**Likely verdict: partly.** They would be glad to hand Ada's loan and p54 to a classroom. They would want the p2 inheritance mechanics and the p3 bank line handled before it reaches a Denver public school. They would wish the enterprise were the story rather than the epilogue.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like**
- **No windfall solves the plot.** Lock's deal is a textbook bailout: "Your debt clears tonight" (p21). Alex refuses it twice (p22: "That's what he's selling"; p34: "No."). He also refuses unlimited power (p49), and he is still paying at the end. That is "There is No Free Lunch" in plot form.
- **The beer-hall story is here in miniature.** He misses a payment, faces the lender, and keeps paying (p9, p54).
- **The mentor is wrong, admits it, and then asks.** B11 blames the membrane chemistry and is wrong (p28). His risk table counts only the record (p30). He then says "I counted the record and missed the people. That was my error. What would you do?" (p30). That is very close to "How do you see it? What are we missing?"
- **Technology cannot replace presence.** Alpha's whole failure is trusting the record over people and pipes. Pages 47 and 51 require humans in the process ("HUMAN ROLE REQUIRED"). B11 never replaces Ada, Tomas or the collaborators.
- **Government overreach and cronyism are the deeper villains.** The emergency power nobody took back (p41), the debanking (p44), and Houses "selected" by Alpha (p20). The word "capitalism" never appears.
- **The failure is quick.** 11% to 94% to a modest 91% household unit that still needs maintenance (p27–33).

**What would bother him**
- **Business owners look like villains by default** unless you catch the half-line on p20. Lock is the face of business in this book.
- **De Soto runs the wrong way.** Adrian's 312 patents are literally dead capital: "Rights under dispute are not a wallet" (p21). The book leaves them dead. "No exclusive owner" (p33) is celebrated, and the attribution record (p35) records credit, not property anyone can use. "Ownership ends a surprising number of arguments" is the moral he'd want; here it is a committee that ends the argument (p50–53).
- **Alex never fails big.** He is right almost every time: the repair, the bolts, the connectors, the refusals. His one failure is a missed installment. Reynolds' best project was a failure that taught him the most, and Alex never gets one.
- **The dog** is called TJ, and the page never says it is for a Founder (p11). He might enjoy the nod if he guesses it.
- **There is one lecture line**, B11's "One business earns customers. The Houses were selected for scale." (p20)

**Does Bi11bot sound like him? No.** The profile asks for plain, slow, slightly roundabout Western speech: a question first, then a story from his own life, then one plain line, with jokes at his own expense. B11 speaks in short, dry, precise machine lines: "Correct." (p16, twice); "A versatile word." (p13); "Trace shows where. Not why." (p15); "That is generally what connectors are for." (p27). He tells no stories. He never mentions a failure of his own until p30, and even that is a correction, not a story. There is no "damn hard." The closest he comes to self-deprecating humour is "I expected a longer failure" (p28). He asks a real question exactly once (p30). Also, the book calls him **B11**, never Bi11bot. Structurally he is right: he doesn't hand out answers, he is sometimes wrong, he steps back. The voice is not Reynolds'.

**Likely verdict: partly. Close to yes on substance, short on voice and on business.** He would not finish feeling the book argues against him. Its plot is more aligned with him than its surface. But he would not hear himself in his own robot, and he would wince at Issue 02.

## A comic writer

**The hook:** strong. Page 1 delivers a competent kid, an unapproved fix and a dare of a line. Page 2 gives you a birthday that comes with "PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS" plus "My debt face." A pro keeps reading past page ten.

**Page turns:** mixed. The B11 reveal falls correctly on a left page after a turn (p6). But pp. 4–5 are a spread, so the bookshelf's "CLICK—RUMBLE" and the open workshop are seen together: the reveal is spoiled. Issue endings on p18 and p36 are well placed.

**A cliffhanger that undoes itself.** Page 18 ends on "TOMAS SITE 6 — SIGNAL LOST … The question is not answered." But p17, panel 2 has already shown "Tomas … alive and limping." A writer would circle this first.

**Clarity problems a pro would flag:**
- The p17–18 valve business is unclear. Is Mera rescuing them or drowning them? The script works hard to say the residential regulator is "elsewhere on the diagram," and no reader will see that.
- Page 54 calls panel 4 a "FULL-PAGE FINAL IMAGE," which is impossible on a four-panel page. It would need its own page, and the count is fixed at 54.

**Dialogue:** the first issue is sharp ("You look expensive." / "It came with a twenty-eight-year warranty."). By Issue 02, everyone (Lock, Lina, Ren, Mera, Leona, Ada) speaks in the same aphoristic register, and the four collaborators are one technical line each (pp. 23–26). Pages 47–51 are mostly screen text.

**Splash pages:** p1 earns it. p37 is a good arrival at Oasis, but it is cluttered with two insets.

**What they would cut or compress:**
- The fetch-quest rhythm of Issue 02 (one collaborator per page, pp. 23–26).
- The procedural screens on pp. 47–51.
- The quest for three objects (Measure, Seal, Covenant bands), which reads like a game design document.

**Would they take the job?** Yes, on Issue 01 and the p38–40 mother-daughter scene, which is the best writing in the book. They would ask to rebuild the back half of Issue 03 so the climax is something a person *does*, not a protocol that registers.

## A game designer

**There is a game here, and a good one.**
- **The loop:** shift work for 40 credits, a weekly fixed installment to Ada, choices that dock your pay, and checking the record against the physical gauge. It plays like *Papers, Please* crossed with *Frostpunk*, with a ledger always on screen (p2, p9, p54).
- **Choices that cost something, on the page:**
  - Step across the line and lose the wage (p10).
  - The one-use quiet exit versus the public broadcast (p16).
  - Sign the estate away (debt cleared, collaborators safe) or refuse (p22, p29).
  - Take executive power or keep your workshop and clearance (p48–49).

  The book says it outright: "Then the refusal has a price."
- **Systems players would push on:**
  - TJ's detachable body as a scouting and puzzle tool (p11, p13).
  - Connector-fitting by touch (p28).
  - Juggling the purifier's limits: area, rest cycles, heat recovery (p31–32).
  - Reputation with each House.
  - Whether to repay early or late, and what Ada does if you miss.
- **What's thin:** there is no economy beyond one wage and one loan. Morrow & Sons, a business the player could run, is the obvious missing mode. It also fits Young AmeriTowne neatly.

**Would they want to build it?** Yes. The record-versus-reality mechanic and the debt clock are a working core loop already.

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine:** a vertical class world in three tiers (Keel, Halyard, Oasis), a missing-father mystery, a debt clock and a water conspiracy. It is easy to pitch: *Snowpiercer* stood on end, with a debt instead of a train.

**Characters an audience follows for a season:**
- Alex. Competent, wry, not a chosen one.
- Ada. Her history with Leona carries real emotional weight (pp. 38–40).
- B11 and TJ. TJ, the dog that comes apart, sells merchandise.
- Mera. The most interesting adult: complicit, sorry, still controlling.
- Lock. A reasonable-sounding antagonist, which is good, but he has no life of his own.

**Season-one cliffhanger:** the flooding conduit (p18), or better, Adrian's fate. "The sequence cuts before Adrian's evacuation or death is shown" (p45), plus Grandmother's "He's closer than you think" (p4). That is a season-two engine, if the show keeps Grandmother, whom the book drops after p5.

**What's missing:**
- An antagonist with a face and wants. Alpha is abstract, and Lock leaves the board after p34.
- A climax that plays as action. Pages 47–53 are a hearing, and a showrunner would make it the mid-season turn, not the finale.
- Stakes for the four collaborators. Their fates are "ACCESS REVIEW PENDING" (p36), and we never see them lose anything.

**Verdict:** they would option it on Issue 01 and the Ada/Leona thread, then rebuild the finale.

## Does it smell like school

**Pages where something is explained so the reader can learn it, or a line states the lesson. Count: 10.**
1. **p3.** "Fixed installments. Same amount each time. No surprise fees." It is in character, but it reads like a credit-union brochure.
2. **p20.** "FAIR PRICE / SERVICE INCLUDED" as a label, plus B11's "One business earns customers. The Houses were selected for scale." This is the textbook contrast stated outright.
3. **p26.** "Waste heat is still material. Vent it and you lose the system." A science-class line.
4. **p31.** "If a household can't clean it, it doesn't own it." The ownership moral, spoken outright.
5. **p42.** "I chose emergency authority. I chose distance from my daughter. Both choices were mine." A responsibility statement delivered *into a recording terminal*, almost to camera.
6. **p47.** "HUMAN ROLE REQUIRED: CONVENOR / AUTHENTICATOR," then "I convene it. I don't own it." A term being defined.
7. **p50.** "Physical flow. / Maintenance capacity. / Reserve continuity." and "DISTRIBUTED VERIFICATION PROTOCOL." Civics vocabulary.
8. **p51.** "RESCINDED WITHIN DEFINED SCOPE" / "UNCHANGED PENDING HUMAN PROCESS." The process explained in captions.
9. **p53.** "It's slow." / "It's visible." The theme stated as a closing couplet.
10. **p54, panel 1.** "A worker records cost, wage and margin." Unit-economics shown as a demonstration.

The school smell is concentrated in pp. 47–53. A teen reading the night before will hit this stretch last, and it is the part they will remember.

**Pages that teach without the smell, because people need the thing and pay for it:**
- **p1.** The working fix versus the approved one.
- **p2.** The debt clock does the arithmetic for you.
- **p9.** A missed installment, and the honesty that costs face.
- **p10.** −40 credits for saving a man.
- **pp. 14–15.** A hand on a cold pipe and a hand on a full one.
- **p22.** "That's what he's selling."
- **p28.** "The parts aren't wrong. The edges are."
- **p49.** "Then the refusal has a price."
- **p52.** "Paper says sixty-two. / Pipe says eighteen." The best teaching line in the book.
- **p54, panels 2–3.** The balance going down slowly, week after week.

The first issue mostly teaches by cost. The third mostly teaches by caption.

**Verdict: yes.** It smells of school in the back third. Issue 01 alone would be a no.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **p20 (YAC, Reynolds).** Turn B11's explaining line into a short scene where the reader *sees* Alpha awarding a House its charter or its water. That way the Houses read as state-picked cronies, not as what markets produce, before the skimming parent quits.
2. **pp. 32–33 or p54 (Reynolds, YAC).** Give Alex a real failure he owns. One possibility: the first Morrow & Sons units fail in Keel homes, and he has to face the customers and Ada about the parts he bought on credit. Then he pays it down. Right now his only failure is one missed installment.
3. **p54 (Reynolds, YAC).** Show the recorded attribution *earning*: the five contributors receive a share from each Morrow & Sons sale. That turns "No exclusive owner" (p33) into written-down, defensible property that becomes capital, and it answers the YouthBiz question of who gets paid.
4. **p30 or p46 (Reynolds).** Give B11 one scene in the profile's voice: a question, then a remembered failure of his own with Adrian shown in flashback panels, then one plain line, with the joke at his own expense. That would make the robot sound like the man who paid for the book.
5. **pp. 2 and 17–18 (YAC, the teen reader).** On p2, make clear on the page that the inherited liability is a rule of Keel imposed by Alpha, not how a loan works, so a teenager can tell it apart from Ada's honest loan. On p17, take Tomas out of panel 2 so the p18 cliffhanger holds, and make Mera's valve read as either rescue or threat at first glance.

---

## In short

Both partners are satisfied in part. YAC would gladly put Ada's loan and the repayment on p54 in front of a class. They would want the inherited debt on p2 and the bank line on p3 handled first, and they'd wish the business were the story, not the epilogue. Reynolds would get most of what he believes from the plot: no bailout, a mentor who is wrong and then asks, a debt still being paid. He would not hear himself in B11, and Issue 02 makes business look like the villain. The first issue teaches through what things cost; the last seven pages teach through captions.

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: partly; smells like school: yes
