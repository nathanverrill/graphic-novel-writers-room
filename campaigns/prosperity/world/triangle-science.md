# Science of the Lithium Triangle

Uses the same plausibility labels as the companion skills.

Baseline is September 2026. Written from general knowledge. Check any number that a story leans on.

**"Lithium triangle" is only the name of the region here.** This skill is not about lithium. Treat lithium as one activity among many (copper, silver, tin, gold, borates, herding, farming, tourism, astronomy, trade, cities) and bring it in only when the writer's story does.

**This is a setting kit, not a plot.** It describes an environment, its constraints, and categories of things that can happen there. Do not steer toward any particular storyline, theme, protagonist, or political reading, and do not assume the writer wants conflict, villains, or a message. Serve whatever story the writer brings: a romance, a comedy, a procedural, a family saga, a thriller, a children's book. Offer options across the range rather than a favorite, and use only the parts that the writer's story touches.

## Plausibility labels

Everything here is labelled with the vocabulary defined in `rules/hard-sf-rules.md`: **T** truth,
**EG** educated guess, **S** speculation, **L** license, and **Cut** for what does not fit. The
rules for a license, the license log and the Thorne and Tyson tests are in that file; read it
before inventing anything the story then has to live with.

Here the "science" is the published science of the region and the laws of physics, chemistry, and biology.
Baseline is September 2026. Exchange rates, inflation, and subsidies here change by the month. **Never hardcode a price or rate in a story without searching first**, and for near future stories prefer ratios ("a month's rent," "three shifts' pay," "a llama") over numbers.

## How to use

Pull only what a scene needs. One correct physical detail does more than a paragraph of exposition. The section "Science that makes scenes" at the end is the fast path.

## Geology: why this place exists

- **Subduction built it.** The Nazca plate dives under South America. That raises the Andes, feeds a chain of volcanoes along the Chile and Bolivia and Argentina border, and causes great earthquakes on the coast (T).
- **The Altiplano and Puna** form the second largest high plateau on Earth after Tibet, mostly 3,500 to 4,500 m. Under it sits one of the largest known bodies of partially molten rock in the continental crust (T).
- **Closed basins.** Water that falls here cannot reach the sea. It flows to the lowest point of each basin and evaporates. Over hundreds of thousands of years that leaves salt flats (salares) (T).
- **Why the minerals.** Magma rising above the subduction zone carries metals and deposits them as it cools: copper in giant porphyry systems on the Chilean side, tin and silver in veins on the Bolivian side. Volcanic rocks and hot springs also release lithium, boron, and potassium, which closed basins collect and extreme evaporation concentrates. High sun, low rain, young volcanism, and no outlet to the sea: few places on Earth combine all four (T).
- **Ancient lakes.** Uyuni and Coipasa are the floors of vanished giant lakes from wetter periods tens of thousands of years ago. Old shorelines and fossil algae reefs ring the hills (T).
- **Scale.** Chile is the world's largest copper producer. Cerro Rico at Potosí was the richest silver mountain in history and bankrolled the Spanish empire. The nitrate desert once supplied most of the world's fertilizer. Mining here is five centuries deep, and lithium is the newest and smallest layer of it (T).

## Salars: how they behave

- **Uyuni** is the largest salt flat on Earth, roughly 10,000 square km at about 3,650 m, and so flat that satellites have used it to calibrate altimeters (T).
- **Salar de Atacama** sits lower, about 2,300 m, with a rough, jagged halite core and lagoons at the edges. Flamingo lagoons, oasis villages, and tourism sit around its rim, with mining in its core (T).
- **Surface.** Hexagonal ridges a meter or two across form on the crust; current research links them to slow convection of brine below (T to EG). The crust can be decimeters to meters thick over mud and brine. Holes called "ojos" open to the brine below. Heavy vehicles can break through away from known tracks (T).
- **Wet season mirror.** A few cm of summer rainwater turns Uyuni into a perfect reflector. It also makes it impassable in places and strands vehicles (T).
- **Islands** of old volcanic rock and fossil reef rise from Uyuni, covered with giant columnar cacti that grow about a cm a year (T).

## Extraction science: how things come out of the ground

**Copper (the region's biggest industry by far)**
- Ore is a fraction of a percent copper, so mines move mountains: giant open pits, and block caves underground where the ore body is undercut and collapses under its own weight (T).
- Sulfide ore is crushed, ground to powder in water, and floated with reagents so copper minerals stick to bubbles. The concentrate goes to a smelter. Oxide ore is heaped on lined pads, sprinkled with sulfuric acid, and the copper is plated out electrically as pure sheets called cathodes (T).
- Byproducts and problems: molybdenum, rhenium, gold, silver, selenium, and tellurium come out of the same stream. So does arsenic, which makes smelter dust and gases dangerous. Tailings, the ground rock left over, are stored wet behind dams or increasingly filtered and stacked dry (T).
- Copper uses far more water than any other activity except farming, which is why the big Chilean mines moved to piped seawater (T).

**Tin, silver, zinc, lead, antimony, gold (Bolivia's belt, the Argentine Puna)**
- Narrow rich veins in old volcanic and intrusive rock, worked underground for 500 years. Cerro Rico is so tunneled that its summit is caving in (T).
- Sulfide minerals exposed to air and water make sulfuric acid. Acid drainage carries iron, zinc, cadmium, lead, and arsenic into rivers, staining them orange and lasting centuries after mining stops (T).
- Small scale gold mining uses mercury, which ends up in rivers, fish, and people (T).
- Rare and strategic metals such as indium, gallium, and germanium occur as traces inside zinc and tin ores and are only recovered at refineries (T).

**Salts: nitrates, iodine, borates, table salt, potash**
- The Atacama's nitrate and iodine crusts exist only because rain almost never washes them away. They fed the world's fertilizer and explosives before synthetic ammonia, and left ghost towns across the desert (T).
- Borates are dug from the edges of salt flats. Salt itself is cut by hand into blocks (T).

**Lithium brine (one industry among these)**
- Salty groundwater under salt flats is pumped and either evaporated in chains of colored ponds for a year or more, or passed through a chemical plant that pulls the lithium out directly and reinjects the rest. The product is a white powder shipped in one tonne bags. Magnesium in the brine makes separation hard, more so at Uyuni than at Atacama. See the lithium skill for detail (T).

**Common to all of it**
- Dust, acid, reagents, tailings, and water use are the real hazards. None of it glows (T).
- Closure is the unsolved science: keeping tailings stable and drainage treated for centuries in earthquake country (T for the problem, EG that most sites will be under funded).

## Hydrology: the slow part

- **Recharge is tiny.** Rain falls mostly on the high eastern ranges in summer, soaks in, and moves toward the salars over decades to millennia. Some groundwater being pumped today fell as rain thousands of years ago and is effectively not renewable on human timescales (T).
- **Fresh water floats on brine.** At the salar edge, lighter fresh groundwater meets dense brine in a sloping, shifting boundary. Pump either side and the boundary moves. Lagoons and wetlands sit exactly on that margin (T).
- **Vegas and bofedales** are high altitude peat wetlands fed by springs. They are the only green in the landscape, the basis of herding, and a carbon store. Once dried they can take decades to recover, if ever (T).
- **Lag.** Effects of pumping can appear years later and km away, and natural drought looks similar. Proving cause is slow and honestly difficult, even for sincere scientists (T).
- **Subsidence.** Satellite radar has measured the Salar de Atacama surface sinking by a cm or two per year where pumping is concentrated (T).
- **Evaporation** potential runs to meters per year, against rainfall of a few cm or less (T).

## Hard numbers that constrain stories

- **Lifting water is expensive.** Raising one cubic meter of water 3,000 m takes about 8 kWh at perfect efficiency, more like 10 to 12 in practice, on top of roughly 3 to 4 kWh to desalinate it. Piping seawater to a high mine is real but only for operations that can pay (T).
- **Oxygen.** At 4,000 m each breath has about 60 percent of the oxygen available at sea level. At 5,000 m, about half (T).
- **Boiling point.** Water boils at about 87 C at 4,000 m. Beans, rice, and potatoes take far longer. Pressure cookers are in every kitchen (T).
- **Engines** lose roughly a third of their power at 4,000 m unless turbocharged. Helicopters and drones lose lift (T).
- **Sun.** The Atacama has the highest measured solar irradiance on Earth's surface, and record UV index readings above 20 have been reported on the altiplano. Unprotected skin burns in minutes (T).
- **Temperature.** Daily swings of 30 to 40 C are normal on the plateau. Summer afternoons can be warm; winter nights fall to minus 15 or minus 20 C (T).

## Climate and weather

- **Why it is dry.** The Andes block Amazon moisture from the east. The cold Humboldt current and a permanent high pressure zone suppress rain from the west. Parts of the Atacama core have gone years without measurable rain (T).
- **Altiplano summer** (called the Bolivian or altiplanic winter in Chile) brings thunderstorms from roughly December to March: lightning, hail, flash floods down dry canyons, washed out roads, flooded salars (T).
- **Coastal fog** (camanchaca) feeds lichen and cactus on the coastal hills and can be harvested with mesh fog catchers (T).
- **El Niño and La Niña** shift where and how much it rains, sometimes catastrophically: the 2015 Atacama floods killed dozens in a desert (T).
- **Wind** rises most afternoons on the plateau. Dust devils, blowing salt, and a wind chill that makes sunny days dangerous (T).
- **Trend.** Warming is amplified at altitude. Most projections show the altiplano getting drier and rain more erratic; tropical glaciers are shrinking fast (Bolivia's Chacaltaya glacier vanished around 2009). Ice rich rock glaciers in the arid Andes are an under studied water store (T for observed, EG for continued drying).

## Altitude and the body

- **Acute mountain sickness** within hours of arrival above about 2,500 m: headache, nausea, poor sleep, breathlessness. Usually passes in days. Rarely progresses to fluid in the lungs or brain swelling, which kill without descent and oxygen (T).
- **Sleep** at altitude is broken by periodic breathing. Oxygen enriched dormitories help (T).
- **Shift workers** who commute between sea level and 4,000 m every week or two never fully acclimatize. Chile regulates this kind of intermittent exposure with medical checks (T).
- **Chronic mountain sickness** affects some long term residents: too many red cells, thick blood, headaches, blue lips (T).
- **Andean peoples** show inherited adaptations after some 10,000 years at altitude, different from the Tibetan pattern: larger chests, more hemoglobin (T).
- **Coca** leaf chewed or as tea is a mild stimulant that eases hunger, fatigue, and altitude symptoms. It is not cocaine in effect (T).
- **Also:** dry air cracks lips and causes nosebleeds, wounds heal slowly, alcohol hits harder, cold injuries come fast after sunset, and cognition dips for newcomers, which matters for anyone making decisions on day one (T).

## Water chemistry people live with

- **Natural arsenic** from volcanic rock is in many rivers and wells. Antofagasta's population was poisoned by its water supply from the late 1950s until treatment began around 1970, with cancers appearing for decades after. Some Atacameño and Puna communities carry a gene variant that processes arsenic more efficiently, one of the clearest examples of recent human evolution (T).
- **Boron, lithium, and salts** are naturally high in much of the region's drinking water. Some Puna villages drink water with lithium levels far above typical; health effects are an active research question (T for levels, S for effects).
- **Mining adds** acid drainage, heavy metals, and sediment to rivers in the old tin and silver belt. The Pilcomayo carries centuries of it downstream (T).
- Result: whether a given contaminant is natural or industrial is a genuine scientific question in many disputes (T).

## Life

- **Flamingos.** Three species (Andean, James's, Chilean) feed on microscopic algae and tiny crustaceans in salty lagoons and nest in a few sites. They move between lagoons across all three countries, so counts at one site mislead. Sensitive to water level and disturbance (T).
- **Camelids.** Wild vicuña (fine wool, protected, communally sheared in round ups called chaccu) and guanaco; domestic llama and alpaca (T).
- **Others.** Viscacha (a rabbit like rodent sunning on rocks), culpeo fox, puma, the rare Andean cat, rhea (suri), condor, giant coot, Andean goose (T).
- **Plants.** Bunch grass (ichu or paja brava), resinous tola shrubs, the queñua tree which forms some of the highest woodlands on Earth, and yareta, a bright green cushion plant as hard as wood that grows about a cm a year, lives for millennia, and was burned by the ton as fuel for mines and railways. Quinoa tolerates salt, frost, and drought (T).
- **Microbes.** Lagoons host living microbial mats and stromatolites, close analogs of Earth's earliest ecosystems. Organisms here tolerate UV, salt, arsenic, and desiccation at once. They interest astrobiologists and biotechnology companies (T). Commercial use raises benefit sharing questions with the communities whose land they come from (EG).
- **The dry limit.** The hyperarid Atacama core is among the few places where soil comes close to sterile. NASA and others test Mars rovers and life detection instruments there because the soils resemble Mars in dryness and chemistry (T).

## Volcanoes, heat, and earthquakes

- **Active volcanoes** include Láscar (frequent small eruptions, ash on nearby villages), Ollagüe (constant fumarole), and many dormant cones above 5,500 m. Ojos del Salado on the Chile and Argentina border is the highest volcano on Earth (T).
- **Uturuncu** in southwest Bolivia has not erupted in a very long time but the ground around it has been rising for decades as magma or fluids move below. Scientists monitor it; an eruption in any story's timeframe is not expected (T for uplift, Cut for an imminent super eruption).
- **Geothermal.** El Tatio at 4,300 m is one of the world's highest geyser fields, best at dawn when steam columns stand in the cold. Bolivia's Sol de Mañana has boiling mud. Cerro Pabellón in Chile, at about 4,500 m, is South America's first geothermal power plant (T). Geothermal brines elsewhere in the world are being tested as lithium sources; pairing the two here is a reasonable guess (EG).
- **Earthquakes.** The coast faces a subduction zone capable of magnitude 8 to 9. Large events struck Antofagasta in 1995, Tocopilla in 2007, and Iquique in 2014, and a long quiet segment off northern Chile is considered overdue. Tsunami risk for ports; shaking, landslides, and damaged tailings dams inland (T).

## Sky

- **Why observatories are here.** High, dry, stable air and dark skies. Water vapor blocks the wavelengths that ALMA's dishes study, so it sits at 5,000 m on the Chajnantor plateau. The Very Large Telescope is on the coast range at Paranal, and the Extremely Large Telescope, with a main mirror about 39 m across, is being finished nearby. A Japanese telescope on Cerro Chajnantor at about 5,640 m is the highest on Earth (T).
- **Needs:** dark skies, radio silence, clean air. Threatened by mine lighting, dust, satellite constellations, and new industrial projects (T).
- **For people on the ground:** the Milky Way casts shadows. The Magellanic Clouds are visible. Andean cultures name dark constellations, shapes made by the dust lanes, including a llama whose eyes are Alpha and Beta Centauri (T).

## Deep time and the human past

- **Fossils.** Dinosaur trackways cover a near vertical limestone wall at Cal Orck'o near Sucre, thousands of prints from a lakeshore tilted upright by the rising Andes, and more at Torotoro. Fossil algae reefs mark ancient lake shores. Marine fossils sit at 4,000 m (T).
- **Meteorites.** The Atacama is one of the best places on Earth to find them: nothing rusts, nothing buries them, and some have lain on the surface for a million years or more. Monturaqui, south of the Salar de Atacama, is a well preserved impact crater (T).
- **Preservation.** The dryness keeps things. The Chinchorro people of the coast made the world's oldest deliberately prepared mummies, around two thousand years before Egypt. Textiles, baskets, food, and bodies survive for millennia (T).
- **Archaeology.** Rock art and giant hillside geoglyphs marking old caravan routes; fortified hilltop towns (pukaras); the Inca road system crossing the desert; Tiwanaku's influence from Lake Titicaca; colonial silver mills at Potosí; llama caravans that linked coast, desert, plateau, and jungle for thousands of years (T).
- **Climate archives.** Lake sediments, salt cores, rodent middens, and ice cores from Andean peaks record wetter and drier ages, which is how we know the great lakes came and went (T).
- **Genes and language.** Ten thousand years of life at altitude left measurable adaptations. Linguists and communities work to document and revive Kunza, Uru, and regional varieties of Quechua and Aymara (T).

## Other science done here

- **Cosmology.** Telescopes on and around Chajnantor measure the faint afterglow of the Big Bang, because so little water vapor is left above 5,000 m. Argentina hosts instruments in the Salta Puna, including a cosmic microwave experiment and a joint radio telescope with Brazil near San Antonio de los Cobres (T).
- **Cosmic rays.** The Chacaltaya laboratory above La Paz, at about 5,200 m, is where the pion was first detected in the 1940s. High sites across the region still host particle detectors (T).
- **Atmosphere.** Stations track UV, ozone, aerosols, and greenhouse gases in some of the cleanest and thinnest air available (T).
- **High altitude medicine.** La Paz, El Alto, Potosí, and the Chilean mining sector are natural laboratories for how bodies cope with low oxygen, permanently and in shifts (T).
- **Agriculture.** Quinoa's tolerance of salt, frost, and drought makes it a subject of global crop research; native potato diversity is a living gene bank; camelid fiber and health are studied across the plateau (T).
- **Earth observation.** Flat, bright, stable Uyuni is used to calibrate satellites. Radar satellites track ground movement at volcanoes, mines, and aquifers (T).
- **Seismology and volcanology.** Dense networks watch the subduction zone and the volcanic arc. Northern Chile is one of the best instrumented earthquake gaps in the world (T).
- **Space analogs.** Rover trials, life detection tests, and crew simulations in terrain that stands in for Mars (T).
- **Who the scientists are:** national universities in Antofagasta, Arica, Salta, Jujuy, La Paz, Potosí, and Oruro; government surveys; international observatory staff; mining company labs; visiting foreign teams; and increasingly community monitors and indigenous researchers. Friction over who gets credit, samples, and access is normal (T).

## Energy

- Large solar farms and a molten salt solar tower with night time storage already operate in the Atacama. The grid to move the power south is the bottleneck (T).
- Green hydrogen and solar powered processing are widely proposed (EG). Exporting sunshine as fuel or computation at scale is possible but depends on water, transmission, and capital (S).
- Cold nights help cooling; dust, altitude, and earthquakes hurt equipment (T).

## Science that makes scenes

Water boiling at 87 C and tea that never gets hot enough. A pressure cooker hissing in every kitchen. A newcomer's headache on day one and bad decision on day two. Blue lips on an old miner. Sunburn through cloud. A nosebleed in a meeting. Static snapping off a fleece. A thermos frozen by morning. A river running orange below an old mine. A copper pit so deep the trucks at the bottom look like toys. A smelter plume at dawn. A dinosaur footprint on a wall above a cement quarry. A meteorite sitting on the desert surface where it fell before humans existed. Salt crust crunching like snow. A pickup sunk to its doors where the crust gave way. Lightning walking across a flooded salar. Steam columns at dawn that vanish by nine. A viscacha asleep in the sun. Flamingos that were here last year and are not this year, and an argument about why. A hydrologist's two graphs that both fit the data. The Milky Way bright enough to walk by. A sign asking visitors to switch off phones near a telescope. A yareta the size of a car that was a seedling when the Inca arrived.

## Cut list

These fail the Thorne test for this setting. Any of them can still be used as a declared **License** under the rules above, never by accident.

- A salar collapsing into a sinkhole or an aquifer "draining" overnight. Change is centimeters and years.
- Glowing toxic sludge or radioactive ore as a default. The real hazards are dust, acid, arsenic, heavy metals, mercury, reagents, tailings, and water loss.
- A super eruption or the plateau magma body waking up within a human timescale story.
- Rain returning to the Atacama core as a regular thing, or engineered weather greening the plateau.
- People adapting to altitude in a day, or newcomers performing normally at 4,500 m on arrival.
- Helicopters, drones, and engines working as they do at sea level.
- Flamingos or vicuña as year round fixtures in one spot. They move.
- One clean scientific answer to who dried the wetland. Expect uncertainty and honest disagreement.
- Infinite cheap desalinated water on the plateau. The energy to lift it is a hard limit.
- Microbes or minerals with magical properties. The real extremophiles are strange enough.

## Output formats

**Fact check:** label each scientific element of a scene T, EG, S, L, or Cut, with a one line reason and a plausible substitute.

**Explainer:** how something works, in plain language at the length the writer asks for, with the one number or image that makes it stick.

**Hazard sheet:** for a place and season, what the environment can do to people, vehicles, and equipment, and what locals do about it.

**Researcher sheet:** a scientist or technician character's field, employer, funding, instruments, field season, what they are trying to prove, and who dislikes their results.

**License log:** a running list for the project. For each L: what it is, the story reason, the nearest T, EG, or S version, what must stay rigorous around it, and whether it is the premise, a small license, or L clarity. Show the log whenever a plausibility review is given, and warn when the count is getting high.

## Keep current

Search before relying on: ore grades, production figures, and processing methods for a specific mine or salt flat; tailings and drainage studies; subsidence and wetland studies; status of the big telescopes; volcanic alert levels (Sernageomin in Chile, SEGEMAR in Argentina, Bolivia's observatory); seismic hazard assessments; arsenic, lead, mercury, and lithium health studies; glacier inventories.

Sources: USGS, Sernageomin, SEGEMAR, Bolivia's Observatorio San Calixto, Chile's DGA and Argentina's provincial water agencies, ESO and ALMA, NASA astrobiology field reports, peer reviewed work on Salar de Atacama hydrogeology, Andean high altitude medicine research, and university groups in Antofagasta, Salta, Jujuy, La Paz, and Potosí.
