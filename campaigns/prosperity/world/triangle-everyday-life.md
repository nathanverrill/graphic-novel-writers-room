# Everyday Life in the Lithium Triangle

Uses the same plausibility labels as the companion skills.

Baseline is September 2026. Written from general knowledge. Customs vary valley by valley; check specifics with sources from the actual community when a story depends on them.

**"Lithium triangle" is only the name of the region here.** This skill is not about lithium. Treat lithium as one activity among many (copper, silver, tin, gold, borates, herding, farming, tourism, astronomy, trade, cities) and bring it in only when the writer's story does.

**This is a setting kit, not a plot.** It describes an environment, its constraints, and categories of things that can happen there. Do not steer toward any particular storyline, theme, protagonist, or political reading, and do not assume the writer wants conflict, villains, or a message. Serve whatever story the writer brings: a romance, a comedy, a procedural, a family saga, a thriller, a children's book. Offer options across the range rather than a favorite, and use only the parts that the writer's story touches.

## Plausibility labels

Everything here is labelled with the vocabulary defined in `agents/skills/hard-sf-rules.md`: **T** truth,
**EG** educated guess, **S** speculation, **L** license, and **Cut** for what does not fit. The
rules for a license, the license log and the Thorne and Tyson tests are in that file; read it
before inventing anything the story then has to live with.

Here the "science" is what is known about how people in the region actually live.
Baseline is September 2026. Exchange rates, inflation, and subsidies here change by the month. **Never hardcode a price or rate in a story without searching first**, and for near future stories prefer ratios ("a month's rent," "three shifts' pay," "a llama") over numbers.

## Read this first

- **These are real, living peoples,** not a backdrop. Most daily life here has nothing to do with mining. People fall in love, argue about football, worry about exams, and complain about the bus.
- **Nobody is frozen in time.** The same woman may wear a pollera and bowler hat to market, a tracksuit at home, and run the family's accounts on a phone. A yatiri may also drive a tour jeep. Hold both.
- **No single "Andean village."** A Lickanantay oasis in Chile, a Kolla herding hamlet in Jujuy, an Aymara quinoa town in Oruro, a Quechua mining city in Potosí, and a Uru Chipaya settlement by Coipasa differ in language, history, state, and outlook.
- **Avoid mysticism as magic.** Ritual is social, practical, and sincere. It is not special effects.
- If a work is headed for publication, recommend the writer consult people and sources from the specific community.

## Who lives here

- **Indigenous nations:** Lickanantay or Atacameño (Chile, around the Salar de Atacama; heritage language Kunza, being revived), Aymara (northern Chile, western Bolivia), Quechua (Potosí and south Bolivia, parts of Jujuy and Salta), Kolla (northwest Argentina), Uru Chipaya and Uru Murato (Bolivia, among the oldest peoples of the plateau), Diaguita and others further south (T).
- **Mestizo and criollo** townspeople, traders, officials, and old mining families (T).
- **Newcomers:** mine workers from the lowlands and cities, migrants from Venezuela, Colombia, Peru, Haiti, and within each country, foreign engineers, tourists, scientists, and hostel owners from everywhere (T).
- **The diaspora.** Most families have more members in Calama, Antofagasta, Salta, Jujuy, Buenos Aires, La Paz, El Alto, Cochabamba, Santa Cruz, or abroad than in the home village. They come back for fiestas, elections, and funerals, and still count as community members (T).
- **Languages.** Spanish everywhere, with regional accents that mark country and class at once. Quechua and Aymara are widely spoken in Bolivia, less by the young; both are official there. Bilingual schooling exists in all three countries with uneven quality. Code switching is normal (T).

## Three countries in daily life

| | Chile | Argentina | Bolivia |
|---|---|---|---|
| State presence | Strong: clinics, subsidies, paved roads, police | Provincial: depends on the governor and the year | Thin outside towns; community fills the gap |
| Feel | Orderly, expensive, formal paperwork | Warm, improvised, political talk everywhere | Collective, organized from below, protest as civic habit |
| Hot drink | Tea, instant coffee, "once" in the late afternoon | Mate, shared from one gourd | Coca tea, api, coffee distilled as essence |
| Staple treat | Sopaipillas, completos, empanadas de pino | Empanadas salteñas, locro, asado | Salteñas mid morning, tucumanas, anticuchos at night |
| School look | Uniform with sweater | White smock (guardapolvo) | White smock, parades with marching bands |
| Football | Cobreloa, the miners' club of Calama | River or Boca allegiance plus the local side | Home games at altitude that visiting teams dread |

## Places people live

- **Oasis village (Chile):** adobe and volcanic stone, a white church with a cactus wood door, irrigated plots of alfalfa, maize, and fruit behind mud walls, poplar and chañar trees, tour agencies on the main street (T).
- **Puna hamlet (Argentina):** a few dozen adobe houses, a school, a chapel, a football pitch of bare earth, corrals of stacked stone, a solar panel array donated by some program, wind (T).
- **Altiplano town (Bolivia):** a plaza with a bandstand, brick and adobe houses with tin roofs, unfinished upper floors, a market street, a coliseum built with royalty money, minibuses idling (T).
- **Mining town and city:** see the money and hard rock skills. Add: neighborhoods named for the mine sections, union halls, a miners' market, company housing in rows (T).
- **Herding post (estancia):** a one room stone hut and corral hours from the village, used seasonally. Often a grandmother or a child with the animals and a radio (T).
- **Houses.** Thick adobe holds the day's heat into the night. Little or no heating; people wear layers indoors and sleep under heavy wool blankets. Cooking on bottled gas, sometimes a clay oven or a fire of tola brush outside. Rooftop tank, outdoor sink, solar water heater if lucky. A patio is the real living room. Dogs on the roof (T).

## Food and drink

- **Altitude cooking.** Water boils near 87 C, so everything takes longer and pressure cookers are universal (T).
- **Staples:** potatoes in dozens of varieties; chuño (potatoes freeze dried by night frost and sun, stored for years); quinoa; maize as mote, toasted kernels, and humitas; broad beans; llama meat fresh or as charque (jerky); lamb; goat cheese; bread baked in clay ovens (T).
- **Dishes:** calapurca (soup heated by dropping a hot stone in the bowl), patasca, locro, picante de llama or pollo, tamales, sajta, chairo, fricasé for hangovers, thimpu, cazuela in Chile, asado on Sundays in Argentina (T).
- **Street and market:** the market lunch (almuerzo) of soup, main, and drink for very little; salteñas before noon; api (hot purple maize drink) with fried pastry at dawn; anticuchos grilled at night; fresh juice stalls; bags of coca (T).
- **Drink:** chicha (fermented maize) in valleys, singani (Bolivian grape spirit), beer by the crate at fiestas, wine from Cafayate and Tarija, pisco in Chile, boxed wine everywhere. Drinking is communal: one glass passes around, and the first drops go on the ground for Pachamama (T).
- **Coca:** chewed as a small wad in the cheek with a bit of alkaline paste, shared as greeting and hospitality, offered in ritual, used for work and altitude. Legal and everyday in Bolivia, tolerated for chewing in northwest Argentina, legally restricted in Chile though traditional use continues in the far north (T).
- **Meals** are early and heavy at midday. Guests are fed before anything is discussed (T).

## Dress

- **Everyday:** jeans, fleece, puffer jackets, football shirts, baseball caps, sun hats with neck flaps, work boots. Hi vis on anyone connected to a mine (T).
- **Cholita dress (Bolivia, also seen in border areas):** layered pollera skirts, shawl, bowler hat, two long braids joined at the back, jewelry as savings. Worn daily by many women with pride, and also a marker others have discriminated against (T).
- **Aguayo:** the bright striped carrying cloth, used for babies, shopping, and everything else (T).
- **Chullo** knitted hats with ear flaps, ponchos in northwest Argentina for gauchos and fiestas, ojotas (tire rubber sandals) on older herders (T).
- **Fiesta:** embroidered dance costumes costing a fortune, masks, matching outfits for a whole fraternity (T).
- **Skin:** sun darkened cheeks, cracked lips, lots of lip balm and sunscreen among the young (T).

## Family and obligation

- **Compadrazgo.** Godparents for baptism, first haircut, graduation, marriage, even a new house or truck. Creates lifelong ties of respect and mutual help, often across class lines (T).
- **Ayni** is reciprocal help between households: I roof your house, you roof mine, and everyone remembers. **Minka or faena** is communal work for the whole village: cleaning canals, fixing the road, building the school (T).
- **Rotating office.** Adults take turns in unpaid community posts over a lifetime, climbing from minor duties to the top authority. Couples often serve as a pair. Refusing harms your standing (T).
- **Assemblies** decide by long discussion toward consensus. Attendance is expected; fines for absence exist. Absent members in the city may still vote, which is increasingly contested (T).
- **Gender.** The ideal is complementary pairs (chachawarmi in Aymara). In practice women run markets, household money, and much of the organizing, and carry the heaviest load. Machismo, domestic violence, and alcohol are real problems that women's organizations confront (T).
- **Marriage** may follow years of living together. Weddings are large, gifts are recorded, and the record creates obligations to reciprocate (T).

## Faith and ritual

- **Catholic and Andean at once.** People pray to the Virgin and feed Pachamama without any sense of contradiction. Mountains are persons (apus, achachilas, mallkus) owed respect (T).
- **Pachamama.** August is her month: a hole is opened in the earth and given food, drink, coca, and cigarettes. Year round, first sips are poured for her. A ch'alla (sprinkling with alcohol, confetti, and streamers) blesses a new house, vehicle, business, or machine (T).
- **Offerings (mesas, q'oa)** prepared by a ritual specialist (yatiri) and burned, for health, luck, travel, a new venture (T).
- **Evangelical and Pentecostal churches** have grown fast. Converts often give up alcohol and fiestas, which eases some household problems and splits communities over ritual and community obligations (T).
- **In the mines,** the Tío, a horned figure in the tunnel, receives coca, alcohol, and cigarettes. Above ground, God; below ground, his territory (T).

## The festival year

| When | What |
|---|---|
| January | Alasitas in La Paz: miniatures of what you wish for, blessed for Ekeko, god of abundance |
| February or March | Carnival. Oruro's is the great one: tens of thousands of dancers, diablada devils, morenada, caporales, brass bands for days. In northwest Argentina, carnival opens by digging up a buried devil figure, with flour, basil, and coplas. Water fights everywhere |
| Holy Week | Processions, pilgrimages on foot |
| May | Tinku in northern Potosí: ritual combat between communities, blood for the earth, now policed. Feast of the Cross |
| June 21 | Aymara New Year and Inti Raymi at the winter solstice: crowds greet sunrise with raised palms |
| June 24 | San Juan: bonfires on the coldest night |
| July 16 | La Tirana in the Chilean desert: a tiny town swells to a couple hundred thousand for masked dance fraternities honoring the Virgin of Carmen |
| August | Pachamama month. Also Urkupiña near Cochabamba, where pilgrims break stones as loans from the Virgin |
| Varies, often summer | Llama marking (señalada, floreo): animals get colored yarn in their ears, with music, drink, and offerings |
| Spring, by village | Canal cleaning festivals in the Atacama oases: the whole community clears the irrigation channels with ritual, song, and feasting. Water as a shared, sacred duty |
| October | Barter fairs such as the pot fair at La Quiaca, where highland and valley people still trade goods without money |
| November 1 and 2 | All Saints and Day of the Dead: tables laden with bread figures (t'antawawas) and the favorite foods of the dead, visits and music at the cemetery. November 8 in La Paz, decorated skulls (ñatitas) are honored |
| Every village | The patron saint's day: mass, procession, dance troupes, bands, fireworks, drinking, sponsored by that year's hosts at great cost and great honor |

## Music and dance

Panpipe troupes (sikuris) playing in interlocking pairs so that no one musician holds the whole tune. Charango, quena, bombo. Brass bands that can play for twelve hours. Huayno, saya, tinku, morenada, caporales, cueca in its Chilean and Bolivian forms, zamba and chacarera in northwest Argentina, and the copla: improvised verses sung alone with a small drum across the Puna. Alongside all of it: cumbia from every speaker, reggaeton, Mexican ballads, rock nacional, Bolivian folk pop, and whatever teenagers worldwide are listening to this year (T).

## Growing up

- Small village schools with a few teachers for all grades. Older students board in town or move in with relatives. University means the city, and many never return (T).
- Children help early with animals, siblings, the stall, the fields. In Bolivian mining areas some work in and around the mines (T).
- Civic parades with uniforms and drums on national days are a big deal (T).
- First haircut ceremony (rutucha) with godparents giving money for each lock (T).
- Military service still shapes young men in Bolivia (T).
- Teenagers live on phones like teenagers anywhere, in places where signal exists only on a particular hill (T).

## Health and healing

- A health post with a nurse, a doctor on rotation, an ambulance hours from a hospital. Births increasingly in clinics; intercultural birthing rooms exist in some hospitals (T).
- Traditional healers work alongside biomedicine: yatiris, bonesetters, midwives, and the Kallawaya, itinerant herbalists from Bolivia famous across the Andes. Illnesses such as susto (fright, loss of spirit) are treated ritually (T).
- Common troubles: respiratory illness from cold and dust, sun damage, altitude related problems, diabetes rising with changing diets, alcohol, road accidents, miners' lung disease (T).
- Old people often stay in the village alone while family is away, with a pension, a few animals, and a radio (T).

## Getting around

Shared minibuses and taxis that leave when full. Overnight long distance buses with blankets. The back of a truck. Pickups, motorbikes, bicycles, and long walks. A few trains (Oruro to Uyuni to the border; a tourist train into the clouds above Salta). Roads that wash out each summer. Blockades as a normal travel risk in Bolivia and Argentina. Hitching rides with mine vehicles when allowed. Flights only from regional cities. Time is elastic; in Bolivia people joke about "Bolivian time" (T).

## Work outside the mine

Herding llamas, sheep, and goats. Quinoa (boom then bust in the 2010s), potatoes, maize, alfalfa, garlic, fruit in oases. Hand cutting salt into blocks and drying it in cones at the edge of Uyuni. Weaving and knitting. Tourism as drivers, guides, cooks, and hostel owners, including hotels built of salt blocks. Market trading and cross border commerce, mostly by women. Teaching, nursing, municipal jobs. Seasonal migration for harvests. Construction in the cities (T).

## Media, phones, and talk

Local radio is the public square: announcements, greetings, messages to herding posts, politics, and music. WhatsApp groups run families, communities, and protests. Facebook for the over thirties, short video apps for the young. Football on a shared screen. Signal that exists only at a known spot. Satellite internet spreading fast (T).

## Manners

Greet everyone when entering a room or a bus. Shake hands or a light hug with a pat; one cheek kiss in Chile and Argentina. Use titles and "don" or "doña." Accept what is offered, at least a little. Do not rush to business. Ask before photographing people, animals, or rituals, and expect to be asked for a payment at tourist sites. Indirectness is politeness; a "yes" may mean "I hear you." Teasing and nicknames are constant and affectionate. Show up to work parties and funerals (T).

## Death

Wakes at home with coca, alcohol, and cards through the night. Burial in walled cemeteries with niches and small houses for the dead. The soul returns each year at All Saints for three years, and is fed and then sent off. Miners who die underground are mourned by the whole cooperative or union. Roadside shrines mark crash sites (T).

## Texture for scenes

A pressure cooker's whistle at noon. The smell of burning tola and koa resin. A grandmother sorting quinoa on an aguayo in the sun. A radio greeting read out to a herding post. Llamas with pink and orange yarn in their ears. Confetti still on a pickup's hood weeks after its blessing. A first sip tipped onto the floor. Kids in white smocks kicking a ball at 4,000 m. A brass band rehearsing badly in a schoolyard. A cheek bulging with coca. Adobe walls warm at dusk and a room freezing by dawn. Dogs barking from rooftops. A market woman's arithmetic, faster than the phone. Bread babies on a table for the dead. A teenager on the one hill with signal. A canal running with water for exactly the hours that are yours.

## Cut list

These fail the Thorne test for this setting. Any of them can still be used as a declared **License** under the rules above, never by accident.

- The timeless, isolated village untouched by the outside world.
- Everyone in traditional dress, all the time, or nobody ever.
- Shamans with actual supernatural powers, curses that work, or mountains that literally speak, unless the story is openly magical realist, in which case that is the License.
- A single indigenous viewpoint on mining, the state, religion, or anything else.
- Uniform poverty, or noble simplicity. People have ambitions, debts, feuds, favorite shows, and opinions about football.
- Coca depicted as a drug habit.
- Women as silent background. They run much of the economy and the organizing.
- Interchangeable "Andean" culture. Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia differ, and so do neighboring valleys.
- Fiestas as quaint entertainment for outsiders. They are the institutions through which standing, obligation, and belonging are made.
- Spanish only settings. Other languages are in the room, even when not spoken to the outsider.
- Daily life revolving around the mine. For many people it is a distant thing that pays a cousin.

## Output formats

**Household sheet:** who lives here, who is away and where, languages spoken, income sources, animals and land, house description, faith, obligations this year (offices, godchildren, fiesta sponsorship), a worry, a hope.

**Day in the life:** hour by hour for a named person in a named season, with food, weather, work, and who they talk to.

**Year calendar:** the agricultural, school, festival, and weather cycle for a specific place.

**Meal:** what is on the table, who cooked it, how long it took at that altitude, who is served first, what is said.

**Fiesta sheet:** patron, sponsors and what it costs them, dance groups, bands, schedule over the days, who comes back for it, what goes wrong.

**Depiction check:** review a scene for accuracy and stereotype. Say what rings true first, then what to fix and how, and where to seek community sources.

**License log:** a running list for the project. For each L: what it is, the story reason, the nearest T, EG, or S version, what must stay rigorous around it, and whether it is the premise, a small license, or L clarity. Show the log whenever a plausibility review is given, and warn when the count is getting high.

## Keep current and go deeper

Customs here are summarized broadly. For a specific community, look for its own publications, community radio, municipal pages, and cultural organizations, and for ethnography and oral history from regional universities. Useful starting points: Museo Le Paige successors and the Consejo de Pueblos Atacameños (Chile), Museo Nacional de Etnografía y Folklore (La Paz), Bolivian and Argentine community radio networks, Thomas Abercrombie, Olivia Harris, Tristan Platt, Denise Arnold, and Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui on Andean society, June Nash on Bolivian miners, and films and music made by people from the region.
