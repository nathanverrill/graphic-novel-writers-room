However, I’m very concerned about the graphic novel. As it stands, I cannot ethically present this to any student associated with Young Americans, as it just simply does not meet the standard of quality we require nor the mission-aligned content. I’ve outlined the big picture challenges below, but with the aggressive timeline we’ve targeted for this, I think we may need to push things back.

The overall quality of the imaging and text is very poor. It’s hard to read, there are typos, and it’s extremely obvious that it was AI generated. I don’t think there is too much we can do about the AI look and feel at this point, but high school students are very opposed to this level of AI generated work. The text, however, will need to be refined significantly so it can be read. It’s REALLY hard to see and the pixelation is unclear.
The plot of this story is mostly good, but it is extremely hard to follow. There is no mention of water in the first chapter, so it feels odd that the missions associated with this chapter focus on water. It feels displaced and random to introduce the water subject matter after reading a story that doesn’t even mention it.
We need a bit more setting background to really understand what’s happening in the 3 different cities. We understand that Alex has debt from his father, but otherwise, we don’t know any of the problems that exist in Keel. We don’t have any background about the water wars or what happened. It’s only mentioned casually in passing and it’s very challenging to understand what’s going on once Alex finds Bi11Bot. “The First Waypoint” section is very unclear as to what they are trying to do. We know he has a map and the mine (which he don’t know anything about) is sealed, but what’s he trying to do?
Chapter 2 makes a lot of assumptions about what the reader already knows. “What Alpha Is” section I believe is trying to set the stage with what was called Alpha Dynamics in the first iteration, but now we have no idea what it is or how it fits into this story.
“The Three Safeguards” is a really cool concept, but not really explained in a way that I can follow. How are they supposed to be used? What are they supposed to do? Is there a degree of magic here? It’s very theoretical, but I can’t understand how it operates.
Much of the language also exists in this riddle-style prose that, when combined with the challenging text quality, the gaps in background, and the slightly unclear sequence, all make it very challenging to stay engaged.
The scenes in the mine and passageway in chapter 2 are all just a bit technical and hard to follow.
“The Lie” is also a really cool concept that seems to be the anchor to this whole story, but is presented in a fragmented way that doesn’t emphasize the importance. Similarly, there isn’t any background in the earlier chapters/sections about water shortages or lack of water, so the reader now doesn’t really understand this. When he releases the water, it appears to be overflowing everywhere, which would be wasting it, so it just seems confusing.
Now why is Alex leaving? Why are all the people leaving? He released the water so now it’s confusing why everyone is talking about leaving that night.
I honestly have no idea what is happening in “The Train” section. I don’t know what a single one of the dialogue bubbles is talking about.
Chapter 3 just needs a bit more background about the families of Halyward because it’s a cool concept, but probably could use a little more explanation.
It would be nice to have a better understanding where these collaborators came from. If they are from the 3 families, why are they sharing? What was the event that caused them to betray their ‘house’? Why are they doing it? We need to understand what their individual talents are that they bring together to form a powerful team. Right now, they come off as loners.
Again, we are presented with the problem (water purity) way too late to be attached to it in any way. This should be introduced in Chapter 1, not Chapter 3.
At the end of Chapter 3, we start veering back into the realm of altruism over capitalism. I think both can exist at the same time and I can’t present content to students that is at direct odds with our mission. We believe free enterprise, entrepreneurship, and financial management are powers of good when used appropriately. We teach about the importance of profit and protecting intellectual property, etc. The end of this chapter becomes very problematic.
