# Lithium Triangle Futures

A plausibility constrained world kit. Everything here is either already happening, a straight extrapolation, or a defensible stretch. Things that would not happen in this region are listed in the Cut List so they stay out.

Baseline is current to September 2026. Politics and ownership move fast. If the user's story depends on a specific company, law, or president, search to confirm before treating it as fact.

## How to use

1. Find out the year, the country and salar, and whose point of view (community member, worker, engineer, company, state, smuggler, outsider).
2. Pick a price scenario (see Scenarios). It drives everything else.
3. Build from the Drivers and the country specific rules. The three countries are not interchangeable.
4. Tag every invented element with a plausibility label (see next section).
5. If a trope from another region or genre comes up (conflict minerals, oil delta militias, post apocalypse), translate it using "Importing tropes from elsewhere" below instead of dropping it in unchanged.

**This is a setting kit, not a plot.** It describes an environment, its constraints, and categories of things that can happen there. Do not steer toward any particular storyline, theme, protagonist, or political reading, and do not assume the writer wants conflict, villains, or a message. Serve whatever story the writer brings: a romance, a comedy, a procedural, a family saga, a thriller, a children's book. Offer options across the range rather than a favorite, and use only the parts that the writer's story touches.

## Plausibility labels

Everything here is labelled with the vocabulary defined in `rules/hard-sf-rules.md`: **T** truth,
**EG** educated guess, **S** speculation, **L** license, and **Cut** for what does not fit. The

Here the "science" is the region's physics, law, economics, and politics.

## The physical facts that constrain every story

- **It is brine, not rock.** Lithium here is dissolved in salty groundwater under salt flats (salares). You pump it from wells, concentrate it, and process it chemically. There is no pit, no tunnel, no ore to carry off in a sack. This single fact kills most "artisanal lithium" and "warlord mine" plots. Nobody mines brine with shovels.
- **Two methods.** Evaporation ponds: huge turquoise, yellow, and white rectangles, 12 to 18 months in the sun, low freshwater use but enormous brine loss to the sky. Direct lithium extraction (DLE): a chemical plant with columns, membranes, and tanks, faster, small footprint, reinjects spent brine, but needs more freshwater, power, and reagents. The near future is a mix, shifting toward DLE.
- **The water fight is about the edge of the salar.** Freshwater and brine sit side by side underground. Pumping either can drop the water table at the margins where the vegas and bofedales (high wetlands), lagoons, flamingos, llama pasture, and village wells are. Damage is slow, disputed, and hard to prove, which is the whole drama.
- **Altitude.** 2,300 m at the Salar de Atacama, 3,600 to 4,500 m across the Puna and Uyuni. Hypoxia, cold nights, brutal UV, wind, almost no rain. Workers rotate in shifts (7x7, 14x14) and live in camps. Altitude is a main reason for automation and remote operation.
- **Emptiness.** Tiny populations, huge distances, few roads, one or two border passes (Jama, Sico, Socompa, Ollagüe). Open terrain with nowhere to hide an army. Conflict here is about chokepoints: a road, a pipeline, a well field, a border post.
- **Other things in the ground.** Copper (giant, nearby, and thirstier than lithium), borates, potash, gold, silver, zinc. Copper mining already built the desalination pipelines, ports, and power lines the region runs on.
- **Climate.** Long term drying, glacier retreat, erratic summer rains, flash floods that wreck ponds and roads. Bolivia's Lake Poopó already dried up. Less water, same or more demand.

## Baseline 2026 (T)

**Chile.** Lithium is legally reserved to the state and can only be produced under special presidential contracts (CEOLs) and nuclear agency quotas. The Salar de Atacama is now run by NovaAndino Litio, a Codelco majority venture with SQM, with rights out to 2060 and commitments not to raise brine or freshwater extraction. Albemarle operates next door. Rio Tinto is partnered with Codelco at Maricunga and with Enami at Altoandinos, both planned as DLE. New salars are being opened under the current Kast government, which kept the framework with a more private friendly tilt. Water rights were historically private, tradable, and separate from land (1981 code); a 2022 reform prioritizes human consumption and makes new rights time limited. Atacameño (Lickanantay) communities have a council, direct revenue agreements, and run their own environmental monitoring. SQM has been sanctioned in the past for over extraction. Satellite studies show the salar surface subsiding where pumping is heaviest. The city of Antofagasta and the big copper mines already run largely on desalinated seawater pumped uphill.

**Argentina.** Provinces own the minerals and the water, not the nation. Jujuy, Salta, and Catamarca each run their own regime, and each has a provincial company that takes a small stake. The RIGI regime locks in tax, customs, and currency rules for 30 years for big projects, so this is where foreign capital is flooding: Rio Tinto (which bought Arcadium), Ganfeng, Posco, Eramet, Zijin, Lithium Argentina, and many juniors. Dozens of projects, many owners, fast permitting, thin oversight. Flashpoints: Salinas Grandes and Guayatayoc communities refusing entry for over a decade; the 2023 Jujuy constitutional reform protests and blockades; the Río Los Patos case at Hombre Muerto, where a vega dried after a company aqueduct and a provincial court froze new permits; Fiambalá. Surface owners do not own minerals; mining easements can be imposed on them.

**Bolivia.** The constitution reserves lithium to the state. The state company YLB has spent 15 plus years and large sums producing very little. Contracts with a Chinese consortium (CATL linked) and a Russian state firm for DLE plants at Uyuni were signed, then suspended by a court after Nor Lípez indigenous communities sued over lack of consultation and unknown freshwater sourcing. The new Paz government has turned toward the US and Western investors, but opening the sector fully needs constitutional change. Potosí's civic movement has already killed one foreign deal (the German one in 2019) over royalties. Bolivia's mining cooperatives are a political and physical force: tens of thousands of members, dynamite, and a history of violent standoffs. Bolivia is also where the 2000 Cochabamba Water War happened, so "water is not for sale" is national identity, not a slogan.

**Market.** Lithium crashed from around 80,000 dollars a tonne in late 2022 to around 10,000 by 2024 and 2025. Juniors went broke, majors bought them cheap. Sodium ion batteries, recycling, and new supply (Australia, Africa, China, US brines) all cap the long term price. Boom and bust is the normal state.

**Crime.** Northern Chile has seen Tren de Aragua and other transnational groups move in (extortion, trafficking, migrant smuggling). The altiplano is an old contraband corridor for cocaine, stolen cars, fuel, and consumer goods. Armed gangs have robbed copper cathode trains and trucks in Antofagasta. Cocaine processing needs sulfuric acid, hydrochloric acid, and solvents, which mines buy in bulk.

## Drivers (what moves the future)

1. **Lithium price.** High price means rush, new salars, water stress, in migration. Low price means mothballing, layoffs, abandoned camps, broken promises.
2. **Battery chemistry.** Sodium ion and recycling reduce lithium's leverage over time. The triangle may have a 20 to 30 year window, and everyone knows it, which makes states and companies hurry.
3. **US and China rivalry.** Ownership, offtake contracts, port access, and who builds the plant are all geopolitical. Expect pressure, forced sales, blacklists, and "friendly" financing with strings.
4. **Water and climate.** Less recharge, more users. Desalination pipelines extend inland and become critical infrastructure and targets for protest.
5. **Indigenous rights law.** ILO 169 consultation, the Escazú agreement, EU battery passport and due diligence rules, and courts that actually issue injunctions. Communities have lawyers, drones, and their own sensors.
6. **State capacity.** Strong in Chile, uneven and provincial in Argentina, weak and politicized in Bolivia. This decides who fills a vacuum.
7. **Automation.** Fewer local jobs per tonne, which undercuts the main promise made to communities.

## Scenarios (pick one as the backdrop)

| Scenario | Price | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Long boom** | High through the 2040s | Every salar has a project. DLE plants, solar fields, worker camps, new roads. Towns triple. Water conflict at maximum. States fight companies for share. Communities split between those with agreements and those without |
| **Sawtooth** (most probable) | Spikes and crashes every 5 to 8 years | Projects open, stall, change owners, restart. Half built plants. Towns with a boom district and a dead district. Deep cynicism. Each new owner disowns the last one's promises |
| **Long fade** | Low after the mid 2030s as sodium ion and recycling bite | Only the lowest cost operations survive (Atacama, a few in Argentina). Stranded projects everywhere. Cleanup liabilities nobody pays. The region pivots to copper, solar export, green hydrogen, tourism, or nothing |
| **Strategic capture** | Any | Lithium treated as a security asset. Operations run under government to government deals, with security perimeters, restricted airspace, and opaque contracts. Less market, more garrison |

## Mining and technology (T to EG)

- DLE plants beside or replacing ponds. Visually: clusters of tall adsorption columns, tank farms, pipe racks, reagent silos, reinjection well fields, and a large lined pond or two kept as buffer. Old evaporation ponds remain as colored scars for decades.
- Power from large solar PV fields plus battery storage, some solar thermal for process heat. Diesel backup never quite goes away.
- Freshwater from wells at the salar margin (contested), or trucked, or, for rich operations in Chile, desalinated seawater piped 150 to 250 km uphill. In Argentina and Bolivia the coast is another country away, so freshwater stays local and contested.
- Remote operations centers in Santiago, Antofagasta, Salta, Jujuy, or overseas. On site crews shrink to maintenance, labs, security, camp services. Autonomous haul trucks already run in Chilean copper; expect autonomous well field inspection, drones for pond and pipeline patrol, robotic sampling, and sensor dense wells.
- Workers: rotating fly in or bus in shifts, mandatory altitude medical checks, oxygen enriched dorm rooms, fatigue monitoring wearables. Many are not local. Locals get contracts for transport, catering, laundry, security, and water trucking through community owned companies, which become the real local economy and the real local power.
- Monitoring arms race: company telemetry versus community run piezometers, satellite radar (InSAR) subsidence maps, drone flamingo counts, university hydrologists for hire. Each side has its own model of the aquifer and they never agree.
- Traceability: every bag of lithium carbonate tagged for battery passport rules. Audits (IRMA and successors) become a lever for communities and a box ticking industry for consultants.
- Downstream promises (cathode plants, battery factories in the region) mostly do not arrive. A few pilot plants and a lot of ribbon cuttings. Bolivia's dream of making its own batteries stays a dream or a showroom.

## Water (T to S)

**Who holds rights**
- Chile: legacy private water rights held by mining companies, some bought decades ago; newer rights time limited; human consumption has priority on paper. Indigenous communities hold some ancestral rights and have bought others. Rights can be leased, traded, and litigated.
- Argentina: the province grants water permits. The same provincial government that permits the mine also polices it and owns shares in it.
- Bolivia: water is a public good and a human right; private ownership is politically impossible. In practice the state allocates, and consultation is the battleground.

**What water conflict actually looks like here (not armies)**
- Road blockades (cortes de ruta) for days or weeks. The single most effective tool. A blocked road stops reagents in and product out.
- Court injunctions freezing permits. Long, slow, and sometimes ignored.
- Assemblies and votes to expel a company. Banners, wiphala flags, livestreams.
- Sabotage: a cut pipeline, a disabled well pump, slashed pond liners, burned pickup trucks. Rare now, plausible more often later (S).
- Community versus community: the village that signed an agreement versus the one downstream that did not; herders versus those with mine contracts; young people who want jobs versus elders. This is the most painful and most realistic conflict line.
- Town versus mine: when the municipal wells drop, water trucks become daily life, and the mine's trucks are visible on the same road.
- Police and gendarmerie repression, criminal charges against leaders, surveillance of activists. Deaths are possible and have precedent in the wider Andes, but this is closer to Peru's mining conflicts than to a civil war.
- Cross border: Chile and Bolivia already went to the International Court of Justice over the Silala waters. Future disputes over shared aquifers and rivers are plausible as diplomatic and legal fights, with troops at border posts as theater, not war (S at most).
- Water theft: unmetered wells, illegal extraction by smaller operators, water truck mafias selling to camps and towns (EG).
- Desalination dependency: inland towns and mines hanging off one pipeline from the coast. A quake, a strike, or a blockade shuts everything (EG).

## Land and mineral rights (T)

The core injustice engine, and it is legal, not violent:

- Subsoil minerals belong to the state (Chile, Bolivia) or province (Argentina). Surface owners and indigenous titleholders do not own what is under them.
- Mining concessions can be staked over community land. In Argentina claims are cheap to hold, so speculators and juniors blanket entire salars, then flip them.
- Indigenous land is often collectively titled but incompletely, with boundaries disputed between neighboring communities. Companies negotiate with whichever community or leader will sign.
- Consultation is a right, consent is not. States treat consultation as a step; communities treat it as a veto. Most conflict lives in that gap.
- Benefit agreements: a percent of sales, jobs quotas, contracts for community companies, scholarships, a clinic, a sports field. They create new local elites and new fights over who controls the money.
- Layers of paper: a single hectare can have a mining concession, a water right, an indigenous land title, a protected area designation, a tourism concession, and a provincial easement, held by different parties, some of them shell companies.
- Closure and cleanup obligations exist but are under bonded. When an owner walks, the liability is argued over for years.

## Foreign conglomerates and the abandonment pattern (T to EG)

The realistic life cycle:

1. **Junior stakes it.** A small Canadian, Australian, or local company claims the salar during a boom. Geologists, a drill rig, a camp of containers. Promises are made to the nearest community by people with no authority to keep them.
2. **Major buys it.** At the peak, or cheaply in the crash, a major (Western, Chinese, Korean, Gulf) acquires the junior. New logo, new community relations team, old promises "under review."
3. **Build or bank.** If the price holds, they build. If not, the project goes to "care and maintenance": a skeleton crew, locked gates, half lined ponds, test wells, unpaid local contractors, a security contract.
4. **Flip or walk.** The asset is sold again, swapped in a merger, written down, or the local subsidiary is left to go insolvent. Parent company liability stops at the shell.
5. **Vacuum.** The camp, the wells, the access road, the water permit, and the social debt remain.

This has already happened in small ways across the Puna in the 2023 to 2025 crash. In a Sawtooth future it happens repeatedly.

## Importing tropes from elsewhere: who fills the vacuum

A common imported trope, drawn from conflict mineral regions elsewhere: a large foreign company buys a small local one, abandons it, and an armed strongman takes over. In this region the literal version is **Cut** (available only as a declared License). Reasons: brine cannot be mined artisanally; there is no lootable ore; the terrain is open and empty with no population to tax or conscript; all three states, including Bolivia, keep military and police presence on the altiplano and near borders; and there is no recent history of territorial armed groups here.

What plausibly fills the vacuum instead:

| Who | Where | What they take over | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The security contractor** left guarding the site | Anywhere | Becomes the de facto authority: controls the road, the gate, the fuel, the water. Starts charging. Owner is three shells away and does not care | EG |
| **Provincial political boss** (caudillo) and family | Argentine provinces | Concessions reassigned to friends, provincial company takes the site, contracts go to cousins. Legal, entrenched, untouchable | T to EG |
| **The transport or contractors' union** | Argentina, Chile | Controls who drives, who hauls, who works. Blockades on demand. Skims every contract | T to EG |
| **Mining cooperatives** | Bolivia | Move on anything diggable nearby (borates, gold, silver tailings, salt), occupy camps, demand concessions, fight police with dynamite. They cannot run a DLE plant, but they can hold the ground and the politics | EG |
| **Community enterprise or faction** | All three | Takes the camp, the vehicles, the wells. Sometimes legitimately as compensation, sometimes one family's grab. Splits the village | EG |
| **Smuggling networks** | Border zones, all three | Use abandoned airstrips, camps, roads, and fuel tanks. Divert acid and solvents. Launder through contractor companies. They want quiet, not territory | EG |
| **Transnational gang cell** | Northern Chile towns, Calama, border crossings; spreading | Extortion of contractors and shops, control of migrant labor and sex trade in boom towns, truck hijacking. Urban and logistical, not a salar holding militia | EG to S |
| **Truck hijackers** | Highways to Antofagasta, Mejillones, and Argentine railheads | A truckload of lithium carbonate is worth a few hundred thousand dollars. Same crews that hit copper cathodes. Needs a corrupt buyer | S |
| **A smaller, rougher operator** | Argentina, Bolivia | Buys the distressed asset for nothing, runs it dirty, ignores water limits, pays off inspectors, sells to whoever does not ask for a passport tag | EG |
| **A foreign state firm with a security deal** | Bolivia most plausibly | Takes the site under a government to government agreement, brings its own guards and workers, closed compound | S |

The closest plausible thing to that strongman is a composite: an ex police or ex military security boss with a contractor company, a cousin in the provincial government, a deal with the smugglers who use his road, and a few dozen armed guards who are technically licensed. He never declares anything. He just becomes the person you have to see. That character is S and works in any of the three countries, best in a remote Argentine department or Bolivia's southwest.

## Mining towns (T to EG)

Real anchors: San Pedro de Atacama, Toconao, Peine, Socaire, Calama, Antofagasta, Mejillones (Chile). Susques, Olaroz Chico, San Antonio de los Cobres, Tolar Grande, Salar de Pocitos, Antofagasta de la Sierra, Fiambalá (Argentina). Uyuni, Colcha K, Río Grande, San Cristóbal, Llica, Potosí (Bolivia).

What a near future lithium town looks like:
- An old adobe and stone core with a church and plaza, ringed by unfinished brick and block houses with rebar sticking up for the next floor, ringed again by container camps and truck yards.
- Pickup trucks with whip flags and company numbers. Buses at 5 am. Hi vis vests drying on lines.
- Rents and prices that locals cannot pay. Shops that take dollars. Hostels converted to worker housing. Tourism squeezed out or coexisting awkwardly (Uyuni, San Pedro).
- Water by truck or by hours. Rooftop tanks. A company donated reverse osmosis plant with a plaque, sometimes working.
- Solar on everything, because the sun is free and the grid is far. Starlink dishes. Cell coverage that follows the mine, not the village.
- A community company office (transport, catering) that is the best building in town. A shuttered office of the previous project owner.
- New arrivals: workers from the lowlands, Venezuelan, Colombian, Peruvian, and Bolivian migrants, Chinese and Korean engineers in their own compound. Bars, informal clinics, evangelical churches, moneylenders.
- Quechua, Aymara, Kunza revival, and Spanish, plus Mandarin and English signage at the plant gate.
- In the bust version: the same town with half the lights off, container camps stripped for parts, a lined pond full of windblown dust, and everyone waiting for the next buyer.
- Ceremonies to Pachamama at project openings, attended by executives, which some locals find respectful and others find obscene.

## Characters and factions that ring true

Community water monitor with a laptop and a piezometer. Young local engineer working for the company her grandmother blockades. Provincial mining secretary who is also a shareholder by proxy. Remote operator in Santiago who has never seen the salar. Chinese site manager on a three year posting. Australian geologist on his fourth owner. Community relations officer who believes it, until she does not. Cooperative leader with a dynamite stick in his belt and a seat at the ministry. Hydrologist whose model gets bought by whoever pays. Truck union delegate. Flamingo biologist. Gendarme at a border post at 4,200 m. Water truck owner getting rich. The security boss described above. An Atacameño council president negotiating a percent of sales while being called a sellout. A lawyer from an international NGO. A battery company auditor with a checklist and two days on site.

## Cut list (do not include)

These fail the Thorne test for this setting. Any of them can still be used as a declared **License** under the rules above, never by accident.

- Warlords with militias holding salars, child soldiers, or artisanal lithium diggers. Wrong geology, wrong geography, wrong states.
- Interstate war between Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia over lithium or water. Lawsuits, border theater, and trade retaliation yes. Shooting war no.
- Foreign military invasion or overt occupation. Pressure, financing, coups by lawfare, and security "cooperation" are the plausible forms.
- The salar "running out" of lithium within the time frame. The resource is vast. Water and price are the limits.
- Salt flats collapsing into sinkholes or towns swallowed. Subsidence is centimeters per year, measured by satellite.
- Instant ecological collapse. The damage is slow: a lagoon shrinking over a decade, a vega browning, flamingo counts drifting down.
- Lithium as priceless. It is a bulk chemical in one tonne bags. Value is in volume and contracts.
- Fully lights out mines with zero humans. Maintenance, labs, and security stay human.
- Bolivia becoming a battery superpower. Possible it finally produces raw lithium at scale; not plausible it dominates manufacturing.
- Communities as uniformly anti mining or uniformly victims. They are divided, strategic, and often want a better deal, not no deal.
- Companies as cartoon villains. The plausible harm comes from shell structures, turnover, indifference, and incentives, not evil plans.
- Privatized water in Bolivia. Politically dead since 2000.
- Dense jungle, guerrillas, or cartels with territorial control on the Puna. Wrong ecosystem, wrong region.

## Output formats

**World brief:** year, scenario, country and salar, who owns what (minerals, water, land, plant), active conflicts, tech level, what changed since 2026, three sensory details, plausibility labels.

**Location sheet:** for a town, plant, camp, blockade, or abandoned site. History of owners, current controller, water source, power, access road, who works there, what is broken, what is contested.

**Faction sheet:** what they want, what leverage they hold (road, permit, water, votes, guns, paper), who they depend on, where they are split internally.

**Plausibility review:** when the user brings a plot, tag each element T, EG, S, or Cut, explain briefly, and offer the nearest plausible substitute for anything cut.

**Timeline:** sawtooth price curve with ownership changes, elections, court rulings, droughts, and one or two shocks.

**License log:** a running list for the project. For each L: what it is, the story reason, the nearest T, EG, or S version, what must stay rigorous around it, and whether it is the premise, a small license, or L clarity. Show the log whenever a plausibility review is given, and warn when the count is getting high.
