# Chapter 2 — Mission Integration, Week 2: Imagine (Missions 3 and 4)

I recommend introducing Missions 3 and 4 in the same way as Chapter 1: foreshadow the skills and
decisions through Alex's story, then use Alex's retrospective voice to invite students into
their own missions after the chapter.

Chapter 1 asks students to understand a problem and discover why it matters to them. Chapter 2
should move them toward imagining what could be different.

The connection is particularly compelling because Alex discovers that Keel's water crisis isn't
simply a lack of water. The Measure reveals that the system is distributing water differently
from what residents have been told. That changes the possibilities for Keel's future.

**The narrative bridge:** Once you understand the problem, you can begin imagining a different
future.

## 1. Connect the missions to the Chapter 2 storyline

| | Mission 3 — Dream Beyond the Obvious | Mission 4 — 2037: If We Get This Right |
|---|---|---|
| Superpower | Creative Visionary | Creative Visionary |
| Primary skill | Imagination | Vision |
| Secondary skill | Teamwork; Financial literacy | Leadership; Philanthropy; Goal Setting |
| Story connection | Alex, Ada, Bi11bot, Tomas and Keel's residents see possibilities beyond the accepted way of doing things | Alex and Ada begin considering the kind of future Keel could have, rather than simply accepting the world Alpha created |
| Student task | Generate many possible solutions and identify 2–3 promising directions | Select one promising idea and envision what success could look like in 2037 |
| Key deliverables | Five individual ideas; collaborative Dream Map; 2–3 team directions | Individual North Star Statements; shared creative expression of the team's 2037 vision |

The distinction between these missions should be clear:

- Mission 3 encourages **divergent** thinking: What else could be possible?
- Mission 4 encourages **convergent** vision: Which possibility is worth pursuing, and what would
  success look like?

## 2. Strengthen the narrative foundations within Chapter 2

I would make three relatively small adjustments to the revised Chapter 2 script. We don't need
to restructure it again.

### Page 3 — The three safeguards demonstrate imagination

Adrian's greatest innovation isn't simply three artifacts. It's the idea that a world governed
by Alpha could operate differently. Once Bi11bot explains how the Measure, Maker's Seal and
Covenant work, Ada could ask:

> ADA: "So your father wasn't trying to build a better Alpha?"
>
> BI11BOT: "No. He was imagining a world where people didn't need Alpha to make every decision."

This introduces imagination as the capacity to challenge an existing system — not merely invent
a new gadget. It also creates a foundation for Mission 3: students should not limit themselves to
improving the system exactly as it exists.

### Page 5 — Ada and Alex should consider more than one way forward

The rewritten Page 5 is already focused on entering the mine, finding the Measure, and securing
enough resources to travel. I would add a short brainstorming moment in which Alex initially
proposes one obvious approach, but Ada and Bi11bot suggest alternatives. For example:

> ALEX: "The old entrance is sealed. We're stuck."
>
> ADA: "There are other ways into a mine. Ventilation shafts. Maintenance tunnels. The active
> workings."
>
> BI11BOT: "Three possibilities. And we've only just started thinking."
>
> ALEX: "Okay. Which one can we actually use?"

This scene models generating alternatives before evaluating them. We should keep it short and
avoid adding new technical complications. The purpose is to show the thinking process that leads
to their chosen plan.

### Page 13 — Show the beginning of a shared vision for Keel

This is the strongest opportunity to foreshadow Mission 4. After the Measure exposes the
discrepancy and workers begin safely restoring water delivery, I would add a small moment when
Keel's residents begin imagining what improved water access could make possible. These could be
brief imagined glimpses rather than developments that have already happened. One character might
ask:

> TOMAS: "Imagine what Keel could do if we actually got the water we were promised."

The scene should not imply that exposing Alpha's false records automatically solves the city's
broader challenges. Rather, it opens the possibility of a different future. That is exactly where
Mission 4 begins.

## 3. Use Alex's retrospective voice to introduce Week 2

I would introduce the two missions after Chapter 2, Page 15, once Alex, Ada and Bi11bot have
boarded the train to Halyard. The chapter should still close on the mysterious follower. The
mission handoff can appear on a separate transition page or in the Evoke Hub, ensuring the
cliffhanger remains intact.

### WEEK 2 — IMAGINE: A Word From Alex

"When we found the Measure, I thought we'd finally discovered the answer to Keel's water problem.

I was wrong.

We'd discovered the truth about what was happening. That wasn't the same as knowing what to do
about it.

Ada saw possibilities I hadn't considered. Tomas knew things we didn't. Bi11bot kept asking
questions that made our first ideas seem pretty small.

And once everyone in Keel saw the evidence, something changed. People started asking a different
question.

Not just *why are things this way?*

But *what could they become?*

That's where your next two missions begin."

### Mission 3 — A Message From Alex

"Back in Keel, I thought there were only two choices: stay trapped by my father's debt or find a
way to leave.

Ada saw another possibility. What if Keel itself could change?

That's the trouble with problems you've lived with your whole life. After a while, you stop
imagining things could be different.

So here's your next mission.

Take the water challenge your team discovered last week. Now imagine every possible way it could
be addressed. Five ideas each. At least.

Practical ones. Wild ones. Ideas that sound impossible.

Maybe a nonprofit could help. Maybe people could share resources differently. Maybe there's a
technology nobody has tried — or a solution that's been right in front of everyone.

Don't pick your favorite too soon. Put all your ideas together. Look for patterns. Combine ideas
that nobody thought belonged together. Then choose two or three directions worth exploring
further.

Bi11bot will probably tell you that your first idea isn't your best. Annoyingly, he's often
right."

### Mission 4 — A Message From Alex

"When we left Keel, the water was beginning to flow differently. But the biggest change wasn't in
the pipes.

For the first time, I could imagine coming back to a city that wasn't the same one I'd left.

Ada had been imagining that future long before I did. What would Keel look like if people had
reliable clean water? What could families build? What opportunities might kids have that we'd
never been given?

We didn't have a complete plan. But we finally had a reason to imagine one.

Now it's your turn.

Your team has come up with some promising ideas. Choose one. Then travel forward to the year
2037.

Imagine your idea worked. Who is better off? What has changed? What would someone living in that
future say about it? And what would have to happen to make that future real?

First, decide what matters most to you. Write your North Star — the change you want to help
create. Then compare your visions as a team.

Create something short that helps the rest of us see that future: a headline, a message from
2037, a graphic novel panel, or something entirely your own.

Make me believe it's a future worth building."

# Chapter 3 — Version 5 Addendum: Week 3 Mission Integration (Missions 5 and 6)

The page-level links for Missions 5 and 6 (pages 4, 9, 12–13 and 14, including Lock asking who
will pay) are already in input/bob-chapter-3-v5-notes.md. What follows is new.

**Objective:** Integrate the learning concepts of Missions 5 and 6 into Chapter 3 through Alex's
experience turning a collaborative invention into a viable entrepreneurial opportunity.

## Mission 5 — What Would It Take — for Real?

Foreshadow budgeting through the challenges Alex and the collaborators encounter when trying to
develop their invention.

- On Page 4, establish that valuable patents do not automatically become viable products or
  businesses.
- On Page 9, show the team identifying necessary people, materials, equipment, time, and
  financing.
- Distinguish essential startup expenditures from costs that can be deferred.
- Reinforce that accurate budgets require research and evidence, not wishful estimates.
- Present budgeting as a practical act of prioritization and courage.

After Chapter 3, use Alex's retrospective narration to introduce Mission 5, inviting students to
research startup requirements for their own solutions and produce a simple team starter budget.
Students must retain the required individual research snapshot and team budget deliverables.

## Mission 6 — What If We Actually Did This?

Foreshadow creative financial problem-solving through the redesign and commercialization of the
water-purification technology.

- On Pages 12–13, show Alex and the collaborators reducing unnecessary costs through
  experimentation, redesign, collaboration, and iteration.
- On Page 14, use Director Lock's commercially realistic questions to introduce customer demand,
  income, financing, royalties, and financial sustainability.
- Make clear that Alex is choosing a financially uncertain licensing strategy rather than giving
  away valuable intellectual property.
- Show that financial constraints can inspire creative solutions without pretending that all
  costs can be eliminated.
- Preserve the importance of safety, product quality, and ongoing maintenance.

After Chapter 3, use Alex's retrospective narration to introduce Mission 6, inviting students to
stress-test their budgets, revise unrealistic assumptions, and identify potential sources of
revenue, donations, sponsorship, or other financing. Students must retain the required individual
reflection and updated team budget deliverables.

## How Mission 6 should work

I would preserve the existing three steps, while highlighting the different thinking required in
each.

| Step | What students do | Connection to Chapter 3 |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Stress Test the Plan | Identify weaknesses, opportunities, unrealistic assumptions, and ways to reduce or phase costs | Alex and the collaborators discover their initial prototype isn't commercially viable |
| 2. Build the Other Side of the Budget | Brainstorm possible funding, income, or revenue sources | Alex considers Lock's offer versus licensing to independent entrepreneurs |
| 3. Update Your Budget | Revise estimated expenditures, revenue sources, and assumptions | Alex revises the product and business model rather than abandoning the invention |

I particularly like Mission 6's emphasis on the difference between income and expenditure.
Students should encounter both the cost of launching an initiative and the resources required to
continue operating it. A nonprofit might rely on donations, grants, partnerships, or service fees.
A new business might earn revenue through sales, subscriptions, services, or licensing. Some
projects may use a combination.

We should avoid implying that one funding model is always superior. Students should be encouraged
to identify the arrangement that fits their solution, beneficiaries, customers, and long-term
goals.

### A recommended team exercise: Bi11bot's Budget Challenge (optional)

To make the mission more engaging, I would add an optional challenge for Bi11bot to pose:

> **Bi11bot's Budget Challenge.** Your proposed startup resources have just been reduced by 25%.
> How would you get started without compromising the essential parts of your solution?
>
> - Start with a smaller pilot
> - Find a partner to share costs
> - Redesign a costly component
> - Find an additional income source

This would help students recognize budgeting as an exercise in prioritization and creative
problem-solving rather than mere arithmetic. It should remain optional so the core mission does
not become too burdensome.

## Mission transition design

Use the established format:

1. A short reflection from an older Alex connecting the chapter's events to students' experience.
2. A direct mission invitation in Alex's voice.
3. The complete student activity within the Evoke Hub.
4. Bi11bot as a questioning partner who challenges assumptions, estimates, and reasoning.
5. Clear individual and team evidence requirements.

### WEEK 3 — IMAGINE: A Word From Alex

"Finding the Maker's Seal took a lot more than finding four people and putting their inventions
together.

We had an idea. A pretty incredible one, actually. We even had a prototype.

But here's something nobody tells you when you're dreaming up the future:

Ideas need resources.

People. Tools. Materials. Time. Money.

And there never seems to be enough of any of them.

At first, we thought the biggest challenge was making the technology work. Then we realized we had
to make it affordable enough for someone to build a business around it.

That meant making choices. What did we absolutely need? What could wait? What could we build
differently? And how would anyone make enough money to keep the whole thing going?

Some of our best ideas failed. Some of the failures turned into better ideas. And the numbers kept
changing.

That's what your next two missions are about.

You've imagined a future worth building.

Now let's figure out what it might actually take."

### Mission 5 — A Message From Alex

"When we first put my father's inventions together, I thought the hard part was over.

Then Lina showed me what the materials would cost. Ren pointed out the equipment we'd need. Suri
reminded us that getting something to work once isn't the same as making it reliable.

And Ada asked a question that made everything worse.

'What can we actually afford?'

I wasn't thrilled with that question. But it turned out to be one of the most useful questions
anyone asked.

Here's your mission.

Take the solution your team imagined last week and figure out what it would really take to get
started.

Who would you need? What equipment or materials? What partnerships? How much time? And how much
money?

Don't just make up the numbers. Research them. Ask Bi11bot to challenge your estimates.

Then make some choices. What do you absolutely need to begin? What could wait?

Put your best estimates into a simple starter budget.

It doesn't have to be perfect. Ours certainly wasn't.

But it has to be based on something more than hope.

Courage isn't pretending your idea will work. It's finding out what it will take to give it a real
chance."

### Mission 6 — A Message From Alex

"So you've got a budget.

Let me guess. It's more expensive than you expected.

Welcome to the club.

Our first prototype cost too much. It used too much power. Half the parts couldn't be manufactured
outside the Houses. For a while, I thought we'd have to give up.

But the team kept asking different questions. Could we make it smaller? Could we use different
materials? Could someone else provide equipment we couldn't afford?

And then came the biggest question of all.

Who would actually pay for this?

Director Lock offered us a guaranteed deal. Instead, we decided to take a chance on entrepreneurs
who could build businesses using the technology.

It wasn't a guarantee. We had to figure out how those businesses could make money — and how we
could earn enough royalties to keep improving the invention and pay down my debt.

That's your next mission.

Go back to your budget. Find what doesn't work. Challenge what you assumed. Ask Bi11bot, your
teammates, and other people to help you find a better way.

Then look at the other side of the budget.

Where could the money come from? Customers? Sponsors? Community partners? Donations? Some
combination?

You don't need all the money today. But you need a believable idea of how your project could get
started and keep going.

Build a better version of your budget.

And remember: creativity isn't just about having a great idea. Sometimes it's about finding a way
to make the idea possible."

## Narrative principle

Chapter 3 should demonstrate that entrepreneurial success requires imagination, collaboration,
experimentation, financial planning, protected intellectual property, and sustainable revenue.
Profit and positive community impact should be portrayed as potentially complementary.

**Desired learning connection:** Students understand that budgets are not merely financial
constraints. They are tools for making informed decisions, managing risk, prioritizing resources,
and finding creative ways to bring valuable ideas into the world.
