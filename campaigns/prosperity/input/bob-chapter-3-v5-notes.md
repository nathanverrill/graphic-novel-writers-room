# **Prosperity — Chapter 3 Writers’ Room Review for Version 5**

## **The Price of Ownership: From Monopoly to Entrepreneurship**

I think your proposed revisions make Chapter 3 considerably more interesting. They shift the conflict away from a familiar story about powerful corporations exploiting inventors and toward a more nuanced question:

What happens when an economy becomes so concentrated that people lose the freedom to create, compete, fail, and build businesses of their own?

The three Charter Houses need not be evil. They can be exceptionally good at scaling technology, providing stable employment, and managing large industrial systems. Their failure is that they have become so dominant—and so focused on protecting their existing businesses—that they have stopped making room for new ideas and new entrepreneurs.

That gives Alex a more compelling choice than whether to sell or give away his father's patents.

He can accept a guaranteed payment from one powerful buyer, or take a risk on many smaller entrepreneurs who could turn those patents into successful businesses.

The second path offers no guaranteed return. Some businesses will fail. But those that succeed can create value for communities, generate income for their founders and collaborators, and gradually help Alex repay his inherited debt.

This approach also connects Chapter 3 directly to the themes of creative destruction, entrepreneurship, innovation, ownership, and financial risk.

## **1\. Recommended revised Chapter 3 arc**

P1–4

A city without entrepreneurs

Halyard prospers, but its three conglomerates dominate ownership. Alex discovers his father's patents and receives a credible offer from Director Lock. One particular patent cluster points toward the Maker's Seal.

P5–9

The innovation that collaboration unlocks

Alex reunites Adrian's four collaborators across five specialized units within the three Houses. Their separate ideas become more valuable when combined, and Alex begins improving the unfinished system through experimentation.

P10–13

A different business model

Lock offers an attractive financial exit. Alex proposes licensing affordable, modular technology to independent entrepreneurs rather than transferring exclusive control to one conglomerate. The team tests, fails, and iterates toward a commercially viable design.

P14–16

Ownership restored, competition begins

The collaborators receive recognized ownership. The Maker's Seal is recovered through their collective actions. Independent businesses begin competing with the Houses, initial revenue flows toward Alex's debt, and Mera observes the consequences from Oasis.

### **Revised central question**

The current central question asks whether Alex would surrender ownership of his future to escape his past.

I would sharpen it:

**Would you trade the uncertain possibility of creating prosperity for many people for the certainty of financial freedom for yourself?**

And the revised Prosperity Principle could be:

Prosperity grows when people have the freedom to create, own, compete, and share in the value they build.

Importantly, this should include both entrepreneurs and employees. Working for one of the Houses can be a perfectly legitimate choice. The injustice is that independent entrepreneurship has become practically impossible.

## **3\. Pages 1–4: Establish the economic conflict and the quest**

### **Page 1 — Halyard: A city where invention no longer means independence**

I would keep Halyard visually prosperous. It's important that readers see why its economic model is attractive.

The problem isn't that the three Houses create no value. They do. The problem is that new entrepreneurs cannot compete against them.

We could communicate this visually through three contrasting details:

* Busy advanced factories, impressive research centers, good employment opportunities, and high-quality infrastructure.  
* Formerly independent shops and workshops now owned by the Houses, with their original signs barely visible.  
* Inventors who develop valuable products but have no realistic pathway to leave their employer and start competing businesses.

I would retain the engineer at the contribution kiosk but change the dialogue.

ALEX: “She invented it. Why doesn't she start a business?”

BI11BOT: “She could try. But the Houses control the factories, distribution networks, and nearly every license.”

ADA: “So she has the freedom to invent. Just not the freedom to compete?”

BI11BOT: “Precisely.”

This is a much clearer expression of the economic challenge.

One important distinction: the patent system itself should not be the villain. Properly structured intellectual-property rights can help small inventors attract investment, license innovations, and compete. Halyard's failure is that the Houses have effectively captured the systems through which those rights are registered, funded, and commercialized.

### **Page 3 — Make Director Lock a genuine alternative**

The Version 4 script already depicts Lock as courteous and patient, but later pages increasingly portray him as someone using surveillance and threats to force a sale.

I would reduce those elements.

Lock should genuinely believe that the Houses are the most responsible way to commercialize Adrian's patents.

His logic is understandable:

* They can offer a large, guaranteed payment.  
* They possess manufacturing capacity and distribution networks.  
* They can finance development and absorb failure.  
* They can commercialize some inventions much faster than a fragmented group of small businesses.

The limitation is that they may prioritize protecting their existing markets over introducing disruptive technologies.

A suggested exchange:

LOCK: “Your father was a brilliant inventor. But an invention isn't a business.”

ALEX: “And the Houses can make it one?”

LOCK: “We have the capital, factories, engineers, and customers. We can take discoveries from a workshop to the entire city.”

ALEX: “And if someone wants to build something different?”

LOCK: “They're welcome to try. But scale is what makes innovation useful.”

That last line should be sincere. Lock sees scale as the answer; Alex will eventually argue that widespread experimentation and competition create another kind of scale.

One open story question: Does Lock represent a joint commercialization authority shared by all three Houses, or is he an independent investment executive authorized to acquire estates on their behalf? The Version 4 imagery already places him above any single House. I would formalize that position.

### **Page 4 — Make the patent-to-Seal connection explicit**

This is a major plot-clarity problem. The current archive shows hundreds of patents and a glowing cluster, but doesn't convincingly explain why Alex must focus on this particular invention.

I would establish that Adrian embedded a Maker's Seal identifier within one collaborative water-purification patent cluster.

That cluster contains five complementary technologies developed with four collaborators, alongside Adrian's own work. The collaborators each possess knowledge or technical records that cannot be reconstructed from the archive alone.

A clear dialogue exchange:

ALEX: “Three hundred and twelve patents. How do we know which one leads to the Seal?”

BI11BOT: “Because your father marked this one.”

A bronze Maker's Seal symbol appears over the water-purification patent cluster.

ADA: “Can you open it?”

BI11BOT: “Part of it. But four collaborators hold the missing pieces.”

ALEX: “So finding them brings us closer to the Seal?”

BI11BOT: “And may finally make the invention work.”

That gives the entire collaborator journey a dual purpose:

Find the people → complete the invention → recover the Maker's Seal.

It should not depend on Alex blindly trusting that something will eventually emerge.

If Version 5 ultimately uses 147 patents rather than 312, the count should be updated consistently throughout the script. The draft I reviewed currently states 312 patents, 47 restricted, and 19 disputed.

## **4\. Pages 5–9: Make collaboration the engine of innovation**

### **Page 5 — Replace “own the failure”**

The existing line is:

> “And every House would rather own the failure than share the solution.”

It sounds dramatic but doesn't accurately describe the issue you're trying to explore.

I would replace it with:

LINA: “Every division has a piece of the solution. But nobody gets rewarded for helping another division succeed.”

ALEX: “Even if it means solving the problem?”

LINA: “Especially if it threatens something they're already selling.”

That introduces the innovators' dilemma through character dialogue rather than a business-school explanation.

### **Page 6 — Suri as the persistent innovator**

I strongly prefer your revision.

Suri shouldn't be the last employee because of an efficiency decision. She should be the person who refuses to abandon an idea that everyone else considers a dead end.

Her laboratory can still be full of empty desks, but they signify loss of belief rather than staff reductions.

Suggested exchange:

ADA: “Where's the rest of your team?”

SURI: “Moved on. Different projects. Better chances of getting promoted.”

ALEX: “And you stayed?”

SURI: “Because I think this works. I just haven't found the missing piece yet.”

This makes Suri a compelling example of perseverance and conviction.

For the confusing “part that could actually die” line, I'd substitute:

SURI: “My biofilter uses living organisms to clean contaminated water. Get the conditions wrong, and the whole system collapses.”

BI11BOT: “Which is why Adrian trusted you to keep experimenting.”

Now the science and the personal responsibility are clear.

### **Pages 7–8 — Keep the collaborator journey**

These scenes can stay largely intact.

The key change is that each newly recovered component should advance both the engineering challenge and the Maker's Seal quest.

For example, Bi11bot can display five empty positions surrounding the Seal's symbol. As each collaborator contributes their work, another position fills in.

This visual mechanic will let readers track progress without repeated explanations.

On Page 8, Ren should say:

“Five different research units. Three Houses. And not one of them figured out what we could build together.”

That's more consistent and meaningful than “five Houses.”

### **Page 9 — The power of crossing disciplines**

This scene already has one of the strongest moments in the chapter: Alex recognizes that the separate inventions don't fit together because each was designed around different standards.

I would broaden that insight slightly.

The Houses have become excellent at optimizing their existing businesses. But their researchers seldom collaborate outside their own divisions, let alone across competing Houses.

Alex's strength is not that he knows more than the specialists. It's that he sees connections among their inventions that the existing organizations overlooked.

Suggested dialogue:

LINA: “Each unit perfected its own technology. None of us designed them to work together.”

ADA: “So everyone's solving their part of the problem?”

ALEX: “But nobody's solving the whole thing.”

This is an excellent moment to reinforce that different skills and perspectives can produce something greater than the sum of their parts.

I would keep Alex's improvised connector solution. It has a satisfying payoff to his mechanical instincts from Chapter 1\.

## **5\. Pages 10–13: Turn the invention into an entrepreneurial opportunity**

This is where I would make the most important philosophical shift.

In the current draft, Alex responds to Lock's offer by proposing to release the technology without patents or ownership.

Your new direction is stronger: Alex chooses a different ownership and commercialization model, not the absence of ownership.

### **Page 10 — What is the invention worth, and to whom?**

Lock returns after seeing that Alex has made the incomplete technology work. His offer improves because the invention is now more valuable.

I would remove the coercive deadline and threats against collaborators as the main source of tension. Instead, use an attractive offer with a genuine decision deadline tied to commercial opportunity or financing.

Lock could tell Alex:

LOCK: “Your father's patents were valuable. What you've done is more valuable. You've brought together technologies we've kept separate for years.”

ALEX: “You could manufacture it?”

LOCK: “At scale. And I can offer you financial security today.”

ALEX: “What happens to the people who helped develop it?”

LOCK: “We can compensate them fairly. But the Houses would own the technology.”

LOCK: “Think carefully about what your father's work is worth—and who can make the most of it.”

The final question should trigger Alex's new idea: perhaps the best buyers or licensees aren't the Houses at all, but entrepreneurs who can build businesses using the technology locally.

### **Page 11 — Make the alternative visible**

I would let Alex explain the opportunity to Ada and Bi11bot.

ALEX: “What if we didn't sell the whole thing to one company?”

ADA: “Who else could afford it?”

ALEX: “Not one buyer. A hundred small businesses. Maybe more.”

BI11BOT: “Assuming they can manufacture it. And find customers. And survive their first year.”

ALEX: “Then we design it so they actually have a chance.”

This turns the chapter from a debate about sacrifice into a problem-solving adventure.

Alex isn't giving up economic value. He's exploring a different way to create it.

### **Page 12 — Design it for entrepreneurs**

Instead of “build it so small and cheap no House would want it,” I suggest:

ALEX: “What if we built it small enough for a local entrepreneur to afford?”

LINA: “This is industrial water technology.”

ALEX: “Then we redesign it. Something people can build, maintain, and sell locally.”

SURI: “A network of small businesses instead of one giant factory?”

ALEX: “Exactly. And every business gets a chance to improve it.”

For the closing line, replace “Then let's not fail” with:

ALEX: “Some of these businesses will fail. So will some of our designs. That's how we find what works.”

REN: “Now you sound like an inventor.”

I would avoid suggesting that failure itself is the goal. The goal is to lower the cost of trying, make failure survivable, and allow successful ideas to spread.

### **Page 13 — Show iterations, not overnight genius**

The existing page already includes several clever improvements. But it currently moves from a large industrial design to a successful compact unit in a few hours, making the breakthrough feel too easy.

I would show visible evidence of repeated failures:

PROTOTYPE 1

Too expensive

Works, but uses costly parts and too much energy.

PROTOTYPE 8

Too unreliable

Smaller and cheaper, but water treatment is inconsistent.

PROTOTYPE 23

Promising design

Affordable, repairable, and ready for a controlled field pilot.

Illustrative iteration numbers for the writers’ room; the final script should choose a credible development timeline.

This would make the story more authentic. The team could have conducted earlier tests on individual components before the full prototype comes together; the later iterations might happen across several days or weeks.

I would also eliminate the artificial countdown to dawn if it prevents credible experimentation.

The successful prototype should be described as ready for real-world testing, not a fully proven, market-ready water treatment product. Safe water purification would require testing, certification, reliable maintenance, and ongoing monitoring before widespread use.

## **6\. Page 14: A better discovery of the Maker's Seal**

I agree that Lina simply producing the Seal from her pocket is unsatisfying. Alex has spent the chapter reuniting people, recovering research, and solving problems. His actions should lead to the discovery.

I would make the Maker's Seal a distributed artifact that can be reassembled only when the rightful contributors recognize and combine their work.

This fits the theme of ownership and the chapter's existing structure.

### **My preferred mechanism: The five-part Seal**

### **The Maker's Seal is hidden in the invention's shared provenance**

Proposed Version 5 plot mechanism

1. Adrian separated the Seal into five authenticated components, each embedded in one research record or physical shard.  
2. Each of the four collaborators retains an essential contribution; Adrian's fifth component is accessible through Alex's inherited records or chip.  
3. When Alex reunites the team, they can reconstruct the invention—but the Seal remains inactive.  
4. The collaborators must agree how their contributions will be recognized, owned, and licensed. They authorize a shared-ownership agreement rather than assigning everything to Alex or a House.  
5. The completed record reconnects the five components, revealing the physical Maker's Seal or unlocking its hidden location.

The last step needs a tangible reveal rather than an unexplained supernatural glow. For example, the assembled shards could form a physical key that opens a sealed compartment in Adrian's original prototype. Inside is the bronze Maker's Seal.

The Seal then verifies the authenticated contributions and ownership agreement. It does not determine who morally deserves ownership or magically create legal rights.

This creates a satisfying causal chain:

Reunite the collaborators  
Complete and improve the invention  
Agree on shared ownership and licensing  
Reassemble the authenticated records

The Maker's Seal is revealed

This also links directly to Adrian's belief in human agency. Alex could have sold what he inherited, but he instead chose to create a structure in which the people who contributed could exercise ownership themselves.

### **A possible climactic exchange**

LOCK: “You're refusing a guaranteed purchase in exchange for what? Hundreds of small companies that don't even exist?”

ALEX: “Not refusing the value. Changing how we create it.”

LOCK: “And who owns the patents?”

ALEX: “The people who built them. Together. We license the technology to people who want to start businesses.”

LOCK: “What if those businesses fail?”

ALEX: “Some will. Others won't. I'm willing to take that risk with them.”

At this moment, the collaborators approve the shared-ownership and licensing arrangement. Their five records align, revealing the hidden Seal.

BI11BOT: “Well. Your father really did hide it where no one person could find it.”

This is a much stronger payoff than having Lina reveal she possessed it all along.

One continuity question to settle: the current draft describes four collaborators but five patent fragments. We should explicitly assign the fifth record to Adrian/Alex and identify which collaborator holds which technical contribution.

## **7\. The business model: Sell once or earn together?**

The financial logic should be understandable to teenagers without overwhelming the story with royalty calculations.

The two options could be shown visually:

Lock's offer

## **Sell once**

* Large guaranteed payment  
* Immediate debt relief  
* Houses control the patents  
* Large-scale commercialization possible  
* Less independent competition

Lower financial risk for Alex; control shifts to one buyer.

Alex's proposal

## **License widely**

* Smaller payments over time  
* Debt repaid gradually  
* Contributors retain ownership  
* Local entrepreneurs can compete  
* Potentially broader innovation

Higher risk and slower returns, but more opportunities for independent businesses.

I would favor a nonexclusive licensing cooperative or contributor-owned venture, rather than Alex unilaterally licensing everything he inherited.

The arrangement could work as follows: the contributors retain agreed ownership shares, independent businesses receive affordable licenses to produce and sell the technology, and those businesses pay modest royalties as sales occur. The revenue supports the inventors, future product development, and Alex's debt repayment according to an agreed allocation.

Importantly, licensees own their own businesses and can build additional value through service, maintenance, localization, and improvements. They are not simply employees or branches of a new monopoly.

A royalty structure might include reduced upfront fees, payments tied to actual revenue, and a provision allowing small businesses to fail without taking on crushing debt.

That model reinforces the same philosophy as the chapter: make entrepreneurship possible, not risk-free.

The writers’ room should also distinguish Adrian's inherited patent rights from the collaborators' documented rights and Alex's new inventions. Alex can only license rights he or the jointly authorized venture actually controls.

## **8\. Pages 15–16: The innovators' dilemma and a new race**

### **Page 15 — A race to market, not a battle of good versus evil**

I would substantially change the ending.

Lock should recognize that Alex's decentralized model is a genuine competitive threat. He need not be angry or vindictive.

In fact, he might be intrigued.

LOCK: “You've found an interesting business model, Alex. But building a prototype is one thing. Serving a market is another.”

ALEX: “Then we'll find out who can do it better.”

LOCK: “You have a network of hopeful entrepreneurs. We have factories, capital, engineers, and customers.”

ALEX: “And they have something you don't.”

LOCK: “Which is?”

ALEX: “A reason to build something better than what you already sell.”

This captures the innovators' dilemma in a way that is accessible and grounded in the story.

The Houses have substantial advantages. The entrepreneurs have flexibility, local knowledge, motivation, and the freedom to try different approaches.

Neither side should be guaranteed victory.

The reader should leave this page wondering whether a network of small businesses can actually compete with a massive conglomerate.

### **Page 16 — Show early signs of success, without resolving the race**

Before returning to Mera, I would add a short montage showing what Alex's licensing model could enable.

An independent workshop

A small team signs its first license and begins assembling water treatment units for nearby communities.

A local pilot

A community organization works with a new entrepreneur to test the system under real operating conditions.

The first earnings

A small verified sale or service payment generates the first royalty credited toward Alex's inherited debt.

The repayment should initially be modest. That's important for narrative credibility.

We could show Alex receiving a financial notification:

LICENSING REVENUE RECEIVED — 38 CREDITS

DEBT REPAYMENT APPLIED — 12 CREDITS

The precise amounts would be set later, after establishing the royalty and debt rules. A small payment is more satisfying than an implausibly large one, because it proves that the new model could work.

ALEX: “Twelve credits. That's barely anything.”

BI11BOT: “Indeed. But it's the first twelve you earned from something you helped create.”

This gives the financial storyline a concrete payoff.

### **Who owns Alex's debt?**

Your question is an excellent one, because the creditor could become an interesting secondary character or institution.

I see three possibilities:

| Creditor | Incentive | Story potential |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| A Charter House-affiliated lender | Prefers Lock's immediate acquisition because it clears the debt quickly | Reinforces the concentrated economic system |
| An independent financial institution | Will accept any credible repayment plan that generates sufficient income | Introduces a potentially pragmatic ally |
| A financial cooperative or secondary debt investor | May be willing to restructure the inherited debt against future licensing revenues | Opens a more innovative financial pathway |

I prefer an independent creditor or a lender with a genuine financial interest in repayment, even if it initially favors Lock's guaranteed payment.

This institution doesn't need to share Alex's vision. It only needs to recognize that a diversified royalty stream could have commercial value.

For example, a lender might agree to restructure part of the debt once Alex demonstrates verified recurring revenue. But the lender should not automatically forgive the balance just because the strategy sounds promising.

That creates a useful future tension: Alex must turn his optimistic proposal into actual financial performance.

### **Preserve the ending with Mera**

I would keep Mera's final scene essentially intact.

The observation that the boy on the train belonged to her is an effective signal of control, secrecy, and a larger threat. It should remain ominous and ambiguous rather than implying literal ownership.

Mera would now be observing something more consequential than the discovery of a second artifact.

Alex has begun creating an alternative economic network that Alpha and the Houses cannot easily predict or control.

The final line can remain:

MERA: “Two found. He'll want the third from me directly.”

It sets up Chapter 4 without requiring further exposition.

# **Prosperity Graphic Novel — Chapter 3 Revision Brief**

### **Overall objective**

Reframe Chapter 3 around entrepreneurship, competition, creative destruction, and the freedom to own and develop new ideas.

Halyard is a prosperous city dominated by three highly capable conglomerates—Aqualis, Helion, and Astra. Their market power, bureaucratic structures, and incentives to protect existing businesses have made independent entrepreneurship and cross-disciplinary innovation increasingly difficult.

The chapter should not portray capitalism, profitable businesses, or employment as inherently problematic. Instead, contrast a concentrated economy where innovation is controlled by a few organizations with a more dynamic economy where people can start businesses, experiment, compete, fail, improve, and share in the value they create.

Alex's central choice is between accepting a guaranteed payment for his inherited intellectual estate and taking a financial risk by licensing useful technologies to many independent entrepreneurs.

At the end of Chapter 3, readers should understand:

* Why Halyard's economic system makes independent entrepreneurship difficult.  
* Why Director Lock's offer is attractive and rational.  
* Why the patents cannot realize their full potential without collaboration.

**Desired ending:** Quest 2 completed. The Maker's Seal recovered. A new entrepreneurial network begins forming. Alex earns his first licensing revenue, and the larger financial risk remains unresolved. Mera watches from Oasis as Alex approaches the final artifact.

## **10\. One larger opportunity for the Prosperity story**

I think this revision gives the graphic novel a stronger progression across the first three chapters:

| Chapter | Artifact | Central economic question | Alex's development |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| 1 — The Inheritance | Three-artifact quest introduced | What determines my opportunities and financial future? | Begins questioning the story he inherited |
| 2 — The Price of Departure | The Measure | Who controls information and resource allocation? | Uses evidence to act on behalf of his community |
| 3 — The Price of Ownership | Maker's Seal | Who has the freedom to create, own, compete, and earn? | Becomes an entrepreneur and helps others become owners |

The Chapter 3 message should be that ownership isn't just about possessing a patent. It's about having the opportunity, resources, collaborators, and freedom to turn an idea into something valuable.

That is an especially useful lesson for Evoke's learning → creating → earning model. Alex is no longer simply inheriting his father's work. He is learning to create new value through collaboration and choosing how to share the risks and potential rewards.

My strongest recommendation is to make the Maker's Seal reveal depend on the contributors becoming genuine co-owners of the invention. That would tie the chapter's central message directly to its most important plot event and make the discovery feel earned, rather than handed to Alex.

More from Young American Bank comments:

Their decisions should involve professional judgment, ambition, loyalty to former colleagues, and personal risk.

| Collaborator | Expertise already established | Proposed motivation for joining Alex |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Dr. Lina Vey — Aqualis | Membrane purification and system integration | Believes the invention could solve a problem Aqualis has struggled with for years. She helped Adrian develop it and wants to see the research reach practical application. |

These motivations build on the existing script; they are proposed character development, not yet established biographical facts.

### **An important distinction**

Each collaborator should have a different relationship with their House.

Lina might be frustrated by bureaucracy but remain proud of Aqualis's engineering achievements. Suri might want to commercialize research that her colleagues have abandoned. Jalen might be genuinely loyal to Helion yet believe its centralized approach has technical weaknesses. Ren might value Astra's resources while wanting freedom to experiment outside its core business.

### **How to introduce their backstories**

I recommend adding a brief flashback or visual reference for each collaborator showing when they first worked with Adrian and what problem they were trying to solve.

We should answer four questions for each character:

1. How did they meet Adrian?  
2. What technical contribution did they make?  
3. Why did their collaboration end or stall?  
4. What personal or professional opportunity motivates them to reunite?

A fifth question is especially important: What are they legally entitled to share?

If the Houses own their employment-related inventions, the collaborators cannot simply take restricted intellectual property and offer it to Alex. The writers’ room should establish that the relevant research was developed under an earlier collaboration agreement, that Adrian retained legitimate rights, or that the Houses' ownership claims are disputed.

Otherwise, the story risks portraying unauthorized appropriation as entrepreneurship—the opposite of the chapter's intended emphasis on respecting intellectual property.

## **2\. Strengthen the story to foreshadow the two missions**

I recommend four targeted additions to the revised Chapter 3 script rather than introducing more exposition.

### **Page 4 — The patents have potential, but are not yet a viable business**

This is where Alex discovers that the patent cluster is connected to the Maker's Seal and that he needs the four collaborators.

We could introduce the distinction between owning valuable intellectual property and being able to commercialize it.

ALEX: “Three hundred and twelve patents. That should be worth a fortune.”

BI11BOT: “Potentially. But a patent isn't a product. And a product isn't necessarily a profitable business.”

ADA: “So what would it take?”

BI11BOT: “The right people, working technology, time, equipment—and more money than you currently have.”

This makes the financial challenge concrete while motivating the team-building quest.

### **Page 9 — The first prototype fails, revealing resource constraints**

When Alex brings the five technologies together, the initial design fails because the components were developed separately.

I would add a simple resource discussion alongside the technical failure.

LINA: “Even if we make the system work, the equipment alone would cost more than a small business could afford.”

ALEX: “What do we actually need to get a working version?”

REN: “A membrane, a biofilter, a pump, a controller, and somewhere to test it.”

ADA: “And what could wait?”

BI11BOT: “At last. A question that might save us some money.”

This is an elegant connection to Mission 5's distinction between essential and non-essential expenditures.

It also showcases different team members contributing knowledge and making tradeoffs together.

### **Pages 12–13 — The budget becomes part of the invention process**

I would make this the strongest Mission 5 and Mission 6 connection.

The team has already developed the concept. Now Alex asks whether local entrepreneurs could realistically afford to build and operate it.

Imagine a workbench with a simple cost sheet alongside the prototype.

Proposed story visual: Alex's prototype budget

| Membrane and filter | Essential |
| :---- | :---- |
| Pump and power supply | Essential |
| Basic testing and safety checks | Essential |
| Automated monitoring upgrade | Can wait, if safe operation is possible without it |
| Custom housing and branding | Can wait |

Illustrative cost categories, not engineering or safety specifications. The final script should distinguish prototype costs from manufacturing, certification, operation, and maintenance.

Rather than inventing lots of fictional credit amounts, I would show a handful of understandable costs and one prominent total.

Then show that the total is initially too high.

ALEX: “At that price, no one outside the Houses could build it.”

SURI: “Then we're asking the wrong question.”

ALEX: “Which is?”

SURI: “What could we change without sacrificing what makes it work?”

That introduces the next stage of creative problem-solving.

On Page 13, multiple iterations could show the prototype becoming smaller, less expensive, and easier to maintain. Each improved version updates the cost estimate.

But I would also show that some costs cannot simply be eliminated—particularly safe water treatment, testing, and maintenance.

The point is not to build the cheapest possible technology. It is to make the most affordable viable and safe technology.

### **Page 14 — Where will the money come from?**

The revised Page 14 already gives us a compelling choice between Lock's guaranteed purchase and Alex's licensing strategy.

This can establish the revenue side of budgeting.

LOCK: “You know what it costs to develop this. Do you know who will pay for it?”

ALEX: “Local businesses. They'll manufacture and service the units.”

LOCK: “And where will they find customers?”

ADA: “Homes. Schools. Community organizations. Places where reliable water treatment is worth paying for.”

LOCK: “And your income?”

ALEX: “We license the technology. They earn revenue serving customers. We earn royalties when they're successful.”

LOCK: “A great many assumptions.”

ALEX: “Then we'd better test them.”

I particularly like Lock's role here. He should not just challenge Alex morally. He should be an experienced businessman asking commercially intelligent questions.

That lets the story model financial thinking without lecturing.
