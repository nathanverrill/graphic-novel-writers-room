# Showrunner notes for this pass

**The fifth draft is direction, not a draft.** `input/fifth-draft/` is the author's book as they
imagined it: its premise, its people, its world and its ending are what this book is about. Its
scenes, their order and its dialogue are not. Plan new scenes, find a new order where it serves,
and write new dialogue; reuse a line from it only where nothing better exists. Keep:

- Alex, seventeen, handed his missing father's debt, and the hidden workshop where Bi11bot wakes.
- Ada, Grandmother, Tomas, Mera, Leona, Lock and the Halyard makers, as the people they are.
- The three safeguards, the Measure, the Maker's Seal and the Covenant, learned by using them.
- Adrian's fate, and why: Alpha closed the door, and Mera could not stop it.
- The ending's choice: Alex refuses to concentrate power, in anyone, including himself.

The changes below come from the analyses of the fifth draft and of the room's last script. Each
is a change to what happens on the page, not something for a character to explain.

1. **A debt owed to a person, and paid.** Somewhere in Keel, a named person fronts Alex
   something real: the medicine, the token, the parts. Alex can't pay on time. He goes to
   that person's face, says so, and they agree terms he keeps. By the end, the balance has
   moved because of payments we have seen him make. No authority cancels anything.
2. **One honest business.** Someone in the book makes and sells a thing people need, at a
   fair price, and makes money doing it: a small Keel or Halyard shop that builds the
   household purifier and sells it to Keel families, or a shop like the shuttered Morrow &
   Sons coming back to life. Set it against the Houses, which exist because Alpha picked
   them, not because they won customers.
3. **Bi11bot talks like Bill Reynolds.** Plain, a little roundabout, Western, a question
   first. He tells short stories from his own long life with Adrian, including his own
   mistakes, and jokes mostly at his own expense. At least once he is wrong, says so in
   plain words, and asks Alex what he would do. He is never a butler and never a lecturer.
4. **No lessons on the page.** No hologram history lecture, no definitions of the three
   safeguards, no recited triplets, no governance speech, no caption that states the theme
   or grades a character's growth. The world and the safeguards are learned by people
   needing them, using them and paying for getting it wrong.
5. **The ending costs something.** Alex's refusal of power costs him something we can see:
   his travel clearance, the workshop, a promise he cannot now keep. The new way of deciding
   proves itself in a scene: someone tries to rig a decision and the people it affects catch
   it on the page.
6. **Lock is a real opponent.** He makes a concrete offer a seventeen-year-old would
   seriously consider, and he has a real case for the Houses. Refusing him has to cost.
7. **Gambling is a cost, not a genius move.** Adrian's gambling hurt his family; the book
   does not make the casino a laboratory or the chip a lucky charm.
8. **Alex and Ada are friends.** Nothing in the art or the dialogue reads as romance.
9. **No real company names.** Helion, Astra and Aqualis are real companies; the Houses need
   invented names.
10. **Page 1 is a person.** The book does not open on a caption about inequality, or on any
    caption that states the theme.
11. **No slips a reader would catch.** No character mentions a chapter or issue number.
    Three Houses everywhere, never five. Bi11bot's identifier is B11 everywhere.
