# Hard SF Rules

How a grounded story invents things without lying to the reader. The subject can be any
system the story leans on: physics, engineering, economics, law, medicine, weather, how
institutions behave under pressure.

## The mix: 80 / 15 / 5

- **80 percent is already here.** The world changes slowly. Buildings, roads, laws, habits,
  furniture, grudges. Most of any near-future story is today, aged.
- **15 percent is today's emerging thing gone boring.** Whatever is a demo or a luxury now is
  cheap, scuffed, taken for granted, and has stickers on it.
- **5 percent is genuinely new.** One or two things. These carry the story's speculative idea,
  so they get the attention.

The future arrives as a layer on top of the past, never as a replacement. Retrofit beats new
build.

## Labels (Thorne's vocabulary)

Borrowed from Kip Thorne's _The Science of Interstellar_, which marks every claim as truth,
educated guess, or speculation. Label every invented element:

- **T, Truth.** Documented. Happening now or already happened.
- **EG, Educated guess.** Not yet true, but a straight line from what is. Most people who know
  the subject would bet on it.
- **S, Speculation.** Needs one or two things to break a particular way, and has real precedent
  somewhere comparable. Thorne's rule applies: speculation springs from the real record, not
  from the writer's wish.
- **L, License.** A knowing departure made for the story. Not one of Thorne's labels. **The
  reference case is Astrophage in Andy Weir's _Project Hail Mary_:** an invented microbe that
  feeds on stars and stores enormous energy. No scientist proposes it, so it is not Speculation.
  Weir declares it, gives it fixed rules, and works out every consequence honestly. The one
  invention is the threat, the fuel and the puzzle; nothing else in the book cheats to make room
  for it.
- **Cut.** Does not fit, and nobody has licensed it.

The mix maps onto the labels: the **80** is Truth, the **15** is Educated guess, the **5** is
Speculation. A License, if the story has one, lives inside the 5 and is usually the premise.

## Rules for a license

1. Very few per story. Ideally one big one, which is the premise, plus at most a handful of
   small ones.
2. Chosen on purpose, for a stated story reason, and written in the license log.
3. It cannot contradict any T around it. Everything near it stays rigorous, and its consequences
   are worked out honestly — the Astrophage rule.
4. Record the nearest T, EG or S version beside it, so the distance stepped is known.
5. Simplifications made for reader clarity are a small sub-type (**L, clarity**). Log them too.

Anything on a skill's cut list can enter the story only as a License under these rules. When the
story wants one, do not refuse: call it an L, give the nearest plausible version, log it, and
keep everything around it honest.

## Two tests

- **Thorne test:** is it allowed by what is actually known? T, EG and S pass. L fails by
  definition.
- **Tyson test:** would an informed nitpicker be thrown out of the story, or nod and keep
  reading? Everything must pass this, L included. A License passes when the story declares it
  through its own logic, keeps it consistent every time it appears, and never uses it to dodge a
  problem the characters should have had to solve.

## Using this while writing

- **Label as you invent.** A new device, law, price, disease or institution gets a label the
  moment it enters the brief, the bible or the script. An unlabelled invention is the one that
  drifts.
- **Keep a license log in the book's canon** (`brief.md` for the room): each L, the story reason
  for it, the nearest honest version, and where it first appears. A license nobody wrote down
  becomes an inconsistency three chapters later.
- **A license is a promise, not a free pass.** Once declared, it behaves the same way every time.
  If a page needs it to behave differently, that is a second license, and it needs its own entry.
- **Never solve a scene with an unlogged invention.** If the characters are stuck and the way out
  is something the world has not established, the way out is wrong. Reach for T and EG material
  first; that is what the worldbuilding skills are full of.
- **Numbers date fastest.** Prices, exchange rates, deployments, laws and company names move
  month to month. Each worldbuilding skill states its own baseline date and says when to check;
  treat a specific figure as T only if it has been confirmed, otherwise write around it.
- **Plausible is not the same as grim.** These rules constrain how the world works, not what the
  story is about. A romance, a comedy or a children's book is as bound by them as a thriller, and
  none of them is obliged to be bleak.

## Reviewing a page against these rules

1. What does this page invent? List each element.
2. What label does each carry, and is it written down anywhere?
3. Does any L contradict a T beside it?
4. Does the page use an L to skip work the characters should have done?
5. Would the informed reader nod, or stop?

A page fails only on 3, 4 or 5. Unlabelled but plausible material is a bookkeeping problem:
label it and move on.
