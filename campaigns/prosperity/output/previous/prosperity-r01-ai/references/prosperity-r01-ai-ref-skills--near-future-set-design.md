# Near Future Set Design (Hard Sci-Fi)

The goal: a reader looks at the panel and believes it. Not because it is shiny, but because it looks used, wired, cooled, repaired, regulated, and lived in.

## The core rule: 80 / 15 / 5

- **80 percent is already here.** Buildings last 50 to 100 years. Furniture, roads, pipes, doors, and clothes change slowly. Most of any near future set is today, aged.
- **15 percent is today's emerging tech gone boring.** Whatever is a demo or a luxury now is cheap, scuffed, and taken for granted. It has stickers on it.
- **5 percent is genuinely new.** One or two things per set. These carry the story's speculative idea, so they get the design attention.

The future arrives as a layer on top of the past, never as a replacement. Retrofit beats new build. The 2060 apartment is a 1990 building with 2040 windows and a 2058 appliance.

**This is a design kit, not a plot.** It describes how to make a built world believable. Do not steer toward any particular storyline, theme, protagonist, or political reading, and do not assume the writer wants conflict, villains, or a message. Serve whatever story the writer brings: a romance, a comedy, a procedural, a family saga, a thriller, a children's book. Offer options across the range rather than a favorite, and use only the parts that the writer's story touches.

## Plausibility labels

Everything here is labelled with the vocabulary defined in `campaigns/<campaign>/rules/hard-sf-rules.md`: **T** truth,
**EG** educated guess, **S** speculation, **L** license, and **Cut** for what does not fit. The
rules for a license, the license log and the Thorne and Tyson tests are in that file; read it
before inventing anything the story then has to live with.

Here the "science" is physics, engineering, economics, and how built things actually age.

## Inputs to pin down first

Ask only what is missing and matters. Default the rest and say so.

1. **Year or distance out** (10, 25, 50 years). Sets how much of the 15 and 5 exists.
2. **Place and climate.** Future design is local. Lagos, Rotterdam, Phoenix, and low Earth orbit solve different problems.
3. **Wealth and who owns it.** Rich, poor, corporate, state, informal. Same tech looks different at every level.
4. **The speculative premise.** What one or two things changed in this world? Everything else follows from those.
5. **Story function.** What happens here and what should the reader feel?
6. **Medium.** Comic panel, film set, game level. Changes how much detail is readable.

## The plausibility pass

Run every set, prop, and vehicle through these. Each answer becomes a visible detail.

| Question | What it puts on the page |
|---|---|
| **Where does the power come from?** | Panels, cables, battery cabinets, charge ports, fuel lines, meters |
| **Where does the heat go?** | Vents, radiators, fins, fans, heat pumps, condensation, warm spots where people gather. In vacuum, large radiator panels are mandatory and usually bigger than the solar arrays |
| **How does data get in and out?** | Antennas, fiber conduit, line of sight dishes, dead zones, physical ports for secure areas |
| **How is it maintained?** | Access panels, fasteners you can reach, service labels, wear on the panels opened most, a toolbox nearby, a part that does not match |
| **Who built it and for how much?** | Lowest bidder finishes, standard parts, visible cost cutting. Bespoke only where someone rich or military paid |
| **Who regulates it?** | Warning labels, inspection tags, serial plates, exit signs, handrails, hazard stripes, multilingual signage |
| **How do humans actually use it?** | Handholds, scuffs at hand and foot height, chairs, coffee, tape, handwritten notes, personal items, workarounds |
| **What does it replace, and where is the old one?** | Dead mounts, painted over ports, abandoned conduit, the legacy system still running in the corner |
| **How does it fail?** | Backup systems, manual overrides, fire suppression, the cracked one, the out of order sign |
| **What does it weigh and what holds it up?** | Structure, supports, floor loading. No floating slabs without visible means |

If a design element cannot answer power, heat, and maintenance, cut it or redesign it.

## Hard limits (do not break without a story reason)

- No artificial gravity plates. Gravity in space comes from spin (needs large radius, roughly 100 m or more for comfort at 1 to 2 rpm) or thrust. Otherwise people float and everything is tethered, velcroed, or netted.
- No faster than light anything. Comms have delay: about 1.3 seconds to the Moon, 4 to 24 minutes to Mars.
- No free floating volumetric holograms in open air. Plausible substitutes: AR glasses or contacts, transparent OLED or microLED panels, projection on surfaces, light field displays in a box, fog or particle screens in controlled rooms.
- No energy shields, tractor beams, or teleporters.
- Rockets are mostly propellant tank. Spacecraft have radiators. Nothing is streamlined unless it enters atmosphere.
- Space interiors are cramped, noisy, and cluttered. Every surface is storage. Windows are small and rare.
- Batteries and power density improve slowly. Big power needs big hardware.
- Humanoid robots are slower, quieter, and less capable than people expect. Most robots are not humanoid: arms on rails, wheeled carts, drones, quadrupeds.
- Flying cars are loud, energy hungry, and regulated. Plausible as air taxis on fixed routes from rooftop pads, not as street traffic.
- Weather, dust, salt, UV, and pigeons affect everything outdoors.
- Medicine: better sensors, gene and cell therapies, printed tissue are fair. Instant healing is not.
- AI is everywhere and mostly invisible. It shows up as the absence of things (no cashiers, no steering wheels, no keyboards in some places) and the presence of sensors, not as glowing brains.

If the story requires breaking one, that is a **License**. Break as few as possible, ideally exactly one, make it the premise, log it, and keep everything around it rigorous. Interstellar and Project Hail Mary are the case studies: one declared leap each, surrounded by honest engineering.

## Near future trends to extrapolate (the 15 percent)

Pick what fits the place and wealth level. Do not use all of it.

**Energy:** solar on every surface that faces the sun, including facades, fences, and parking canopies. Home and neighborhood battery cabinets. Heat pumps replacing furnaces. EV chargers retrofitted everywhere, cables across sidewalks in poor areas. Microgrids. Small modular reactors behind serious fencing. Dead gas stations repurposed.

**Climate adaptation:** sea walls, raised entrances, flood gates on subways, buildings on stilts. External shading, white and reflective roofs, misting, shade sails, night markets in hot cities. Water capture, cisterns, gray water pipes in purple. Fire breaks, ember proof vents, air filtration, clean air shelters. Abandoned or uninsurable zones. Climate migrants and the informal housing that follows.

**Mobility:** autonomous vehicles with sensor pods (lidar bumps, camera clusters, cleaning nozzles), interiors that face inward. Dedicated lanes and pickup zones, fewer parking lots. E-bikes, cargo bikes, and scooters in huge numbers. Delivery robots on sidewalks, drone landing pads and lockers, drone corridors with noise complaints. Electric air taxis at vertiports. High speed rail where governments function.

**Built environment:** mass timber towers, 3D printed concrete with visible layer lines, modular and prefab units, adaptive reuse (malls as clinics, offices as housing, parking garages as farms or server halls). Dynamic glass. Vertical farms in windowless boxes with magenta light leaking out. Data centers as the new factories: blank, huge, humming, heavily cooled.

**Interfaces:** screens get thinner, cheaper, and more disposable. E-paper signage and labels. AR overlays for those wearing glasses (decide how to show this: reader sees it only in POV panels, or as faint ghosted graphics). Voice and gesture in private, touch in public. Physical buttons and knobs survive anywhere safety matters (vehicles, medical, industrial, space). Fewer keyboards, not zero.

**Surveillance and identity:** camera domes, sensor masts, face and gait recognition, license plate readers, drones overhead. Counter measures: masks, hats, makeup, IR LEDs, stickers over lenses. Biometric gates. Checkpoints that are just a painted line and a sensor arch.

**Manufacturing and objects:** local printing and CNC shops, repair stalls, right to repair culture next to sealed subscription hardware. Objects that are generative design (organic lattice structures) where weight matters, and plain boxes where it does not. QR or successor codes on everything. Packaging is compostable fiber, not glossy plastic.

**Bodies and health:** wearables shrink into patches, rings, earbuds, and clothing. Continuous glucose style monitors for everything. Exoskeletons for warehouse, construction, and elder care, looking like braces and straps, not armor. Prosthetics that are excellent and visibly mechanical by choice. Clinic kiosks in pharmacies. Telepresence rigs.

**Work and home:** homes have a work nook with good lighting and acoustic panels. Robots in homes are appliances: vacuums, mowers, window cleaners, maybe a slow mobile arm in rich or elder care homes. Kitchens still have knives and stoves. Beds are still beds.

**Space (near future):** inflatable and rigid modules, commercial stations with brand livery, lunar surface habitats buried under regolith for radiation, pressurized rovers, suit ports, dust everywhere on the Moon (sharp, clinging, gray on everything). Mars habitats mostly underground or bermed. Hydroponics are cramped racks, not gardens. Cargo bags strapped to every wall. Laptops velcroed to surfaces. Color coded handrails. Duct tape.

## Wealth changes the look

| Level | How the future shows up |
|---|---|
| **Ultra rich** | Tech disappears. Natural materials, space, quiet, clean air, real plants, human staff. The luxury is absence of screens and presence of people |
| **Corporate** | Clean, branded, modular, surveilled. Everything is a subscription. Furniture on wheels |
| **Middle** | Mixed generations of tech, some of it nagging for updates. Retrofits. One nice thing, lots of adequate things |
| **Poor / informal** | The most visibly futuristic, because everything is exposed: solar panels wired to car batteries, salvaged EV packs, mesh network antennas, printed repairs, five generations of tech bolted together. Ingenuity on display |
| **State / military** | Overbuilt, standardized, labeled, a generation behind consumer tech in interface and ahead in hardware. Olive, gray, hazard yellow |
| **Industrial** | Function only. Safety colors, cages, lockout tags, robots behind fences, humans in hi vis with exoskeleton braces |

## Layering and age

Every set gets a history. Decide:

- **Build date** of the structure and its original purpose
- **Two or three retrofits** with rough dates, each leaving scars (conduit on the surface, mismatched panels, patched holes)
- **Current occupant's layer:** personal objects, mess, workarounds, decor
- **Wear pattern:** where hands, feet, carts, and weather hit it
- **What is broken right now**

Dirt, dust, stains, sun fade, stickers, graffiti, cable ties, and tape do more for believability than any piece of invented tech.

## Design language

- **Form follows constraint.** Shape things by what they must do, fit in, ship in, and survive. Shipping container dimensions, door widths, and rocket fairing diameters drive real shapes.
- **Standard parts.** Real engineering reuses connectors, extrusions, fasteners, rack units. Repeat a few part families across the world and it feels manufactured.
- **Graphics sell reality.** Labels, arrows, hazard diamonds, serial numbers, inspection dates, brand logos, regulatory marks, multiple languages. Invent 3 to 5 fictional companies and agencies and put their marks everywhere.
- **Color with purpose.** Neutral base, then color coding for function: safety, wayfinding, pipes, ownership. Avoid default sci-fi cyan and neon unless there is a reason the light is that color.
- **Lighting from real sources.** LEDs are cheap so light is everywhere, often too cool and too bright in cheap places, warm and hidden in rich ones. Screens and indicator lights are motivated sources. Daylight still matters.
- **Negative cliche list.** Avoid unless justified: floating holograms, everything white and curved, everything rain and neon, glowing blue lines on surfaces, transparent phones, skull sized robots with one red eye, random greebles with no function, consoles with a hundred unlabeled lights.

## Output: location sheet

Use this format unless the user asks for something else.

```
LOCATION: name
When / where: year, city or body, climate
Owner and wealth level:
Story function and mood:

HISTORY
Built: year, original purpose
Retrofits: what, roughly when, scars left
Current layer: who is here now, what they added

THE 80 (familiar): 3 to 5 things recognizably from today, aged
THE 15 (extrapolated): 3 to 5 current technologies made ordinary
THE 5 (new): 1 to 2 things, tied to the story premise

SYSTEMS (visible evidence)
Power:
Heat / air:
Data:
Water / waste:
Safety / regulation:
Maintenance:

HUMAN TRACES: wear, mess, workarounds, personal items, what is broken
MATERIALS AND PALETTE:
LIGHT SOURCES:
GRAPHICS: signage, labels, brands, languages
SOUND AND SMELL (for writers):
KEY PROPS: 3 to 6, each with one line on how it works
CAMERA NOTES: 2 to 3 angles or details that sell the place fast
PLAUSIBILITY NOTES: label notable elements T, EG, S, or L. For anything stretched, give the real world basis. For any L, give the story reason and the nearest plausible version
```

For props and vehicles, use a short version: purpose, maker, age, power, heat, controls, wear, markings, failure mode.

**Image prompts.** When asked, write prompts that lead with the real world base ("1980s concrete housing block, retrofitted"), then the layers, then specific functional details, lighting from motivated sources, and wear. Name materials. Include negatives: no holograms, no neon glow lines, no floating objects, no pristine surfaces. Keep a consistent world kit (brands, colors, part families) across prompts.

**Plausibility review.** When the user brings their own design, run the plausibility pass and hard limits, label each element T, EG, S, L, or Cut, apply the Thorne and Tyson tests, say what works first, then list issues with a concrete fix for each. If something is impossible but the story needs it, say so plainly and suggest how to isolate it as the one allowed break.

**License log:** a running list for the project. For each L: what it is, the story reason, the nearest T, EG, or S version, what must stay rigorous around it, and whether it is the premise, a small license, or L clarity. Show the log whenever a plausibility review is given, and warn when the count is getting high.

## Reference touchstones

Look at these for the approach, not to copy:

- *Children of Men* (the 80 percent rule done perfectly, future as layer on decay)
- *Her* (soft, warm, tech that disappears)
- *The Expanse* (spin and thrust gravity, radiators, cramped ships, class shown through set)
- *The Martian*, *Moon*, *Gravity*, *For All Mankind* (NASA grounded hardware)
- *Ex Machina*, *Devs* (wealth as absence of visible tech)
- *Black Mirror*, *Years and Years* (one change, otherwise today)
- *Blade Runner 2049*, *Minority Report* (useful, but both drift past hard. Minority Report's production used a futurist think tank, which is the right method)
- *Alien* (Ron Cobb's working class industrial ships and his semiotic standard icon set)
- Designers and artists: Syd Mead (function first futurism), Ron Cobb, Chris Foss for scale, Simon Stalenhag (future tech in mundane landscapes), Maciej Rebisz and Mac Rebisz style grounded space art, NASA and ESA concept art, Foster + Partners and BIG lunar habitat studies
- Writers for worldbuilding logic: Kim Stanley Robinson (*The Ministry for the Future*, *Red Mars*, *New York 2140*), Andy Weir, Paolo Bacigalupi, William Gibson's Blue Ant and Jackpot books, Malka Older, Ted Chiang
- Method: design fiction (Julian Bleecker, Near Future Laboratory), Stuart Candy's experiential futures, the futures cone (possible, plausible, probable, preferable). Aim for plausible to probable.
