# Andean Hard Rock Futures

The rock side of the triangle. Unlike lithium brine, this is ore you can blast, carry, steal, and sell, so the human dynamics are different and rougher. Uses the same plausibility labels as the companion skills.

**"Lithium triangle" is only the name of the region here.** This skill is not about lithium. Treat lithium as one activity among many (copper, silver, tin, gold, borates, herding, farming, tourism, astronomy, trade, cities) and bring it in only when the writer's story does.

**This is a setting kit, not a plot.** It describes an environment, its constraints, and categories of things that can happen there. Do not steer toward any particular storyline, theme, protagonist, or political reading, and do not assume the writer wants conflict, villains, or a message. Serve whatever story the writer brings: a romance, a comedy, a procedural, a family saga, a thriller, a children's book. Offer options across the range rather than a favorite, and use only the parts that the writer's story touches.

## Plausibility labels

Everything here is labelled with the vocabulary defined in `rules/hard-sf-rules.md`: **T** truth,
**EG** educated guess, **S** speculation, **L** license, and **Cut** for what does not fit. The
rules for a license, the license log and the Thorne and Tyson tests are in that file; read it
before inventing anything the story then has to live with.

Here the "science" is the region's physics, law, economics, and politics.
Baseline is September 2026. Search to confirm any specific company, law, or price before treating it as fact.

## First, the honest correction on rare earths

The lithium triangle is **not** a rare earth region. Do not set a rare earth mine on the Puna or the Atacama and call it realistic.

Where South American rare earths actually are:
- **Brazil** is the real story: ionic clay and hard rock deposits in Goiás and Minas Gerais, already producing, with heavy US financing.
- **Chile**: ionic clays near Penco in the wet, forested south central coast (Aclara's project, fought by locals for years, in permitting). Also possible recovery from old iron and copper tailings in the Norte Chico, and minor showings in the far north. None of it is in the salar country.
- **Bolivia**: Cerro Manomó, a rare earth and thorium carbonatite in the eastern lowlands near Brazil. Explored, not mined. Wrong side of the country.
- **Argentina**: small occurrences (San Luis, Santiago del Estero, thorium and rare earth veins in the Puna). Exploration stage at best.

Plausible ways to get rare earths into a triangle story:
- **Tailings reprocessing** (EG to S): a company reworks a century of copper or iron tailings for rare earths, cobalt, and leftover copper. Dusty, chemical, cheaper than a new mine, and it disturbs waste everyone wanted left alone.
- **A speculative junior** (T pattern): drills a Puna thorium and rare earth vein, hypes it to investors during a price spike, never builds. The camp and the promises remain.
- **Separation plant, not a mine** (S): rare earth value is in chemical separation, which China dominates. A Western or Chinese backed separation plant near a Chilean port, fed by Brazilian or Chilean concentrate, is more plausible than a mine in the desert. It brings acid, solvents, and low level radioactive waste (thorium), which is the local fight.
- **Move the story**: if rare earths are the point, set those scenes in Goiás, Penco, or the Bolivian lowlands and connect them to the triangle by ownership or logistics.

What the AI boom really pulls out of this region is below, and it is a better story than rare earths anyway.

## What the AI boom needs and what this ground gives

Data centers, chips, optics, power, and cooling. Tier is for "this region supplies it."

| Material | Why AI needs it | Where it comes from here | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Copper** | Power distribution, transformers, busbars, cabling, cooling, grid build out. Tens of tonnes per megawatt of data center, plus the grid behind it | Chile is the world's largest producer: Chuquicamata, Escondida, Collahuasi, and more. Argentina's San Juan projects (Los Azules, Vicuña district) coming. The dominant fact of the region | T |
| **Silver** | Contacts, connectors, solder alloys, solar cells that power the data centers | Potosí and San Cristóbal (Bolivia), Puna district (Jujuy), many Chilean byproducts | T |
| **Tin** | Solder. Every circuit board on Earth | Bolivia: Huanuni, Colquiri, Potosí cooperatives. Southern Peru next door | T |
| **Indium** | Indium phosphide lasers for optical links between AI chips and racks, display coatings | Bolivia's zinc, tin, and silver ores are among the richest in indium in the world. Mostly leaves the country unpaid for inside zinc concentrate, recovered by foreign smelters. Mallku Khota, a big silver, indium, and gallium deposit, was nationalized in 2012 after violent community conflict and has sat undeveloped since | T |
| **Gallium, germanium** | Power electronics (gallium nitride), RF chips, fiber optics, infrared | Trace byproducts in the same zinc and polymetallic ores. China's export controls since 2023 made them strategic. Recoverable only if someone builds refining | EG |
| **Antimony** | Semiconductors, flame retardants in cables and boards, military uses | Bolivia is a historic top producer, mostly small mines and cooperatives in Potosí and Oruro. Chinese export limits in 2024 spiked the price | T |
| **Tungsten** | Chip interconnects, tooling | Bolivia, small scale and cooperative | T |
| **Bismuth** | Lead free solders, thermoelectrics | Bolivia (Tasna), historic | T |
| **Molybdenum, rhenium** | Alloys, turbines, some chip layers. Chile is the top rhenium source | Byproducts of Chilean copper | T |
| **Tellurium, selenium** | Thermoelectric cooling, thin film solar | Byproducts of copper refining sludge | T to EG |
| **Borates** | Boron is in every neodymium magnet, plus specialty glass and fiberglass | Dug from salar margins across the Puna and Bolivia, often by small operators | T |
| **Gold** | Bonding wire and plating, but mostly money | Everywhere. Drives the illegal mining economy | T |
| **Zinc, lead** | Carrier ores for the byproducts above | Bolivia, Jujuy (Aguilar, now closed) | T |
| **Cobalt** | Some batteries, alloys | Old cobalt district and tailings in Chile's Atacama region. Reprocessing proposals | EG to S |
| **Tantalum, beryllium** | Capacitors, thermal parts | Pegmatites in Argentina's sierras (San Luis, Catamarca). Tiny | S |
| **Iodine, nitrates** | Niche chemicals | Chile's Atacama caliche. World's main iodine source | T |
| **Rare earths** | Magnets in drives, fans, robots; polishing slurries; fiber amplifiers | See correction above | S here |

Key idea for stories: the strategic metals (indium, gallium, germanium, tellurium, rhenium) are **byproducts**. Nobody mines them directly. They hide in copper, zinc, and tin ore, and whoever owns the smelter or refinery captures them. The miner often is not paid for them at all. That invisible value leaving the country in a concentrate truck is a real grievance and a strong plot engine.

## Physical and human facts

- **You can carry it.** A backpack of high grade tin, silver, or gold ore is worth stealing. A truck of copper cathode is worth hijacking. This is what makes hard rock socially different from brine.
- **Three scales coexist.** Mega open pits run by multinationals and Codelco (Chile, Argentina). State mines run by Comibol (Bolivia). Cooperatives and informal miners, from a few men to thousands, working old veins by hand (Bolivia most of all).
- **Altitude and age.** Mines at 4,000 to 5,000 m. Many districts have been worked for 500 years. Cerro Rico above Potosí is so hollowed that its summit is caving in, and people still work inside it.
- **Tailings are everywhere.** Dams, dumps, and abandoned piles from every era, some above towns, many with acid drainage, arsenic, lead, and cadmium. Seismic country. Dry stacked and filtered tailings are replacing wet dams at big mines; old ones stay where they are.
- **Smelters and refineries are the chokepoints.** Bolivia has the Vinto tin smelter and a long unfinished dream of a zinc refinery. Chile's copper smelters are old and dirty; much concentrate ships to Asia. Arsenic in the ore and sulfur dioxide in the air define smelter towns.
- **Water.** Copper uses far more than lithium. Chile's big mines have moved to desalinated seawater pumped uphill. Bolivia's and Argentina's cannot. Rivers like the Pilcomayo already carry centuries of mine waste downstream to farming and indigenous communities.
- **Glaciers.** Argentina's glacier law restricts mining near ice and periglacial ground; the big San Juan copper projects sit right in that fight, and the national government has pushed to loosen it.

## Baseline 2026 (T)

**Chile.** Mature, corporate, heavily automated copper sector. Autonomous haul trucks and remote operations centers already run. Codelco is state owned and strained by debt, aging pits, and the shift underground at Chuquicamata. Strong unions; strikes matter. Communities litigate. Organized crime has arrived in the north: armed robbery of copper cathode trains and trucks, extortion, and transnational gangs in the mining cities. A wave of data center building near Santiago has already triggered court fights over water.

**Argentina.** Provinces own the minerals. The RIGI regime gives big projects 30 years of fixed tax and currency rules. BHP, Lundin, Glencore, McEwen, First Quantum and others are lined up on giant copper deposits in San Juan, Catamarca, and Salta. Little large scale mining history in some of these provinces, thin regulators, strong governors. The 2015 Veladero cyanide spill is the memory everyone invokes. Announced hyperscale data center plans in Patagonia tie the AI story directly to the country.

**Bolivia.** Mining cooperatives (well over a hundred thousand members) are a political bloc that governments fear. They pay little tax, work without much safety or environmental control, and hold concessions across the old tin and silver belt. Children and teenagers work in and around the Potosí mines. **Jukus**, armed ore thieves, raid the state mine at Huanuni and others at night, sometimes in gangs of dozens, and there have been gun and dynamite battles with soldiers and police. Cooperative miners killed a vice minister in 2016 during a blockade. In 2006, cooperative and state miners fought each other at Huanuni and sixteen died. Gold mining by cooperatives with Chinese and Colombian partners has spread through the country's rivers with mercury and violence. The Paz government is courting Western investment, and the US is signing minerals agreements across the region, but the constitution, the cooperatives, and regional civic movements all constrain what can be promised.

**Geopolitics.** China restricted exports of gallium, germanium, antimony, and rare earth technology. The US answered with financing, stockpiles, and minerals deals across the hemisphere. The IEA now flags gallium, germanium, indium, and antimony as the under watched risks for semiconductors and AI. Both powers are shopping in the Andes. Chinese buyers dominate Bolivian concentrate purchasing.

## Drivers

1. **AI and electrification demand** pushes copper hardest and makes minor metals suddenly strategic.
2. **Export controls and stockpiling** turn obscure byproducts into national security items overnight. Prices for antimony or gallium can go up five times in a year, then collapse.
3. **Who builds the refinery.** Value and control sit in smelting and separation. Expect fights over building plants in country, and over the pollution when they are built.
4. **State capacity.** Strong in Chile, provincial and personal in Argentina, negotiated with armed interest groups in Bolivia.
5. **Automation.** Mega mines need fewer people every year. Cooperatives absorb the people the formal sector sheds.
6. **Water, glaciers, tailings.** The physical limits and the legal weapons.
7. **Crime.** Gold launders money. Ore, cathodes, fuel, explosives, and acids all have black markets.

## Technology (T to EG)

- Autonomous haul trucks, drills, and trains. Remote operations centers hundreds of km away. Tele operated underground loaders. Workers on 7x7 or 4x3 rosters, flown or bussed in.
- Underground block caving replacing exhausted open pits: giant engineered cave zones, subsidence craters on the surface, seismic monitoring.
- Sensor based ore sorting, bulk leaching of low grade ore and old dumps, bioleaching. Chloride leaching for sulfides. Reprocessing of tailings for copper, cobalt, rare earths, and minor metals.
- Seawater desalination and 150 to 200 km pipelines to mines at altitude. Solar and wind power contracts, battery storage, trolley assist haul roads, first hydrogen or battery trucks.
- Filtered, dry stacked tailings at new mines. Satellite radar and fiber optic monitoring on dams.
- AI used in the mine itself: geology models, predictive maintenance, autonomous dispatch, drone survey, computer vision for safety and theft. The mine that feeds AI is run by it.
- Traceability: isotopic and chemical fingerprinting of concentrates, digital passports, audits. Also the workaround industry: blending, relabeling, and laundering cooperative or stolen ore into legal exports (T for gold, EG for other metals).
- At the cooperative end: jackhammers, dynamite, coca, carbide or LED lamps, hand sorting by women (palliris) on the dumps, small ball mills, mercury for gold. Cheap Chinese equipment and a few drones. The gap between the two ends of the industry is the visual story.

## Conflict: what is plausible here

More violent than the brine story, but still not warlord country.

- **Ore theft at scale** (T Bolivia, EG spreading): juku gangs with inside help, night raids, shootouts, soldiers posted at mine mouths, stolen ore sold to buyers who do not ask. When tin, silver, or antimony prices spike, it intensifies.
- **Cooperative versus state, company, or each other** (T): occupations of veins and concessions, dynamite, road blockades that shut the country, deaths in clashes. Cooperatives invading a foreign or state concession during a price boom is routine.
- **Avasallamiento** (T): Bolivia's word for armed seizure of a mine or land by a rival group. It is a legal category because it is common.
- **Cathode and concentrate hijacking** (T Chile): organized crews, tracking jammers, insider information, export through corrupt brokers.
- **Illegal gold frontier** (T in Bolivia's lowlands and Peru, S moving onto the altiplano): armed groups controlling river stretches or hillsides, mercury, trafficking, forced labor, killings of guards and rivals. Southern Peru's mine wars, where gangs have massacred security workers in tunnels, are the nearby precedent.
- **Community blockades and court cases** (T) over water, dust, tailings, glaciers, and consultation. Same pattern as the lithium skill.
- **Tailings failure** (EG): a dam breach or a slow leak after an earthquake or flood. Brazil's disasters are the template. In a story, this is the plausible catastrophe.
- **Smelter towns** (T): arsenic, lead, and sulfur dioxide exposure, children with blood lead levels, company denial, closure threats that pit jobs against health.
- **Strikes** (T): Chilean copper unions can move the world price. Subcontracted workers are the angrier group.
- **State of emergency and militarized mine zones** (EG to S): soldiers guarding strategic mines and transport corridors, framed as fighting ore theft and organized crime, also used against protesters.
- **Cross border flows** (T): Bolivian and Peruvian ore, gold, and mercury move through informal crossings. Explosives and fuel move the other way.

## Foreign conglomerates, abandonment, and the vacuum

Life cycle, same as lithium but with a crucial difference at the end:

1. Junior or local firm stakes and drills. 2. Major buys it in a boom or a crash. 3. Build, or put on care and maintenance. 4. Flip, write down, or let the local shell go insolvent. 5. Vacuum.

**The difference: hard rock can be worked by hand.** When a formal operator leaves a vein deposit, people move in within weeks. This is where the conflict mineral trope from other regions comes closest to real.

Who fills it:

| Who | Where | How it plays | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cooperatives** | Bolivia | Occupy the workings, high grade the best veins, strip the plant, then lobby to legalize the takeover. Hundreds to thousands of members. Internally hierarchical: old partners profit, day laborers and teenagers take the risk | T |
| **Juku crews turned operators** | Bolivia | Thieves become the de facto miners of an abandoned level. Armed, territorial over tunnels, tied to ore buyers | T to EG |
| **Ore buyers and traders** (often foreign) | Bolivia, Peru border | Finance cooperatives with equipment and cash advances, take the ore at a discount, export it. The real power, rarely visible. Do not care who dug it | T |
| **Illegal gold networks** | Bolivian lowlands, spreading | Foreign financed dredges and pits behind a cooperative's legal front. Armed guards, mercury, trafficking | T |
| **Criminal group taxing the zone** | Peru now, Bolivia and northern Chile plausibly | Charges per tonne, per truck, per tunnel. Kills those who refuse. Does not run the mine, farms it | EG to S |
| **Provincial strongman and friends** | Argentina | Concession reverts, gets reassigned to a connected firm or the provincial company, abandoned camp becomes someone's asset | T to EG |
| **Security contractor** | Anywhere | Same character as the lithium skill: guards the gate, ends up owning the road | EG |
| **Scavengers** | Anywhere | Copper cable, steel, liners, vehicles, fuel, explosives magazines. The explosives are the dangerous part | T to EG |
| **State reoccupation** | Bolivia, Chile | Comibol or the army retakes a site, often after deaths, often temporarily | T |
| **Nobody** | Chile and Argentina mega pits | A large open pit cannot be worked by hand. It just sits: a fenced hole, a tailings dam nobody maintains, a town that empties | EG |

**Closest plausible thing to a warlord:** a cooperative boss in Bolivia's southwest or a gold zone. He controls a few thousand members, their votes, and their dynamite. He has a senator, a Chinese or local ore buyer who bankrolls him, a juku crew he denies knowing, and an arrangement with the police. He can shut a highway in a day. He holds no territory on a map and wears no uniform, but nothing moves in his district without him. That is T to EG, not speculative. The near future stretch (S) is that a criminal organization captures or replaces him, as has happened in Peru.

What stays **Cut**: a militia holding a Chilean or Argentine open pit; a breakaway mineral statelet; mercenary armies fighting over a deposit.

## Rights (T)

- Subsoil belongs to the state (Chile, Bolivia) or province (Argentina). Surface and indigenous titleholders do not own the ore.
- Chile: mining concessions are strong, court granted, nearly perpetual property. Hard for anyone to dislodge.
- Argentina: cheap to hold claims, easy to flip, easements can be imposed on surface owners, the province is regulator and partner.
- Bolivia: no private ownership of deposits, only contracts with the state. Cooperatives get preferential terms and are barred on paper from partnering with private capital, which they do anyway through disguised deals. Nationalization is a live threat and a campaign promise. Mallku Khota is the cautionary tale told on both sides.
- Byproduct metals are often not covered in contracts or royalties at all.
- Closure bonds exist in Chile, weakly in Argentina, barely in Bolivia. Historic liabilities belong to nobody.
- Consultation is a right, consent is not, and the gap is where the conflict lives.

## Places and texture

Real anchors: Calama and Chuquicamata (the old company town was evacuated and is being buried under waste rock), Antofagasta, Tocopilla, Diego de Almagro, El Salvador, Copiapó (Chile). Potosí and Cerro Rico, Llallagua and Siglo XX, Huanuni, Oruro, Vinto, San Cristóbal, Atocha, Tupiza, Uyuni's train graveyard (Bolivia). Abra Pampa and its lead legacy, Mina Pirquitas, El Aguilar, San Antonio de los Cobres, Andalgalá and its long blockade against the Agua Rica project, Jáchal and Iglesia near Veladero (Argentina).

Details that sell it: the Tío, the devil figure in every Bolivian mine entrance, fed coca, alcohol, and cigarettes. Miners' markets where dynamite, fuse, and ammonium nitrate are sold over the counter. Palliris picking through dumps. Cheeks full of coca. Llama sacrifice and blood on the mine mouth in August. Carnival in Oruro. Red and ochre tailings terraces above a town. A smelter stack plume at dawn. Pickup trucks with whip flags. A remote operations hall in Santiago that looks like air traffic control. Haul trucks with no cab. A Chinese ore buyer's walled yard with a scale and an assay lab. A company town with a bowling alley and a locked gate. A ghost nitrate town in the desert, which is what every mining town here knows it might become.

## Cut list

These fail the Thorne test for this setting. Any of them can still be used as a declared **License** under the rules above, never by accident.

- Rare earth mines on the salars or the Puna as a major industry.
- Warlords or militias holding industrial open pits in Chile or Argentina.
- Interstate war over minerals. Lawsuits, nationalizations, sanctions, and trade coercion yes.
- Foreign military occupation of mines. Financing, security cooperation, advisors, and pressure are the real forms.
- Fully unmanned mines. Fewer people, never zero.
- Cooperatives running high tech plants. They high grade and sell ore. They do not run refineries.
- Bolivia becoming a chip or magnet manufacturer.
- Minor metals as treasure. A tonne of indium is valuable, but it arrives as parts per million in zinc concentrate, not as ingots in a vault.
- Instant ecological apocalypse. The plausible horrors are a tailings dam failure, slow poisoning of a river, or a collapsing mountain, all with precedent.
- Communities or cooperatives as simple victims or villains. Cooperatives exploit their own laborers, fight for real needs, and wreck rivers, all at once.
- Evil master plans. Harm comes from shell companies, byproduct accounting, price swings, and nobody owning the cleanup.

## Output formats

Same as the lithium skill: **world brief**, **location sheet**, **faction sheet**, **plausibility review** with plausibility labels and nearest plausible substitute for anything cut, and **timeline**. Add one more:

**Supply chain trace:** follow one metal from rock to rack. Who digs it, who buys it, where it is smelted, who captures the byproduct, how it is laundered or certified, what chip, cable, or optic it ends up in, and who along the way never gets paid. Good spine for a story that connects a tunnel in Potosí to a data center.

**License log:** a running list for the project. For each L: what it is, the story reason, the nearest T, EG, or S version, what must stay rigorous around it, and whether it is the premise, a small license, or L clarity. Show the log whenever a plausibility review is given, and warn when the count is getting high.
