# Enclaves, Borders, and Gray Zones in the Lithium Triangle

Uses the same plausibility labels as the companion skills.

**"Lithium triangle" is only the name of the region here.** This skill is not about lithium. Treat lithium as one activity among many (copper, silver, tin, gold, borates, herding, farming, tourism, astronomy, trade, cities) and bring it in only when the writer's story does.

**This is a setting kit, not a plot.** It describes an environment, its constraints, and categories of things that can happen there. Do not steer toward any particular storyline, theme, protagonist, or political reading, and do not assume the writer wants conflict, villains, or a message. Serve whatever story the writer brings: a romance, a comedy, a procedural, a family saga, a thriller, a children's book. Offer options across the range rather than a favorite, and use only the parts that the writer's story touches.

## Plausibility labels

Everything here is labelled with the vocabulary defined in `rules/hard-sf-rules.md`: **T** truth,
**EG** educated guess, **S** speculation, **L** license, and **Cut** for what does not fit. The
rules for a license, the license log and the Thorne and Tyson tests are in that file; read it
before inventing anything the story then has to live with.

Here the "science" is the region's physics, law, economics, and politics.
Baseline is September 2026. Search before relying on any specific law, deployment, or dispute.

## The honest picture first

There is **no unclaimed land, no terra nullius, and no sovereign micro territory** here. Every border is set by treaty and mapped. If the story needs a place outside government, it will not be a gap on the map. It will be one of these:

1. A place the state has legally handed to someone else (autonomy, concession, treaty privilege, lease).
2. A place the state cannot be bothered or does not dare to govern (contraband towns, high passes).
3. A place where two states' attention does not quite meet (the space between border markers).
4. A place where a company, a community, or a boss is the only authority that shows up.

All four exist now. The near future makes them bigger, richer, and more contested.

## History that people still carry

This matters because characters will bring it up.

- **War of the Pacific (1879 to 1884).** Chile took the coast from Bolivia and the far south from Peru. Antofagasta, Calama, and San Pedro de Atacama were Bolivian. Bolivia became landlocked. It still has a navy, still marks a Day of the Sea every March, and its constitution still claims a right to the ocean.
- **1904 treaty.** Fixed the Chile and Bolivia border and gave Bolivia free transit through Chilean ports in perpetuity. Bolivia considers it signed under duress. Chile considers it closed.
- **Puna de Atacama.** The high plateau that is now Argentine lithium country was Bolivian, then Chilean held, then awarded mostly to Argentina by arbitration in 1899. Old families and indigenous communities predate all three flags and have relatives on every side.
- **The tripoint.** Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia meet on Cerro Zapaleri, a volcano at about 5,600 m, near the Jama pass road and Bolivia's flamingo lagoons reserve. Remote, cold, and a natural setting for anything involving three jurisdictions at once.
- **1970s mines.** Chile laid tens of thousands of landmines along its borders with Bolivia, Peru, and Argentina during Pinochet era war scares. It declared clearance complete in 2020. Floods moved some over the years, and rumors of stray mines persist (T as folklore, S as actual hazard).

## What is actually disputed (T)

| Dispute | Where | Status | Story use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Triángulo terrestre** | Chile and Peru coast near Arica | About 3.7 hectares of beach both claim after the 2014 court ruling on the sea border. Empty. Flares occasionally | The only literal "little disputed piece of land," but it is sand at sea level, outside the triangle |
| **Bolivia's sea claim** | Whole Chile and Bolivia relationship | ICJ ruled in 2018 that Chile has no duty to negotiate. Politically immortal | Background resentment, nationalist politics, leverage over ports |
| **Silala waters** | High border, Potosí and Antofagasta | ICJ 2022: an international watercourse, both sides largely agreed by the end | The template for future fights over shared rivers, aquifers, and mineral basin water |
| **Lauca River** | Far north | Old dispute over Chilean diversion, dormant | Same |
| **Border marker incidents** | Altiplano | Markers (hitos) are km apart. Both sides have detained the other's soldiers, customs officers, and police for crossing. Communities accuse each other of moving markers | Small, human scale international incidents. Very usable |
| **Southern ice field** | Patagonia, far away | A stretch of the Chile and Argentina border is still undefined | Not the triangle. Mention only as proof that undefined borders exist |

Shared salars and aquifers that straddle borders are not disputed today. As mineral and water values rise, expect legal and diplomatic disputes over who is draining whose side (EG to S). Expect them in courts and foreign ministries, with troops at posts as theater.

## Places the state does not really govern (T to EG)

**Contraband towns (Bolivia).** Challapata, Sabaya, Pisiga, and other altiplano towns on the routes from Chilean ports. Open markets for smuggled and stolen vehicles. Residents have ambushed and expelled customs police. Outsiders are watched. Community justice, including lynchings of suspected thieves, substitutes for courts. Not separatist, not lawless inside: tightly ordered by local rules, families, and trade unions of transporters. The state enters with force, takes casualties or makes a deal, and leaves.

**The crossings between the crossings.** Hundreds of km of open plateau with a handful of official passes (Jama, Sico, Socompa, San Francisco, Ollagüe, Colchane and Pisiga, Tambo Quemado, Villazón and La Quiaca). Everything else is tracks across salt and sand used by smugglers, migrants, and locals visiting relatives. Chile has dug anti vehicle trenches and deployed the army on its northern border since the migration crisis. People have died of cold and altitude crossing on foot.

**Border twin towns.** Villazón and La Quiaca, Bermejo and Aguas Blancas, Pisiga and Colchane. Porters carry goods across bridges and rivers all day in plain view. Whole economies depend on price gaps. Officials on both sides take a share. The direction of flow reverses when currencies do.

**Near future:** gangs taxing the routes and the migrants (EG); a contraband town that is richer and better armed than the provincial police and negotiates openly with the government (EG); drone and sensor lines along the Chilean border that push traffic to worse terrain (EG); a bust era mining camp absorbed into the smuggling network as a depot (EG).

## Legal self rule and community controlled territory (T)

**Bolivia.** The 2009 constitution allows indigenous autonomies with their own statutes and governments. Uru Chipaya, beside the Coipasa salt flat, is one. Others in the highlands have pursued it. Beyond formal autonomy, large areas are collectively titled indigenous territories (TCOs) such as Nor Lípez around Uyuni. They do not own the minerals but hold the surface, the consultation right, and the ability to block the road. Ayllus and their authorities run daily life, land allocation, and justice.

**Chile.** No autonomy, but real territorial control in practice: Atacameño communities co administer the Los Flamencos reserve, staff the gates, collect the entrance fees to the lagoons and valleys around San Pedro, and decide access. Indigenous Development Areas give a planning voice. Communities own water rights and land, and negotiate directly with mining companies.

**Argentina.** Community land titles, often incomplete or contested. The Salinas Grandes communities control entry to the salt flat, charge tourists, and have kept lithium companies out for over a decade by physically refusing access. Provinces resist anything resembling autonomy.

**Near future:** an autonomy or community federation that controls the only road, the water source, or the power line route, and bargains like a small state with companies and embassies (EG). Internal splits over who speaks for it (T). A state that revokes or hollows out autonomy when the minerals get valuable enough (EG to S). Communities on three sides of the tripoint coordinating across borders as one people (EG).

## Foreign and treaty enclaves (T)

- **Observatories.** The European Southern Observatory operates in Chile under an international agreement granting it diplomatic style privileges and immunities. ALMA sits on the Chajnantor plateau at 5,000 m directly above San Pedro de Atacama and the salar. Other foreign observatories dot the Atacama. They are fenced, internationally staffed, and protected by radio quiet and dark sky rules that restrict transmitters and lighting for many km around. That collides with mine communications, satellite internet, and drone traffic (T to EG conflict).
- **Bolivia's port rights.** Under the 1904 treaty Bolivia runs its own customs operations inside the Chilean ports of Arica and Antofagasta and moves cargo in free transit. A Bolivian bureaucratic island inside a Chilean port, regularly the subject of strikes, fees disputes, and nationalist speeches.
- **Bolivia Mar.** A strip of Peruvian beach near Ilo leased to Bolivia for 99 years in 1992 as a symbolic outlet to the sea. Mostly empty. A monument and not much else. Perpetually about to be developed.
- **Free trade zones.** Iquique's ZOFRI is the engine of the region's legal and illegal commerce: goods enter duty free and leave by truck for Bolivia, Paraguay, and the black market. Smaller zones exist in all three countries.
- **Chinese space station in Neuquén.** Outside the triangle, but the key precedent: a 50 year lease to a Chinese state agency with restricted Argentine access and years of controversy about what happens inside.
- **Chancay.** The Chinese built and operated megaport in Peru. Precedent for foreign control of the logistics end of the mineral chain.

## Corporate territory (T)

A large mine concession is an enclave in practice. The company controls the gate, the private road, the airstrip, the water, the power, the clinic, the cell tower, and the camp rules. Police enter by invitation or under an agreement. Herders and neighbors need permission to cross land their grandparents used. In Chile, mining concessions are very strong property. After closure or mothballing, the fence stays and the authority behind it thins out. See the security skill for who steps in.

## Border zone law (T, verify before use)

- All three countries treat a band along the border as a security zone with limits on foreign ownership. Bolivia's constitution bars foreigners from owning land or water within 50 km of the border, which covers much of the southwest salar country. Chile restricts border land ownership by nationals of neighboring countries. Argentina requires security clearance for foreign purchases in border zones and has loosened and re tightened rural land limits depending on the government.
- Result: foreign projects near borders run through state companies, provincial companies, local partners, long leases, and front men. Who really owns the land under a plant is a legitimate mystery plot.
- Militaries own or control large tracts near borders and sometimes lease or trade them.

## Near future zones (EG to S)

| Zone | How it works | Label |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical minerals special economic zone** | A province or national government ring fences a salar district with its own tax, customs, labor, and permitting rules, plus a dedicated security force. Argentina's 30 year stability regime is the first step | EG |
| **Bioceanic corridor customs regime** | Sealed trucks and trains run from Argentine and Bolivian plants to Chilean or Peruvian ports under a special transit status. Integrated border posts already exist at some passes. The corridor becomes a linear enclave with its own police and its own blockades | EG |
| **Sealed foreign compound** | A state linked project under a government to government deal: own guards, own staff housing, local police stay outside the wire, inspectors need appointments. Most plausible in Bolivia, and only until national politics turns on it | S |
| **Foreign operated port terminal or dry port** | A terminal in Antofagasta, Mejillones, or Ilo, or an inland rail hub, run by a foreign operator with priority for its own cargo | EG |
| **Data center and solar enclave** | Hyperscale campus in the desert with private power, private water pipeline, restricted airspace for drones, and a tax deal. The Atacama has the best solar resource on Earth | EG |
| **Charter city or private jurisdiction** | Proposed by investors on the Honduras model. Wins a law in one country during a market friendly government, faces court challenges and nationalist fury, gets repealed or frozen. Useful as a failed or besieged project, not a thriving one | S |
| **Security cooperation site** | A foreign military or law enforcement presence described as training, counter narcotics, or disaster response. Bolivia's constitution bans foreign bases outright, so there it would be advisors and equipment, not a facility | S |
| **Conservation lockup** | A lagoon or salar placed under international conservation funding with rangers and surveillance. Communities split between those employed by it and those fenced out | EG |
| **Autonomous indigenous mining district** | A community or autonomy secures the right to partner directly with a buyer, runs its own checkpoint, collects its own fees. Tolerated while it suits the state | S |
| **The strip between the markers** | A stretch of plateau where marker posts are far apart, maps disagree by a few hundred meters, neither army patrols, and smugglers, herders, and an abandoned exploration camp share the ground. Becomes an incident the day someone finds something valuable under it: ore, brine, water, or an archaeological site | S |

## Who is the authority, by place type

| Place | Who you actually answer to |
|---|---|
| Official pass | Customs, migration, gendarmes or police, agricultural inspectors, all underpaid at 4,000 m |
| Track across the salar | Nobody, until the people who use it notice you |
| Contraband town | Transport unions, founding families, neighborhood councils, the saint's fiesta committee |
| Indigenous territory | Assembly and rotating traditional authorities. Decisions by exhausting consensus |
| Mine concession | Site security manager, then the company, then the police they call |
| Mothballed concession | The man with the gate key |
| Observatory | International organization's site director. Host state at the perimeter |
| Treaty port area | A tangle of two countries' customs, port unions, and trucker associations |
| Free zone | The zone administrator, the importers' association, and whoever they pay |
| Sealed compound | Its own management, its embassy, and one ministry in the capital |
| Border twin town | Both municipalities, neither fully, and the porters' union most of all |

## Scene details

A boundary marker: a squat concrete or iron pyramid with a number, alone on a plain, the next one a speck on the horizon. A trench dug across a smugglers' track, already bridged with planks. A migration post that is two containers and a flag frozen stiff. Hundreds of used cars parked on a salt flat at dawn. A community checkpoint: a rope, a ledger, a woman in a bowler hat collecting the entrance fee. A sign in three languages at an observatory gate forbidding phones. Bolivian customs officers in their own shed inside a Chilean port. A laminated 1904 treaty article on a trucker's dashboard. A rusted "danger mines" sign nobody has dared to remove. Porters bent under refrigerators crossing a footbridge. The Bolivian navy drilling on Lake Titicaca. A flag raising on the Day of the Sea in a town that has never seen the sea.

## Cut list

These fail the Thorne test for this setting. Any of them can still be used as a declared **License** under the rules above, never by accident.

- Unclaimed land, a forgotten valley off the map, or a legal vacuum between borders.
- A breakaway republic, independent city state, or recognized micro nation.
- A lawless zone with no order at all. Places the state avoids are tightly governed by someone else.
- War to redraw borders or retake the coast. Rhetoric, lawsuits, port slowdowns, and border theater only.
- A foreign military base in Bolivia, or an openly acknowledged one anywhere in the triangle without a political firestorm.
- A thriving libertarian charter city. A stalled, sued, or besieged one is plausible.
- Permanently extraterritorial corporate land. Concessions feel like enclaves but remain under national law, and states remind companies of that when it suits them.
- Indigenous autonomy as full sovereignty over minerals. Surface, consultation, and the road, yes. Subsoil, no.
- Active minefields as a routine hazard. Cleared on paper, alive in memory and rumor.

## Output formats

Same as the companion skills (**world brief**, **location sheet**, **faction sheet**, **plausibility review**, **timeline**). Add:

**Jurisdiction stack:** for any place, list the layers from paper to practice. Whose flag, which province or department, which municipality, what special legal status (autonomy, concession, reserve, border zone, treaty privilege, lease, free zone), who holds surface, subsoil, and water, who patrols, who collects money, who settles disputes, and who a stranger needs permission from to stay the night.

**Border crossing sheet:** official or informal, altitude and season, who staffs it, what moves through in each direction, what it costs legally and otherwise, what can go wrong.

**License log:** a running list for the project. For each L: what it is, the story reason, the nearest T, EG, or S version, what must stay rigorous around it, and whether it is the premise, a small license, or L clarity. Show the log whenever a plausibility review is given, and warn when the count is getting high.
