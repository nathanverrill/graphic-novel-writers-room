# Prosperity Prologue — Story

## What the Showrunner Is Reaching For

**Explicit decision.** This is the four-page prologue, **“The Wall,”** set about 2070 and turning into Keel in 2043. It opens not on a dystopia but on where Alex ends up: an ordinary, comfortable, earned life. The ending should let the partner and backer see, quietly and visually, that the story ends in earned success, protected ownership, independent businesses licensing and competing, a business that serves people and makes money doing it, failure that taught something, and a debt paid back in full rather than cancelled. `(rules/decisions.md; rules/showrunner-rules.md)`

The prologue must communicate these ideas through what is seen on the wall, never through explanation. Nothing names the partner, the backer or the missions. No line explains a lesson. There is no co-op, collective or giveaway: the businesses are independent companies that license Alex’s patents and compete with one another and with the Houses. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The intended emotional movement is from quiet, recognizable prosperity to curiosity about the past, then to the first image of the debt and hardship that made the ending possible. The daughter’s questions guide the reader through Alex’s wall of evidence. Her final question leads to his first direct admission about the beginning, and the page turn into Chapter 1 is a visual and temporal reversal: the warm house becomes Keel’s rust and charcoal pump hall. `(rules/decisions.md; input/pitch.md)`

The central dramatic question of the prologue is: **How did this ordinary, earned life begin?** The answer is withheld until the final page: Alex began with a debt he had never borrowed. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The prologue is made for high-school readers who have no prior knowledge of Keel, Alpha, the Water Wars or the safeguards. This piece therefore establishes no additional background and does not assume the reader knows the larger story. It ends at the threshold of Chapter 1 rather than explaining the world beyond the transition. `(rules/reader.md; rules/showrunner-rules.md)`

## Premise

About 2070, Alex, now in his mid-40s, shows his approximately nine-year-old daughter a hallway wall covered with the visible record of his working life: patents, machines, news clippings, collaborators, a failed prototype, an unfinished sketch and a final account statement marked **PAID IN FULL · BALANCE 0 CREDITS**.

The daughter sees a life that looks like successful invention. Alex corrects that impression in stages. He made some things, fixed most, and borrowed ideas from people smarter than him. The most important object on the wall is not necessarily the most polished patent: it is a crude pump-bracket drawing that never became a patent. The drawing mattered because it solved a real problem, while patenting it would have added no necessary value. The story never supplies a technical, legal or financial explanation for that distinction. `(rules/decisions.md)`

Her admiration prompts him to look back at the beginning. He admits that the beginning was not fun: he owed **four hundred thousand credits he’d never borrowed**. This is Alex’s rounded conversational description of the exact **418,607-credit** debt balance present in Chapter 1. The statement on the wall records that same obligation after it is eventually repaid in full, leaving a zero-credit balance. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The sketch dissolves into Keel’s pump hall in 2043, where seventeen-year-old Alex has his palm flat on the pump. The pump repair itself is not the invention that makes Alex successful. It is the first visible example of his ability to fix real machinery under pressure, an ability that later becomes part of his invention and licensing work. `(rules/decisions.md)`

## Story So Far

### Sequence 1 — The earned life on the wall

**Material type: explicit decision.**

The prologue opens in about 2070, in a quiet, well-kept house on an ordinary street at warm evening light. The house has solar tiles, a vegetable garden and a ten-year-old car in the drive. It is comfortable but not flashy. This establishes the destination before the story turns toward the cost of reaching it. `(rules/decisions.md)`

Inside, the hallway wall is covered in framed patents, photographs of machines and news clippings. Alex is in his mid-40s, grey at the temples and wearing a plain sweater. He wears Adrian’s old steel watch on his left wrist. His daughter, about nine years old, stands on tiptoe in front of the wall. Alex is an independent inventor and repairer who owns or co-owns the patents represented on the wall, licensing them to separate manufacturing and deployment companies. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The wall is the prologue’s visual record of what Alex has built and what his work has become. It must show, without explanatory dialogue:

- successful, earned prosperity rather than a windfall;
- ownership made visible and protected;
- independent businesses licensing Alex’s patents;
- those businesses competing with one another and with the Houses;
- a business serving people while making money;
- a failure that taught something;
- a debt paid in full rather than cancelled. `(rules/decisions.md; rules/stakeholders.md)`

The story does not explain these themes. The reader infers them from the house, the framed material, the businesses and the account statement. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### Sequence 2 — Alex separates invention from ownership and collaboration

**Material type: explicit decision.**

The daughter’s question invites Alex to claim the entire wall. Instead, he gives a qualified answer: he made some things, fixed most, and borrowed ideas from people smarter than him. The photograph shows Alex with Lina, Suri, Jalen and Ren, his former lab collaborators. The wall also shows that Alex’s inventions have entered ordinary public life through other companies. The daughter recognizes one purifier from school. ### Sequence 3 — The drawing that never became a patent

**Material type: explicit decision.**

The daughter’s attention moves from framed public records to a crumpled, grease-stained sheet with a pencil sketch of a pump bracket. The loose sheet is mounted inside a mismatched frame. The frame is what the daughter identifies as different, while the paper remains visibly damaged and informal. It is marked by smudged fingerprints and has no patent number. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The daughter assumes that an important invention should have become a patent. Alex says that this one never needed to be. The reason remains implicit: the drawing mattered because it solved a real problem, while patenting it would have added no necessary value. The story never supplies a technical, legal or financial explanation. The sketch plants the visual link to the final transition: this same pump-bracket sketch matches the Chapter 1 opening repair. `(rules/decisions.md)`

Her next line treats inventing as something that must have been enjoyable. Alex does not answer immediately. He looks at the sketch, then down at Adrian’s watch, with his thumb covering its face. The gesture connects the drawing to the watch and makes the watch a private, partly concealed object before the prologue explains its significance. Adrian’s exact identity and relationship to Alex remain open. In the prologue, the watch signifies concealed history and continuity rather than a stated backstory. `(rules/decisions.md)`

## Character Movement

### Alex

At the start, Alex is seen as the owner of a comfortable, earned life and the person associated with the wall’s patents, machines and clippings. His daughter’s questions pressure him to qualify that image. He moves from allowing the wall to speak for itself, to acknowledging collaboration and repair, to revealing that the beginning was defined not by fun but by an unexplained debt. He is an independent inventor and repairer who owns or co-owns the patents represented on the wall and licenses them to separate manufacturing and deployment companies. `(rules/decisions.md)`

His present-day movement is not a completed confession. He gives one decisive fact—**four hundred thousand credits he’d never borrowed**—then the story leaves him behind and moves to his seventeen-year-old self. The rounded figure refers to the exact 418,607-credit Chapter 1 balance, and the statement on the wall records the same obligation after repayment. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### The daughter

She begins in open curiosity, standing on tiptoe and treating the wall as a record of her father’s achievements. Her questions move from broad ownership—“Did you make *all of these?””—to a familiar purifier, then to the odd sketch. Her belief that inventing must have been fun prompts Alex’s first correction about the beginning. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### The reader

The reader first sees the outcome as an ordinary, comfortable household. The wall then supplies evidence of patents, licensing, independent competition, collaboration, failure and repayment. The daughter’s questions make the reader inspect the wall with her. Alex’s final line reverses the emotional meaning of the display and creates the need to continue into Keel. `(rules/decisions.md)`

## Stakes

### Personal

Alex’s present-day life is comfortable, but the wall and his concealed watch show that the comfort has a history he does not initially volunteer. His past includes a debt of four hundred thousand credits that he says he never borrowed. This is the rounded form of the exact 418,607-credit balance present in Chapter 1. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### Relational

The daughter wants to understand what her father made and why the wall contains objects that do not look like patents. Alex must decide how much of the beginning to tell her. He answers her final question honestly but incompletely, giving her—and the reader—the first difficult fact rather than a full explanation. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### Financial and material

The ending visibly includes ownership, licensing, competition, failure and a final account balance of zero credits. The opening of the larger story is associated with an obligation of four hundred thousand credits, while the later Chapter 1 figure is 418,607 credits. These are the same debt: the prologue uses a rounded conversational figure, while Chapter 1 gives the exact balance. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The framed statement records that same obligation after it is eventually repaid in full. The creditor, terms, interest, collateral, payment schedule and mechanism by which the debt was repaid are not established in the prologue. `(rules/decisions.md)`

### Narrative

The final reveal creates the forward pull into Chapter 1. The reader now needs to know how a seventeen-year-old in Keel came to owe a large debt he had never borrowed, and how the pump-bracket sketch connects to that beginning. The repair itself is not the later invention that makes Alex successful; it is the first visible demonstration of the practical ability that later informs his invention and licensing work. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The repair is the first visible example of Alex fixing real machinery under pressure, not the later invention that makes him successful. `(rules/decisions.md)`

## Ending

The prologue ends on the full-width transition into Keel’s pump hall, not on an explanation of the debt. Seventeen-year-old Alex stands with his palm flat on the pump beneath the caption **“KEEL. 2043.”** Chapter 1 begins after this final panel; no Chapter 1 material is added here. `(rules/decisions.md; rules/showrunner-rules.md)`

The ending links the present and the past through three visual or factual continuities:

- Adrian’s old watch is on Alex’s wrist in the present and remains a continuity object for the larger story. Its significance in this prologue is concealed history and continuity; Adrian’s exact relationship to Alex remains unstated.
- The crumpled, loose pump-bracket sketch, mounted inside a mismatched frame, matches Chapter 1’s opening repair. It mattered because it solved a real problem; the story does not specify why patenting it would have added no necessary value.
- The prologue’s rounded debt figure of four hundred thousand credits links to Chapter 1’s exact figure of 418,607 credits. They are the same debt, and the framed **PAID IN FULL · BALANCE 0 CREDITS** statement records that obligation after eventual repayment. `(rules/decisions.md)`

The prologue does not explain Alpha, the safeguards or the artifacts. It does not show Bi11bot, TJ, Adrian or any other person besides Alex, his daughter and the four unnamed people in the framed photograph. `(rules/decisions.md; rules/showrunner-rules.md)`
