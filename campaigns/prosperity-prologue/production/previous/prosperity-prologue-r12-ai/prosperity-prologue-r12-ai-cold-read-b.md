### Pulled me forward
The transition on page 4 where the crude sketch on the wall turns into the actual rusty pump bracket in the flashback. That was a cool visual trick, and it finally got us out of that boring hallway and into a setting that looks like an actual story.

### I slowed down / skimmed
Pages 1 and 2. It’s literally just a dad and a kid looking at framed papers and receipts on a wall. It felt like a museum tour or an educational pamphlet about "how to be an inventor." I completely skimmed the stuff about the patents and the school water purifier. 

### I was confused
On page 3, Alex stares at his watch and covers it up. I don't know why the watch is important or why it has a dark screen that stays dark. It felt like a heavy, dramatic moment but I had zero context for it.

### Things I was expected to already know
- The watch. The art notes call it "Adrian's old steel watch," but as a reader just looking at the panels, no one has said the name Adrian. I just see a broken watch and feel like I'm supposed to know whose it is.
- "Keel" (Page 4). I assume it's the name of the factory or the city in the flashback, but I don't know what it actually is.

### Lines I read twice
"Because it never needed to be." (Page 3). It felt like a forced, overly dramatic riddle line about a piece of paper. 

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
Very easy to tell apart. It's just a middle-aged dad, a little girl, and then a younger teenage version of the dad. 

### I felt something
I felt a lot of dread on page 2 that this entire book was going to be a boring lecture about water conservation and school science projects. But when the flashback hit the dirty, greasy pump room on page 4, I felt a slight bit of relief that something might actually happen.

### I predicted
That the rest of the book is going to be one long flashback showing how teenage Alex invented a water machine to pay off his massive debt.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
A dad tells his daughter the story of how he survived water rationing and got out of debt by inventing a machine when he was a teenager.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 2. Looking at framed patents and a "paid in full" receipt is exactly the kind of boring homework reading I'd drop immediately if I wasn't forced to finish it.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
I don't *need* it, but page 4 is the first time I was slightly interested. The dystopian, greasy factory setting of "Keel" looks way cooler than the suburban hallway. I'd turn the page to see what he's doing in the factory, but I wouldn't spend my own money on a second issue yet.
