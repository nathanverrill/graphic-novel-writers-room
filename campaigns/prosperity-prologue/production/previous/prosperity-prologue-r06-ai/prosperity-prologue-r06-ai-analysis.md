The one hook that cuts against it, debt "I'd never borrowed," is told plainly and with no grievance, and the reader has already seen the "PAID IN FULL" statement twice before the line lands. The repayment comes before the injury, so the frame reads as integrity, not victimhood. No one is likely to stop reading over politics here. A teacher in a progressive district will find nothing partisan. A conservative parent will find nothing to flag.

**Does the book conclude, or leave a reader concluding, that free enterprise is bad?** No. pp.1–2 show an invention spreading through independent licensed shops, and pp.2 and 4 show a debt repaid in full.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like.** The wall is their curriculum shown, not told. Licensing to forty independent shops (p.1), a failed first version labelled "v1 - didn't work" (p.2 P3), credit given to colleagues ("Borrowed a few ideas from people smarter than me," p.2 P1), and a statement reading "PAID IN FULL" (p.2 P4). That last one echoes their founder's story of paying back debts he legally didn't have to. Profit and intellectual property sit on the wall with pride, which speaks to their October complaint about "altruism over capitalism." The pages are easy to follow: one room, two people, one cut. Nothing in them would embarrass them in front of a school board.

**What would bother them.** Water still isn't named in readable text. There's a "purifier" in a headline and a pump in the last panel, but the word "water," scarcity and contamination aren't legible on the page. The setting is "KEEL. 2043." and nothing more. The debt "I'd never borrowed" is a hook they'll want explained soon and correctly. "PHANTUM" may read as a typo. Lettering and art can't be judged from a script, and that was their first complaint last time.

**Likely verdict:** satisfied in part. They'd present these four pages. They'd hold final judgement until they see whether the debt is explained properly and whether water and the setting arrive fast.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like.** A man who failed ("v1 - didn't work"), owed money and paid it all ("PAID IN FULL"). That is his beer-hall story in miniature, and he lists exactly this among his likes. Alex's self-deprecation ("Made some. Fixed most. Borrowed a few ideas from people smarter than me") is close to his own voice: plain, joke at his own expense, no lecture. "Not in the beginning" (p.4 P1) is the kind of honest correction he'd enjoy. Nothing here is a windfall, a bailout or a giveaway. The wall says the success was earned.

**What would bother him.** Very little. The debt he "never borrowed" (p.4 P3) could tip toward victimhood, which he dislikes, but Alex says it flatly and the reader already knows he paid. That is his "don't play the victim, even if you have been victimized," acted out. He might want ownership made more concrete. The unpatented sketch "never needed to be" a patent (p.3 P2) is a little riddling and doesn't say why.

**Bi11bot:** not in these pages, so I can't judge his voice. There is no dog named for a Founder either. One inference: the old watch with the "small dark display window with no readout" (p.3 P4) may be where Bi11bot lives. If so, it's a quiet, promising introduction, a mentor who isn't talking yet.

**Likely verdict:** satisfied.

## A comic writer

- **The hook** is p.4 P3: "In the beginning, I owed four hundred thousand credits I'd never borrowed." It's a good line, delivered plainly, and it earns the cut to Keel. It arrives late, though. Three pages pass in a hallway before anything is at stake.
- **Page turns** are well placed. Page 1 (right) ends on "Did you make *all of these*?", which pulls you over. The turn from p.3 lands on a silent beat (Alex's thumb over the watch), and page 4 opens on the answer, "Not in the beginning." The hook line and the full-width match-cut sit at the bottom of a left page, so the turn after it should open straight into Keel. That's correctly built.
- **Dialogue** is lean and natural. There are about eight balloons in four pages, and none of them explains anything. "Because it never needed to be" is the one line that leans into riddle.
- **Splash pages:** none. The p.4 full-width match-cut from pencil bracket to rusted metal is the strongest visual idea and should be drawn big.
- **What they'd cut:** the sketch is shown three times (p.2 P4, p.3 P1, p.3 P4). The p.3 P1 close-up could go, and the scene would reach Keel half a page sooner.
- **Would they take the job?** Probably yes, on the strength of the cut and the restraint. They'd want to see the next ten pages before committing, because the frame promises a story it hasn't started yet.

## A game designer

There's a game shape in four pages, but no game yet. The wall works as a ready-made progress screen: failed prototypes, licences and a debt counter that reaches zero. The implied loop is repair, earn, pay down the debt and build up the wall, with "BALANCE 0 CREDITS" as the win state. The obvious choice that costs something is whether to patent or not (p.3 P2), traded against licensing out to the forty shops. Players would want to push on how the debt arose and whether it can be negotiated, and on the licensing system. The pages define none of these mechanics. **Would they build it?** They'd be curious, not committed. The resource-and-debt structure is a good start (inference) if the Keel chapters supply real systems.

## A streaming showrunner

- **The engine** is a flashback framed by a grown man telling his child (*This Is Us* / *How I Met Your Mother* structure), driven by a clear debt to pay off. Climbing from 400,000 down to zero is a season-long counter an audience can track.
- **Characters to follow:** only young Alex, and so far he's just a silhouette with his hand on a pump. The daughter is a framing device. The four laughing collaborators in the photo (p.2 P1) are the obvious ensemble, and a showrunner would want them introduced fast.
- **Season-one cliffhanger:** not yet visible. Possible mystery boxes are the watch with the empty display (p.3 P4), who imposed the debt, and why the sketch never became a patent.
- **What's missing:** an antagonist, the world (what Keel is and why water matters), and any danger. The first issue would need to deliver all three after this frame for a pickup.
