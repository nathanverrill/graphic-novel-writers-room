# analysis.md — prosperity-prologue (pages 1–4)

I read only the four pages. The book is a quiet frame. A father, Alex, and his nine-year-old daughter stand in a hallway in front of a wall of his patents, his failures and a paid-off account. A sketch with no patent stirs a memory, and a match cut on page 4 takes us to Keel in 2043, where Alex is seventeen. That is the whole book. It sets up a story and finishes none, so every verdict below is about an opening.

**Surface vs story.** The surface is a successful inventor-founder whose wall says "I failed, borrowed, paid it back, and sold things people use." The story so far supports that reading. The one loose thread is P3 p2. A teacher or the partner could read "it never needed to be [a patent]" as "the thing that really mattered was given away." That is the altruism-over-profit worry from the partner's earlier notes, planted in a single line. The four pages give no reason for it either way. Nobody stops reading over politics here. If students stop, it will be because nothing has happened yet.

**Does the book conclude, or leave a reader concluding, that free enterprise is bad? No.** P1 p3, P2 p2–p4 and P3 all show a founder whose products, patents and repaid debt are on display with pride.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like.**
- Water is on page 1: the capture panels, the cistern and "a small purifier unit" (P1 p1).
- A real product in a real school: "That one's in my school!" (P2 p2).
- Failure kept and framed: "v1 - didn't work" (P2 p3). That matches their "willing to fail" value.
- A debt shown and then shown paid: "PAID IN FULL · BALANCE 0 CREDITS" (P2 p4) against "deep in debt" (P4 p3). It echoes Bill Daniels repaying his debts without making a speech of it.
- Many separate founders with their own shop signs (P1 p3): business that serves people, shown and not explained.
- Plain dialogue that is easy to follow, and nothing partisan.

**What would bother them.**
- P3 p2, "Because it never needed to be." They have told the room they teach "the importance of profit and protecting intellectual property." An unexplained "it didn't need a patent" is exactly where they would ask "what does this teach?"
- "KEEL. 2043." (P4 p4) arrives with no explanation. Their earlier complaint was too little setting.
- As a reading assignment, four pages contain no initiative or decision by a young person yet. Young Alex appears in the last panel.
- Whatever the final art looks like (see the offence table).

**Likely verdict:** satisfied in part. They could put these pages in front of a student without embarrassment, which is a real change from "I cannot ethically present this." But one ambiguous line about intellectual property is unanswered, and the setting is still only a caption.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like.**
- "Made some. Fixed most. Worked with some people smarter than me." (P2 p1) is self-deprecating and plain, and gives credit to others. It is close to his own register.
- A failure kept on display (P2 p3). "Free markets work only when there's failure."
- Debt faced and repaid (P2 p4, P4 p3). It points at his beer-hall story without telling it.

**What would bother him.**
- Very little. He might find four hallway pages slow.
- The things he hoped for are absent: Bi11bot, the dog named for a Founder, a mentor who asks questions. Absent is not the same as wrong.
- He might share the partner's question about P3 p2. It is an inference that the "no patent" line would bother him; his de Soto view, that property should be "written down, recorded and defensible," pulls against it.

**Does Bi11bot sound like him?** Bi11bot does not appear in these pages, so I can't judge. Alex's P2 p1 line does follow his voice notes: plain, at his own expense, with one clean line at the end.

**Likely verdict:** satisfied. Nothing crosses his lines, and the values on the wall are his.

## A comic writer

- **The hook.** It is a quiet mystery, not a hook. The real hooks are the sketch with no patent (P3 p1–2) and the watch with the dead display, covered by a thumb (P3 p4). Both are good, but both are small.
- **Page turns.** P3 → P4 is the best beat in the book. The thumb covers the watch with no dialogue, and the turn opens on "Not in the beginning." P1 → P2 turns on "Did you make *all of these*?", which is fine. The book ends on a caption, "KEEL. 2043.", with nothing at stake for the next page.
- **Dialogue.** Lean and natural. Ten balloons across four pages. "Made some. Fixed most." is the line they would quote.
- **Splash pages.** None. The P4 p4 match cut from the sketch to real bracket hardware wants to be a splash or a half-page. Squeezed into one panel of a four-panel page, it loses its force.
- **What they would cut.**
  - The account statement is staged twice (P2 p4, and P4 p2's "do not … make it a visual destination").
  - P4 p1 to p2 is two near-silent beats where one would do.
  - About a page of hallway could go to buy room for the splash.
- **Would they take the job?** As a prologue to a longer book, yes: it is controlled and well paced. As a four-page pitch on its own, no. There is no conflict on the page yet.

## A game designer

- **Loop.** Implied, not shown: rationed water, debt, repair or invent, sell, repay. "Made some. Fixed most" plus "deep in debt" is a credible crafting-and-ledger loop.
- **Choices that cost something.** None on the page. Patent or not (P3 p2) is the only implied choice, and the choice and its cost happened offstage.
- **Systems to push on.** Water rationing, credit and debt, intellectual property versus sharing, and the many separate water businesses (P1 p3) as a market to compete in.
- **Would they build it?** Not from these pages. The premise interests them; the pages give them no rules to play with.

## A streaming showrunner

- **Engine.** A framing device in the style of *This Is Us* or *Big Fish*: an older narrator, a curious child, a flashback. The engine of the actual story (what young Alex wants and who stands in his way) is not yet on the page.
- **Characters for a season.** Alex: yes, the two time periods give him range. The daughter is a device, not a lead. Adrian exists only as a watch, and that is the strongest lure.
- **Season-one cliffhanger.** It isn't set up, though the dead watch and the sketch that never got a patent suggest a reveal.
- **What's missing.**
  - An antagonist and stakes.
  - What Keel is.
  - Why 2043 matters.
  - Any sign of the water conflict beyond "rationed."
- **Verdict:** they would read on, but they would not order a series on these pages alone.

## What would change the verdicts

1. **P3, the sketch scene.** Show on the page why the bracket never needed a patent: for example, it was a field repair that only ever existed once, or Alex sold the fix and moved on. This would settle the partner's IP worry about the existing line. *For Young Americans Center.*
2. **P3 p3.** Give the daughter a name and one judgment of her own (she notices something on the wall he didn't point to), so she is not only admiring. *For a teenage girl, and female teachers.*
3. **P4 p4, made a splash.** Open the Keel panel to full page and put rationing in the art: a queue at a meter, a locked tap, a ration board. The setting then lands without anyone explaining it. *For students, the partner and the comic writer.*
4. **A final beat on P4.** Put something at stake in young Alex's hand (a failing pump, a debt notice, a deadline), so the last page turn pulls the reader forward. *For the showrunner and the comic writer.*
5. **The rendered pages.** Hand-lettered, art that does not read as generated. This is the partner's first complaint, and the one a skeptical sixteen-year-old raises first. *For Young Americans Center and students.*
