# Skill: Storycraft Core

Use this with every creative role.

## The medium

A graphic novel is not illustrated prose. Meaning comes from the interaction of image, words, sequence, page architecture, and the reader's act of connecting panels.

The page and the page turn are storytelling units. A panel is a unit of attention. A balloon is physical space.

## Core principles

1. Every page must earn its existence.
2. Prefer causality over chronology: one choice should create the next problem.
3. Character is revealed by consequential action under pressure.
4. Visual information should do work that dialogue would otherwise have to do.
5. Words and images should complement each other rather than redundantly narrate the same fact.
6. Enter scenes late and leave when the dramatic transaction is complete.
7. Escalation means changing the problem, not merely increasing volume.
8. Plant information before the reader needs it; pay it off after they have nearly forgotten it.
9. Preserve orientation unless confusion is intentional and useful.
10. Build memorable contrasts: scale, pace, silence/noise, public/private, hope/fear, order/chaos.
11. Page turns are opportunities for surprise, revelation, reversal, or a changed question.
12. Clarity is not the enemy of complexity. The reader can handle difficult ideas if the immediate dramatic objective is legible.

## Scene test

For any scene or sequence, identify:

- Who wants what right now?
- What prevents them from getting it?
- What choice do they make?
- What changes because of that choice?
- What does the reader now know, feel, or anticipate that they did not before?
- Why is this better as comics than as a paragraph of explanation?

## Information hierarchy

Separate:
- what the character knows,
- what the reader knows,
- what other characters believe,
- what is objectively true in project canon.

Dramatic irony is useful only when those differences are intentional.

## Exposition

Prefer evidence, behavior, environment, conflict, artifacts, and consequences. Dialogue may explain what cannot reasonably be shown, but characters should not tell one another things they both obviously know just for the reader's benefit.

## New concepts

When introducing a new institution, technology, location, rule, or social idea:
1. Show it doing something.
2. Give the reader one concrete anchor.
3. Delay secondary terminology.
4. Demonstrate the consequence of misunderstanding it.
5. Repeat the concept later in a changed context.

## Caveat

Rules are tools, not commandments. Break them when the effect is deliberate and the reader benefit is greater than the cost in clarity.

## Final principle

The reader should experience the story, not the writers' room.
