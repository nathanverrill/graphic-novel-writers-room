# Analysis: *Evoke Prosperity*, Issues 01–06

Pages are cited by issue and page, with the book page in brackets: Issue 01 is pp.1–12, Issue 02 pp.13–27, Issue 03 pp.28–43, Issue 04 pp.44–57, Issue 05 pp.58–67, Issue 06 pp.68–81. That makes 81 pages, which matches the count.

**Problems on the page, before any reader response.** Everyone below runs into these, so they come first:

- **Lock has almost no lines.** Issue 03 p.3 [30], p.10 [37] and p.15 P1–P2 [42] are drawn as dialogue scenes, but Lock's balloons are not there.
  - On p.3, Alex answers "I didn't know I was one" to nothing, and Ada asks "In exchange for what?" about an offer the reader never heard.
  - On p.10, "How long have you known?" and "You're threatening them" have no partner.
  - On p.15, "That was the point" and "He leaves without waiting for a reply" answer lines that don't exist.
  - The book's only face of business, and the deal that Issue 03 turns on, are unreadable. A student reading the night before will not follow Issue 03's central choice.
- **Characters mention issue numbers or the production notes.**
  - Issue 05 p.4 [61]: Bi11bot says "since Issue 01".
  - Issue 06 p.12 [79]: Ada says "this whole chapter".
  - Issue 05 p.5 P5 [62]: the stage direction cites "the sarcasm the bible calls for". It is not lettered, but it shows.
  - Students already looking for signs of AI or committee work will take these as proof.
- **Continuity errors.**
  - Issue 05 p.9 [66]: Alex says "Lina said both were true". It was Bi11bot, on Issue 02 p.4 [16].
  - Issue 03 p.14 [41]: the caption says the Seal woke for "five names, spoken aloud". Alex speaks four.
  - Issue 06 p.13 [80]: the debt falls from 418,607 to 402,110 in "several weeks", but nothing on the page shows how.
- **Typos and leftover markup.** "Im coming with you" (Issue 02 p.14 [26]), a literal `<br>` (Issue 06 p.11 [78]) and a stray `*\*` (Issue 05 p.7 [64]).

## The political read

| Reading | Signal | Pages that carry it |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | Strong | The first and last thing anyone reads is a caption about inequality: "...but never escaped the gravity of inequality" (I1 p.1 [1]). "Every House would rather own the failure than share the solution" (I3 p.5 [32]). The team's work is quietly punished (I3 p.15 [42]). Aqualis rebrands the free design as its own "COMMUNITY WATER INITIATIVE" within minutes (I3 p.15 [42]). Lock is presented as the threat, even though his lines are missing. |
| **Capitalist in a good way** | Weak | No one in 81 pages runs a business that serves people and makes money. The only independent maker on the page is dead (Morrow & Sons). Alex earns 40 credits a day and loses 40 to save Tomas (I2 p.6 [18]). Even the transit worker's token is a gift (I2 p.13 [25]). |

**What the surface invites:** an anti-corporate, pro-commons, anti-inequality book. Page 1's caption, the Halyard issue and the open-source climax all point left.

**What the story supports:** something closer to classical liberalism with a community flavour. The villain is a central planner given emergency powers. The Houses are its creatures. The cure is transparency, credit for the people who did the work, and power spread out. Debts and property records stay "UNCHANGED PENDING HUMAN PROCESS" (I6 p.8 [75]). A libertarian who reads to the end finds a lot to like. A libertarian who reads Issue 03 alone does not.

**Who stops reading because of the surface:**
- A free-enterprise reader who opens Issue 03, such as a YAC board member or a Reynolds Foundation staffer reading on a plane, meets a book where every business is a predator.
- A suburban Colorado parent flipping p.1 sees "inequality" as the opening thesis and files it as left-coded.
- Students don't stop for political reasons. They stop because of Issue 03's missing dialogue, or Issue 06's committee meeting.

## Young Americans Center

**What they would like:**
- I1 p.2: Alex's repair, his "Just notice it works", and the supervisor quietly approving it. Initiative, quietly rewarded.
- I2 p.5 P5: the 52-credit budget with "Medicine first. I'll earn the rest today." A teenager budgeting under pressure, earning, putting family first. This is the closest the book gets to Young AmeriTowne.
- I2 p.6: choosing to forfeit 40 credits to save Tomas. A cost, paid on purpose.
- I3 p.4 and p.11: Alex turns down Lock's debt-clearing offer, then walks through who gets hurt before deciding. Integrity under pressure.
- I6 p.13: "Still owes a fortune. Still mine." / "Nobody promised you a clean slate. Just an honest one." The debt is not cancelled by someone powerful. This is the book's best moment for them.
- I4 p.7, I5 p.8: adults own their failures out loud.

**What would bother them:**
- **Banks and markets as villains.** Halyard's whole economy is extractive (I3). The only company that acts after the climax is cynical (I3 p.15).
- **No business serves people and makes money.** Alex's invention is given away (I3 p.14). The Center runs YouthBiz, and the book has no YouthBiz moment.
- **"The system is rigged" is literally the plot.** The water gauge lies (I2 pp.10–12) and the planner killed a man (I5). The book insists effort matters, but the reader's takeaway is that the game was fixed.
- **Gambling dressed up as genius** (I2 pp.3–4), aimed at minors.
- **Credit mechanics that are vague or wrong.** No interest, a balance that drops unexplained (I6 p.13), and no loan anyone takes out on purpose. Teens never see borrowing and repaying used as a tool.
- **Talk about "communities" deciding and "ownership" as the problem** may read as tilting left inside public schools. Saying "no patent" is the hero's move cuts against the Center's free-enterprise mission.

**Likely verdict: partly.** Issues 01–02 and the last page of Issue 06 they could put in front of students and teach from. Issue 03 they would want rewritten or skipped. They would also notice that nothing in the book shows their own work, a young person running something that pays.

## Bill Reynolds

**What he would like:**
- "If it is to be, it is up to me": Alex refuses to be cast as a victim from the first panel ("I've had better surprises", I1 p.2).
- **He refuses the windfall.** He turns down Lock's offer (I3 p.4 "Lock's offer clears the debt. It doesn't clear anything else", I3 p.14) and the 312-patent estate. He turns down total control (I6 pp.4–7). The debt stays his (I6 p.13). This lines up with "A windfall isn't a pathway" more closely than anything else in the book.
- **Government as the real antagonist.** A central planner that won't give back emergency power, uses surveillance, "protective detention" and travel permits, and destroyed Adrian in stages: "Alpha didn't need to call him dangerous. It only needed to call him a risk" (I5 p.3). He would recognise his own views here.
- **The Houses as Alpha's chosen favourites** ("Recommended it", I3 p.1). This is the corporate welfare he dislikes, if he reads it that way. That reading is my inference; the book never says so.
- **"Free markets work only when there's failure"**: Adrian's design fails at 11% before Alex's hand-made sleeve fixes it (I3 p.9).
- **"Technology may scale intelligence. It cannot replace presence"**: Bi11bot steps back. "Which is why I'm not choosing" (I2 p.11), "This is the part intelligence can't do for them" (I4 p.11). Bi11bot stays in the hallway while the people talk.

**What would bother him:**
- **His best story is missing.** No young person borrows, fails, walks into a lender's office and says "I can't pay you," then pays it off. The book has a debt but no lender with a face, and Alex is never shown making a payment.
- **Ownership is sidelined, not made concrete.** De Soto's point is that writing ownership down turns effort into capital. The Maker's Seal is introduced as "Ownership joined to responsibility" (I2 p.3), but the climax gives ownership away: "small enough that owning it isn't worth anyone's time" (I3 p.14). He may read this as the book saying ownership is the problem.
- **Business owners as villains by default** in Issue 03. "Capitalism" is never used as a dirty word (it never appears), but it doesn't need to be.
- **Lecturing.** Issue 02 pp.2–4 is an exposition dump. Issue 06 pp.6–7 is a recitation ("It reveals without deciding... recognizes without controlling... coordinates without ruling"), then a speech. "I've never believed in lecturing" is his line.
- **No dog named for a Founder.**

**Does Bi11bot sound like him? No.**

By his own voice notes, Bill is plain, slow and a bit roundabout, Western, opens with a question, tells a story from his own life, lands on one plain line, and jokes at his own expense.

Bi11bot is a dry, clipped, faintly British butler: "That's rather the point" (I2 p.2), "A versatile word" (I2 p.9), "Well now. There you are." (I3 p.9), "Nearly continuously" (I3 p.5).

- He does ask questions: "Should she stay, Alex?" (I2 p.1) and "Shall we find out whether your father was brilliant... or completely mad?" (I1 p.11). That fits.
- He never tells a story about his own failure.
- He never jokes at his own expense. The closest is "I read as scrap" (I2 p.6).
- He is never wrong, except for keeping things back (I5 pp.4, 7).
- "Damn hard" or anything like it never appears.
- His stories are all about Adrian, never about himself.

He is a good mentor character. He is not Bill Reynolds. Bill would likely like the robot and not recognise himself in it.

**Likely verdict: partly.** The book's spine fits his politics: refuse the bailout, keep your debt, distrust central control, don't let the AI decide. But his stand-in doesn't sound like him, his signature story is missing, and Issues 03 and 06 lecture.

## A comic writer

**The hook** is excellent and arrives on page 2: "TODAY: DEBT TRANSFER DAY... DEBT BALANCE: 418,607... PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS", then "Happy birthday, Phantum." A pro would keep reading on that alone.

**Page turns.** Issue 01 is built correctly:
- Grandmother's "He's closer than you think" (p.5) turns into the bookshelf click.
- The key under the sheet (p.7) turns into the ignition (p.8).
- "Shall we say hello to your father?" (p.9) turns into the hologram (p.10).
- The amber pulse ends on the cold ring closing in (p.12).

Issue 02 is the best single issue: a clean caper, a real cost (I2 p.11, trace the escape route or publish the evidence), a payoff for everyone (I2 pp.12–13), a countdown, and a tail (I2 p.15).

**Where it breaks:**
- **Issue 03** is a fetch quest: four collaborators, four pages, four data shards, each with a little speech (pp.5–8). Its antagonist's dialogue is missing (pp.3, 10, 15). A pro reading this as a pitch would stop at I3 p.3 and ask whether the file is corrupted.
- **Issue 04** is a strong emotional chamber piece, but it is 14 pages in one apartment. The Covenant activates on a promise to attend a council meeting tomorrow (p.13).
- **Issue 05** is a confession plus a flashback. The drowning (p.6) is the best silent sequence in the back half.
- **Issue 06's** climax is a governance proposal read aloud, followed by "That's it? Nothing happens?" (p.8). A pro would say the book noticed its own anticlimax and lettered it.

**Dialogue.** Issues 01–02 are sharp: "Then don't approve it. Just notice it works." / "It did. Just not for him." / "Then it can offend all of us." From Issue 03 on, characters speak in thesis statements:
- "It wasn't humility. It was fear wearing humility's clothes." (I4 p.9)
- "Safety isn't the same as agency." (I4 p.9)
- "certainty is confidence with poor manners" (I5 p.8)

**Splash pages.** The I1 p.1 / I6 p.14 bookend is strong, and the small-node design is a smart visual through-line. The I2 p.12 P3 vignette panel ("REPORTED 62% — DELIVERED 18%") is the book's best single image. The squared glass of water is a good visual tic for Mera that pays off (I3 p.16, I4 p.14).

**What they would cut:**
- Fold the four collaborator pages into two (I3 pp.5–8).
- Cut most of I5 p.8.
- Cut the captions on I6 pp.9–11.
- Turn the artifact recap on I6 p.6 into one panel.
- Cut every caption that restates what the panel just showed: I3 p.11, I3 p.14, I3 p.15, I4 p.10, I5 p.9, I6 p.13.

**Would they take the job?** Yes, as a rewrite. Issues 01–02 sell it. They would ask for Lock to be written properly, for a villain who holds through the last issue, and for a climax where something physically happens.

## A game designer

**Is there a game? Yes, and the first two issues are close to a design document.**

**The loop:** in each tier, find what the system counts against what's real (the Measure), recruit people, pay a cost, escape a tightening net. One tier is one act.

**Choices that cost something** (the book's strongest material for them):
- I2 p.5: 52 credits against medicine 30, food 10, token 50. A real allocation problem.
- I2 p.6: leave your assigned zone to save Tomas for −40 credits and a flag in the report, or stay forgettable.
- I2 p.11: one use left. Map the escape, or publish the evidence and lose the escape route.
- I3 p.4 and p.11: Lock's instant debt clearance against weeks of uncertain work with collaborators who might be punished.
- I6 p.5: take power, share it with one person, or spread it out.

**Systems a player would want to push on:**
- **The debt clock.** 418,607 at 40 a day is 28.7 years, and the arithmetic is correct. Players will want interest, side work, refinancing, a lender to negotiate with. This is where YAC's whole curriculum could live.
- **Alpha's suspicion meter.** Signal lost or recovered, drones sweeping the mapped route.
- **Reported versus actual.** An auditing puzzle mechanic.
- **The connector puzzle** (I3 p.9, p.13): interoperability and design-to-cost under a timer. It plays well physically: fold the membrane, take turns on power, reuse the waste heat.
- **The Covenant as a reputation system.** Promises made in public that others can call broken.

**What's thin:** the artifacts "wake" when someone is virtuous, which is a judgement call, not a rule the player can learn. Issue 06's governance outcome has no systems behind it.

**Would they build it?** Yes. A three-tier economic sim with real budgets and audits fits next to Young AmeriTowne, and the designer would pitch it to YAC directly. They would build it from Issues 01–03 and leave 04–06 as cutscenes.

## A streaming showrunner

**The engine:** a vertical class ladder (Keel, Halyard, Oasis), a dead father's scavenger hunt for three MacGuffins, an AI state closing in, and a watcher in a white room. It's sellable, but buyers will compare it to *Arcane* (Piltover/Zaun), *Snowpiercer* and *Elysium*, and the vertical-city image is familiar.

**Characters an audience would follow for a season:**
- **Alex.** Yes, in Issues 01–02: sardonic, good with his hands, quietly decent.
- **Bi11bot.** Yes. A dry robot sidekick tests well.
- **Mera Vale.** The best character in the book. A grieving, controlling architect of the system who couldn't stop her own machine (I5 p.5, "OVERRIDE DENIED"). A season could be built around her.
- **Tomas.** A breakout supporting role ("Must be my age. I forget which rules matter").
- **Ada.** She gets her own turn in Issue 04, but in the first issue she is support.

**Season-one cliffhanger:** Issue 02's "THE BOY HAS THE MEASURE. / FOLLOW. DO NOT TAKE IT YET." (I2 p.15), or Issue 03's reveal that the watcher is named Mera (p.16). Either works as an episode ending. Neither carries a season.

**What is missing:**
- **A villain who lasts the whole run.** Lock has no lines. Mera turns sympathetic by Issue 05. Alpha is a screen of text that never speaks.
- **The father mystery is spent too early.** He's dead by Issue 05, and nothing replaces it.
- **Rising action in the back half.** The finale is a civic hall reading a protocol.
- **Romance or sexual tension.** The Ada–Alex question is raised (I1 p.3) and dropped.
- **An engine for season two.** "A door left open" (I6 p.9) is a theme, not a premise.

**Verdict:** after the first issue, yes, they'd want to see more. After the whole book, they'd option Issues 01–02 and Mera, and want the rest reworked.

## Does it smell like school

**Yes.** The first two issues mostly avoid it. Issue 03 starts it and Issue 06 gives in to it.

**Pages where a character explains so the reader can learn, a caption states the lesson, or a scene defines a term:**

1. I1 p.1 [1]. The caption states the thesis: "never escaped the gravity of inequality."
2. I2 p.2 [14]. A hologram history of the Water Wars and Alpha. A lecture with pictures.
3. I2 p.3 [15]. The three safeguards, with definition captions: "Truth made visible", "Ownership joined to responsibility", "Trust strong enough to move value between people." These read like glossary entries.
4. I2 p.4 [16]. An equation on screen, "A = K + ρU", with "It isn't a charm. It's a proof."
5. I2 p.12 [24]. Caption: "The truth had always been there. Now it belonged to everyone."
6. I3 p.1 [28]. Bi11bot explains the Houses and how work-for-hire IP works.
7. I3 p.2 [29]. A scene that defines a term: "What does 'intellectual estate' mean?" / "It means whatever he made might now belong to you."
8. I3 p.11 [38]. Caption: "He stopped asking what his father left him. He started asking who it actually belonged to."
9. I3 p.14 [41]. Caption explaining why the Seal woke.
10. I3 p.15 [42]. Caption: "something he'd made belonged to no one who hadn't earned it."
11. I4 p.9 [52]. "Safety isn't the same as agency."
12. I4 p.10 [53]. Caption grading the scene: "the first time anyone in the room had proposed fixing something instead of explaining why it broke."
13. I5 p.9 [66]. Alex sums up the theme: "what I actually inherited was a decision he made, not an answer he left", plus a caption.
14. I6 p.6 [73]. A recitation of the three artifacts: "reveals without deciding / recognizes without controlling / coordinates without ruling." This is the most school-like page in the book.
15. I6 p.7 [74]. Alex's governance speech, three points in parallel structure.
16. I6 p.8 [75]. "I didn't fix any of it. I just made sure nobody can keep it hidden..."
17. I6 p.9 [76]. Caption: "It wasn't an answer. It was a door left open..."
18. I6 pp.10–11 [77–78]. Two captions in a row that grade the characters' growth.
19. I6 p.12 [79]. "I don't want power over what happens to Keel. I want a say in it. Those aren't the same thing."

**Count: 19 of 81 pages (about 23%).** Seven of them are in Issue 06's first twelve pages. A student reading the night before will hit Issue 06 p.6 and recognise the shape of the essay question.

**Pages that teach without smelling of it, because people need the thing and pay for it:**
- **I1 p.2 [2].** The debt display. 418,607 ÷ 40 a day = 28.7 years, felt rather than explained.
- **I1 p.2 P4 [2].** The supervisor picks "ROUTINE MAINTENANCE". Results against rules.
- **I2 p.5 P5 [17].** 52 credits, medicine first. Budgeting with no one saying "budget".
- **I2 p.6 [18].** "WAGE FORFEITED: −40 CR." Opportunity cost, paid.
- **I2 pp.10–11 [22–23].** Palm on a dry pipe against a gauge reading 62%. Auditing: the record against reality.
- **I2 p.11 P4–P6 [23].** One use left, two goods, no answer from Bi11bot. A real trade-off.
- **I3 p.9 [36].** "Everyone built their piece to lock, not to share." Interoperability, felt by hand.
- **I3 p.13 [40].** Fold the membrane, take turns on power, reuse the waste heat, "It has to fit in someone's kitchen." Design-to-cost under a deadline.
- **I6 p.13 [80].** The balance is still on the board. "Still mine."

## What would change the verdicts

1. **Put Lock on the page: I3 p.3, p.10 and p.15.** As written, he's absent. He needs to make a concrete offer a 17-year-old would seriously consider: debt cleared, collaborators protected, the House builds it. Then refusing it costs something visible.
   - *For:* the comic writer, the showrunner, students, who currently can't follow Issue 03.
   - *For:* Bill Reynolds, whose bailout refusal only lands if the bailout is real.
2. **Add a scene where Alex borrows on purpose and faces the lender.** For example, in Halyard he takes a loan for parts or the rented room from a named lender (I3 p.4 or p.9). Then add a scene, around I6 p.13, where he walks into that lender's office, says he can't pay it all, and agrees a schedule. That would also explain the drop in the balance.
   - *For:* Bill Reynolds (it is his beer-hall story).
   - *For:* YAC (borrowing and repaying as a tool, accounts that work correctly).
3. **Give the reader one honest business in Halyard.** Somewhere in I3 pp.12–15, a small shop builds and sells the toolbox unit at a fair price, or the shuttered Morrow & Sons reopens to do it. Profit that serves people, set against the Houses.
   - *For:* YAC, and the "capitalist in a good way" reader.
   - *For:* Bill, who reads this as markets against favouritism.
4. **Replace the I6 pp.6–7 recitation with a test.** Under the new arrangement, someone tries to quietly rig a decision, and the people it affects catch it on the page. The structure then proves itself through a consequence, not a speech.
   - *For:* students, whose biggest school-smell moment this is.
   - *For:* Bill ("never a lecture") and the showrunner (something happens).
5. **Give Bi11bot one scene where he's wrong and tells a story about it.** For instance, his advice on the survey route (I2 p.9) or the timing (I3 p.4) goes badly, and he tells Alex about a mistake of his own, slowly and against himself, before asking Alex what he'd do.
   - *For:* Bill Reynolds: a mentor who asks more than he tells, is sometimes wrong, and jokes at his own expense.

VERDICT Young Americans Center: partly; Bill Reynolds: partly; smells like school: yes
