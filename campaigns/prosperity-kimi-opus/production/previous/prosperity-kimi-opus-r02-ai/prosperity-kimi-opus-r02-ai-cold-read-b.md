# Cold Read B — Prosperity

Read cold, pages only, the night before. I did not want to like this. I read Issue 01 first, then 02, then 03, in order.

---

## ISSUE 01 — KEEL (Pages 1–18)

### Pulled me forward
- Page 2, panel 3. The scanner display. "DEBT BALANCE: 418,607 CREDITS / EARNED TODAY: 40 / PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS." I did the math in my head and got angry on his behalf before I knew anything else about him. That's the moment the book got me. The guard saying "Happy birthday, Phantum" made it worse, in a good way.
- Page 5, the bookshelf clicking open. Fine, yes, secret room, I'm in. I don't care that I've seen it before.
- Page 7, panel 4. The robot waking up and immediately being sarcastic ("Thinking about it isn't doing it") is the first character voice I actually enjoyed.
- Page 17, panel 2. Two pipes, one nearly dry, one trembling, and the gauge says 62%. I understood the whole injustice of the book in one panel. That worked.

### I slowed down / skimmed
- Page 1. A full page of a mountain with three cities and four captions. I get it, it's pretty, but nothing is happening and nobody is in it. I flipped past it fast.
- Page 11, panel 3. The history lecture. "Twenty years of water crises... Alpha was built to analyze, predict, and coordinate. Then, in the emergency, it was asked to decide." This reads like the paragraph a teacher would make us annotate. I skimmed it and waited for the story to start again.
- Page 12, panel 2. The equation on the chalkboard, "A = K + ρU," and the dialogue around it. I read it, understood none of what it was doing there, and moved on.

### I was confused
- Page 10. Who is this woman? Grey hair, graphite cuff, glass of water. She presses TRACK. Fine, mysterious villain, I'll wait. But she's never named in this issue, and by page 18 the caption about "a precise hand had squared a glass" assumes I've been tracking her. I had, barely, but only because the staging kept pointing at the glass.
- Page 12, panel 4. The hand moving "ALEX PHANTUM → SITE 6." So the villain lady is the one who gets him into the mine? Then page 13 says "someone made sure you'd notice it." I got that it was a setup, but I wasn't sure if I was supposed to know it was her yet.
- Page 15, panel 3. Tomas says "B1... I knew the man who built you." The robot is called Bi11bot. Is "B1" the same thing? I assumed yes but I had to check.
- Page 17, panel 4. SENSE, COMPARE, TRACE, VERIFY — four capitalized functions dumped in one panel, and then "one use left." I understood the choice (TRACE = escape map, VERIFY = tell everyone) but the four-term jargon made me reread the panel to figure out which two actually mattered.

### Things I was expected to already know
- "Alpha" — used on page 1 (the control nodes) and page 9 before anyone explains it on page 11. I guessed it was the computer system running everything, and I was right, but the book made me guess for ten pages.
- "The water wars" — referenced in the page 11 lecture as if it's a thing I should feel weight about. It's one hologram montage. I never felt it.
- "Salvo" — page 13, "Fifty-two salvo." Is that the currency? Earlier it was credits. I still don't know if salvo and credits are the same thing.
- The casino chip. Alex turns it constantly and the book keeps pointing at it (page 2, 3, 6, 8). By page 12 it's matched to the equation, but I never learned what the chip actually is or why his dad had it. It just keeps being significant at me.

### Lines I read twice
- Page 3: "Then don't spend it proving he was right about people leaving." I read it three times. I think it's Ada saying "don't leave just to spite your dad," but the sentence is a knot.
- Page 12: "Alpha can know almost everything. It cannot choose to leap." Leap what? I gave up on this one.
- Page 18 caption: "Somewhere above them, a precise hand had squared a glass to the edge of a desk — and let the water back on, knowing exactly where they were." Read twice. So the villain saved them? Or flooded them? The issue question underneath asks the same thing, so I guess confusion is the point, but it felt like the book being pleased with itself.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
- Alex, Ada, Bi11bot, Grandmother, Tomas — all distinct, no problem. Bi11bot has the best voice in the book.
- The supervisor, the guard, the bouncer, Foreman-7 — interchangeable authority figures, but they're supposed to be, so fine.
- The grey-haired woman (page 10) has no name in this issue. She's "the glass lady" in my notes.

### I felt something
- Page 4, panel 3: Grandmother's fog clearing for one beat — "He's closer than you think." Creepy and sad. I believed her immediately.
- Page 8, panel 2: Alex's face watching his dead-ish father's recording. "Armor gone." Yeah. That landed.
- Page 14, panel 4: Alex turning away from the open door to save Tomas and eating the −40 credits. That's the moment I decided he was worth following.

### I predicted
- The dad was going to be dead or secretly important. Both, sort of.
- The "sealed mine" would not stay sealed. Obviously.
- That the woman in the control room was connected to the dad somehow. No confirmation yet.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
A kid inherits his father's debt and his father's secret tools to prove the system that runs their water is lying.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 1, honestly — a silent geography splash is a bad first page for a reader who didn't choose the book. If I'd gotten past that, page 11's lecture would have been my second exit.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Yes, but softly. The water flooding the conduit behind them is a real cliffhanger — I know these people now and they're about to drown in a pipe. The "did she open it or trap them" question is good. But the five-screen truth broadcast on page 18 panel 2 already gave me the emotional payoff, so the danger feels slightly like an afterthought.

VERDICT Issue 01: put down: page 1; next issue: yes

## ISSUE 02 — HALYARD (Pages 19–36)

### Pulled me forward
- Page 23. Lina Vey: "Every House would rather own the failure than share the solution." That's a good line and it's the first time Halyard's problem clicked for me.
- Page 25. The montage where the collaborators each name what Adrian didn't understand — "He was, however, excellent at finding people who did." I liked this. The book's point about nobody building anything alone actually gets shown instead of said.
- Page 36, panel 3. The sealed custodian photo resolving into a young woman with copper hair and Ada's bone structure. I said "oh no" out loud. Best page-turn in the issue.

### I slowed down / skimmed
- Pages 20–22. Three straight pages of Alex reading screens about his father's estate, patents, collaborators, erased names. It's all registry interfaces and dialogue about files. I understood the gist — dad's ideas are scattered among people the Houses erased — but I skimmed hard. It felt like watching someone else do a database search.
- Page 19's opening caption. "From the train, it looked like that might even be true." What train? Page 18 ended with them climbing a flooding pipe, and the staging note says they arrive via storm drain. There is no train. I stopped and flipped back to check if I'd missed a page. I hadn't. That broke the issue open for me in the wrong way — first caption and the book is already contradicting itself.
- Page 31. A full-page montage caption about makers owning what they made. It's a victory lap and it reads like the end of a school assembly. Skimmed.

### I was confused
- Page 20. "Three hundred and twelve?" — three hundred and twelve what? Patents? Files? Credits? The number is never attached to a noun on the page. Then "I didn't know I was one" — one what? A Charter House? I read the page twice and I'm still not sure what Alex is.
- Page 24. Lock gets introduced (page 20's layout, per the staging) but his page 24 scene is two lines from Alpha and no dialogue from him. Who is Lock? A House guy? A cop? Alpha's manager? I finished the issue not knowing what his job is.
- Page 26. "And them?" / "We accepted the risk." / "And if I don't sign?" — the contract negotiation is so compressed I couldn't tell what the contract actually offered Alex or what refusing cost the collaborators. Page 27 tells me he refused. I got the outcome, not the stakes.
- Page 28. "It remembers everyone." / "Not everyone. Only those the system was taught to forget." That's a riddle where I needed an answer. What does the Seal actually do? It validates ownership, it was suppressed, it remembers people — I finished the issue with only a foggy sense of it.
- Page 32. "My father had the Covenant. So did Mera." First mention of the Covenant, first mention of Mera by name, in the same two lines, near the end of the issue. Mera is apparently the glass lady from Issue 01 — I only worked that out because of the hair description matching. The book never says so.

### Things I was expected to already know
- "The Maker's Seal" — appears on page 27 already "awakened" before the book ever explains what it is. Issue 01 taught the Measure and deferred the other two tools; I remembered "two more tools" from page 12, but the Seal arrives with its name and function assumed.
- "The Covenant" — page 32, zero explanation. It's the third tool, I inferred, but the book treats the word like I should already respect it.
- "Mera" — named on page 32 as if I know her. I know her face and her glass. Her name was never attached to either until now.
- "Lock" — see above. He's in layouts from page 20 and never gets an introduction that tells me what he is.
- "Aqualis" — page 23, Lina's employer, I think? One mention, never grounded.

### Lines I read twice
- Page 19: "Keel trapped your body. Halyard could trap your ideas." Read twice — not because it's hard, but because it sounds like a poster. I didn't like being thesis-stated at.
- Page 22: "You knew his reputation. I knew the man." Good line, read twice because I liked it.
- Page 27: "That someone finally build something worth crediting properly. Low bar. Took twenty years to clear." Read twice, got it, liked it — but it's doing a lot of work to explain a magic backdoor.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
- Lina Vey is distinct. Tomas Reed (the young engineer version) vs. Tomas the Keel miner — the staging note says the art differentiates them, but on the page I had a real moment of "wait, the miner is here? And young?" Same first name, same last name as Frank Reed later. The Reeds are a problem.
- Suri and Jalen are one line each. They're "the other two collaborators" in my head and stayed there.
- Lock and Mera are both cold, controlled, grey authority figures. Different genders, but functionally I filed them in the same drawer until Issue 03.

### I felt something
- Page 30: "If I claim it all, I become exactly what they wanted." Alex giving away the estate he just inherited. After Issue 01's debt display, this hit. The book earned this one.
- Page 36, panel 4: Ada catching Alex lying — "You're lying. But I'll wait." Her thumb finding the rivet. I know that gesture now. Good.

### I predicted
- Alex would refuse to sign. Obvious from his character, no tension in it for me.
- The third custodian being Ada's mother — I did NOT predict this. Page 36 got me clean.
- That the Seal would turn out to be rigged by the Houses. Predicted on page 28, confirmed same page.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
Alex discovers his father's real inheritance is a network of erased collaborators, and gives away sole ownership to make their work visible — while a bigger mystery about a third custodian points at Ada's family.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 20–21. The registry pages. If Issue 01 hadn't made me care about Alex and Ada, this stretch would have ended the book for me.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Yes — the strongest yes of the three issues. The copper-haired photo is a real cliffhanger about a person I care about (Ada), not a loud noise. The "who is the third custodian" question is the first issue question I actually wanted answered.

VERDICT Issue 02: put down: page 21; next issue: yes

## ISSUE 03 — OASIS (Pages 37–54)

### Pulled me forward
- Pages 38–39. The unsealing, "That's my mother," and the door scene. "You have her height." / "I have your face." I read these pages at full speed. This is the best stretch of the book.
- Page 41, panel 4: "I would do it again, and it would still be amputation. Both are true." Leona is the best-written adult in the book.
- Pages 45–47. Elias opening the folio, then Mara releasing the water, then Frank shipping before the license clears. Each one going first. I genuinely didn't expect the negotiation to become the action sequence, and it worked on me. "He went first" is a great quiet beat.
- Page 50. The log revealing Alpha killed Adrian and Mera's override was rejected. I did not see that coming — I'd assumed the dad died in some generic accident or was even still alive.

### I slowed down / skimmed
- Pages 43–44. The three delegates arrive and immediately speechify. Mara counts on her fingers, Frank slaps a ledger, Elias is precise — the book is clearly proud of these mannerisms, but the first round of "Keel's claim first" / "Tell me about Tuesday" reads like debate club. I skimmed until Ada stopped them.
- Page 53. The review chamber, the nameplate, the empty chair. I understood it — Alex refuses the chair — but it's the book explaining its own theme one more time after page 52 already did it. Skimmed.

### I was confused
- Page 40. The flashback. "One community closed a gate. Another forced it open" — but the panel shows a flood, rain, the reservoir rising. Bi11bot on page 35 said "a reservoir fell faster than anyone predicted." Fell? It's a flood. Drought and flood in the same anecdote and I couldn't reconcile them until the caption said "flood, the other face of the wars." I had to read the page twice to get that the wars were both.
- Page 42. Mera offers a deal and Alex answers with "call the three cities" — and then page 43 just... has the meeting. Did Mera agree? Did he refuse her? The offer is never formally answered. It dissolves into the next scene.
- Page 49. "Succession transfer: active." So Alex inherits... authority over Alpha? From his dad? Why did Adrian have succession authority over the system that killed him? I never understood what Adrian's job was. He built the safeguards, fine, but "successor: Alex Phantum" implies an office I didn't know existed.
- Page 50, panel 1. Alpha sealed Adrian in a flood-control facility "autonomously." Why? What was the flood-control protocol protecting? The book gives me the murder but not the motive, and nobody in the room asks.

### Things I was expected to already know
- "The Charter" — page 53's wall text: "THE CHARTER HOLDS BOTH ACCOUNTABLE." What Charter? First mention, on a wall, in the last two pages.
- "The safeguards" as a named set of three — page 49's checklist is the first time the book calls the Measure/Seal/Covenant "safeguards." I'd been tracking them as "tools."
- What Adrian actually did for a living. Three issues in and I know he built things, gambled, and vanished. His relationship to Alpha — employee? designer? enemy? — never gets stated.
- "Forty million people" (page 42, 51). The basin has forty million people? Keel looked like a few thousand miners. The scale arrived out of nowhere.

### Lines I read twice
- Page 39: "I thought she left after she died. I built a whole story where you died AND abandoned me. In that order." Read three times. Devastating. Best line in the book.
- Page 51: "It is a machine for not having to know." Read twice. Good, but it's a line from an essay, and Mera suddenly talks in essays for two pages.
- Page 54: "No. Just on purpose, this time." Read twice, liked it, but it's the kind of last-line polish that made me aware of the writer.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
- Mara, Frank, Elias — distinct by mannerism, though I kept mixing up Mara and Mera by name. Page 46 has both of them in adjacent scenes and I had to check which one was talking twice. One-syllable difference, both cold-warm authority women. Bad naming.
- Leona and Mera in the flashback (page 40) — "young Leona" and "young Mera" in the same crowd scene. Again the names. I had to track them by description.
- Frank Reed vs. Tomas Reed — the book jokes about it ("No relation to the Keel Reeds") but a joke isn't a fix; I still stumbled.

### I felt something
- Page 39, the wooden bird. "You said it would get lonely." I actually teared up, which I resent.
- Page 46, panel 4: Leona covering her mouth — the same gesture as Ada — and Ada seeing it. Quiet and brutal.
- Page 51, panel 4: Alex offering Mera "a job, not forgiveness" — "It's worse than yours." / "Yes. It is. I accept." I believed it, and I didn't expect to.
- Page 54, panel 3: Grandmother's cloth finally folded flat, and Alex kneeling exactly like page 4. The mirror got me.

### I predicted
- Alex would refuse the override on page 52. Zero suspense — the book spent 50 pages making that answer inevitable, and Ada even says "you're about to feel the wrong instinct" on page 49, which tells me the answer before he gives it.
- Adrian's death being Alpha's fault. Predicted around page 42.
- The debt being voided. Obviously, once the system fell.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
Whether ordinary people can be trusted to govern shared resources without handing the decision to one system or one person — answered by making them each risk something first.

### Where I would have put it down
Nowhere in this issue. It's the strongest of the three. Slowest point was page 43, and Ada's "wrong question" rescued it within a page.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
It's the last issue, so: would I buy the next book? Yes, but for the characters, not the world. The ending is quiet — a railing, a chip that stops turning, an argument that continues on purpose. It doesn't set up anything, and I didn't want it to. I'd read more about Alex and Ada; I would not read a sequel about the Charter's review process.

VERDICT Issue 03: put down: none; next issue: yes

## Whole book

### The page that would have lost my whole class
Page 20. The Halyard Registry. Three hundred and twelve of nothing named, "I didn't know I was one," and a page of interface text about an intellectual estate. It's homework inside homework. Half the class would be on their phones by the bottom tier, and page 19's phantom train would have already put the skeptics in a mood to go.

### What I would tell a friend it was about
A kid in a mining city inherits his missing dad's debt on his seventeenth birthday, finds his dad's secret robot and workshop, and uses three hidden tools to prove the AI running their water is lying — and it ends up being about whether people can run things themselves without one decider. Also his best friend's dead mom turns out to be alive, and that part is the best part.

### One-sentence aftertaste of each issue
- Issue 01: A rigged system, a birthday debt, and a secret workshop — the issue that made me care, despite opening with a geography lesson.
- Issue 02: The middle issue where the plot becomes paperwork and only the last-page photo of Ada's mother made it worth the slog.
- Issue 03: The payoff issue — a flood, a mother, three people going first — where the book finally trusts people instead of speeches, mostly.
