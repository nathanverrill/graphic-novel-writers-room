# First read — prosperity-kimi audition

Read A first, then B. Same night, same patience. I'm a 17-year-old who got this as homework and wants it to be bad.

---

## Version A

### Pulled me forward
Page 1, the splash. Three cities stacked on one mountain with the pipe getting thinner as it goes down — I got the whole world in one image before a single word. And the kid with his palm on the pump like a doctor listening to a chest — that's a person, immediately. "Then don't approve it. Just notice it works" is a good line and I believed him.

Page 2, panel 2, the scanner readout. 418,607 credits, earned today 40, clearance 28.7 years. I did the math in my head and felt sick. That's the moment the book had me. "Happy birthday, Phantum" landing right before it is cruel in the right way.

### I slowed down / skimmed
Page 3, panel 2. "Everything has a price, Ada." / "Not everything." I've read this exchange before, in other books, almost word for word. I skimmed the rest of the panel.

### I was confused
Page 2, panel 4: the torn poster of a man's face that Alex doesn't look at. I get that it's probably the dad, but the page doesn't give me anything to do with it. Confused but intrigued — barely.

### Things I was expected to already know
- "Alpha" — the scanner is called an Alpha scanner (page 2, panel 1). Never explained. Is Alpha a company, a government, an AI? Three pages in, no idea.
- The "K" shoulder patch, the credit system itself — I could infer these, fine.
- The casino chip. Clearly matters. No idea why yet. That's okay, it's a hook, not a leak.

### Lines I read twice
"Then don't spend it proving he was right about people leaving." Read it three times. I *think* it means: don't waste your life confirming your absent father's low opinion of people. But I'm still not sure I parsed it right, and it's the closing beat of the whole audition, so it needed to land clean. It didn't.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
Alex and Ada, easily — he talks in shrugs and prices, she talks in refusals. The guard and supervisor are interchangeable but they're supposed to be.

### I felt something
The queue of workers glancing up at his giant balance and saying nothing (page 2, panel 3). That silence did more than any dialogue on the page.

### I predicted
Dad gambled, dad ran, the chip and the poster are his, Alex will try to leave and the debt will stop him. Also Ada's "not everything" means she's the person who stays.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
A kid inherits his runaway father's impossible debt on his seventeenth birthday in a town built to keep him, and wants out.

### Where I would have put it down
I wouldn't have. Three pages, and I want page 4.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Yes, but softly. The last line is a riddle I half-solved, not a hook. What pulls me to the next issue is the debt and the chip, not the final panel. If the last line had landed clean it would be a need; as it is, it's a want.

## Version B

### Pulled me forward
Page 2, panel 2 — the supervisor checking the official gauge, needle in the red, while the pump runs level under Alex's patch. That beat proves Alex is right and the system is wrong in one image, and "Keep moving, Phantum" tells me the supervisor knows it and can't say so. Best small moment in either version.

The bouncer on page 2, panel 4: "Care to see if luck runs in the family?" / "I've also got his debt." Now the poster and the chip *do* something — the world itself knows who his father was. That exchange made the dad realer in two lines than A managed in a whole page.

### I slowed down / skimmed
Page 1. "Exhausted copper-zinc-lead-silver polymetallic mine." I stopped being a reader and started being a student right there — it reads like a textbook caption, and it's exactly the kind of thing that would make me say "this feels AI-made" and check out. The three labeled captions (OASIS. HALYARD. KEEL.) are fine, but the panel description's homework tone leaks into how the page feels. No people on it, either — A gave me a kid with his hand on a machine; B gives me a diagram.

Page 3, panel 3 — four straight lines of thesis dialogue ("I keep thinking about waking up somewhere I get to choose what happens next" / "I keep thinking about why everybody gave up choosing it down here"). Nobody talks like this. I skimmed.

### I was confused
Page 2, panel 3: "TODAY: DEBT TRANSFER DAY" on the display. Wait — so this happens to everyone on their seventeenth birthday? Or is the machine editorializing? A let me sit in Alex's surprise; B announces it like a calendar event, which made me stop and wonder about the rules instead of feeling for the kid.

### Things I was expected to already know
- "Alpha control nodes" (page 1) — same problem as A. Named, never explained.
- "House insignias: water, energy, mobility" (page 1) — Houses? What Houses? This is a whole political structure dropped in a caption with no story attached yet. It went on my ledger and stayed there.
- The etched equation on the chip — intriguing, fine.

### Lines I read twice
"Nobody stays, either. Not really." Read twice, gave up. I don't know what it means — people are physically there but checked out? It's a riddle line and I had no patience for it.

Same closing line as A — "don't spend it proving he was right about people leaving" — same problem. But B adds "Alex looks at her. The chip stops turning," which at least tells me it landed on *him*, so I let it go more easily.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
Alex and Ada, yes — and B's Ada has a clearer position (she's the one arguing for staying and fixing Keel). The supervisor in B is an actual character; A's isn't. Bouncer, guard — furniture.

### I felt something
The supervisor's jaw working before he says "Keep moving." And the bouncer line — the whole town knowing your father's shame before you do.

### I predicted
Same as A, plus: the etched equation on the chip is dad's real inheritance, and Ada will be the one who wants to fix Keel while Alex wants to leave it. That conflict is set up more clearly here.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
A debt-trapped kid in a company town discovers his father's legacy is more than the debt, and has to choose between escaping and fixing the place.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 1. The polymetallic mine and the diagram-feel of the splash would have told me this is a lesson disguised as a comic, and I'd have been back on my phone. If I'd been forced past it, page 2 would have won me back.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Yes — more urgently than A, oddly, even though A's individual pages read better. Because B spent its page 3 setting up a real argument between Alex and Ada (leave vs. stay), I want to see which one wins. The closing beat lands softer as prose but harder as story.

---

## Which one I would keep reading, and the moment that decided it
A — by a nose, and it's the first page that decided it. A's splash put a human being in the foreground of the world and let the image do the explaining; B's splash explained. I'm exactly the reader who smells "educational" and bails, and B's page 1 smells. But it's close: B's page 2 (the gauge, the bouncer) is the best page in either audition, and B's page 3 gives Alex and Ada a real disagreement instead of a mood. If I could have A's page 1 with B's page 2, I'd stop worrying about this book entirely.

## One-sentence aftertaste of each
- **A:** A cold, clean gut-punch of an opening that trusts its pictures and slightly over-trusts its last line.
- **B:** Warmer and better argued, with the best single beats — but it keeps stopping to teach me the world.
