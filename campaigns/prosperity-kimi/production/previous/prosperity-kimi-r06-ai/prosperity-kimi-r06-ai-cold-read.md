# Cold Read — Prosperity

Read the whole thing in one sitting, the night before, wanting it to be bad. It wasn't, mostly. Here's what actually happened.

---

## ISSUE 01 — KEEL (Pages 1–18)

### Pulled me forward
Page 2, panel 2. The scanner readout — "DEBT BALANCE: 418,607 CREDITS / PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS" — is the first thing in the book that made me actually react. That's a horrible birthday present and I understood the whole world from one screen. Page 5, the bookshelf swinging open, is the moment I stopped skimming for good. Page 14, the frame dropping on Tomas right when the door is unwatched — I noticed the story handing Alex a choice and I wanted to see which way he'd go. Page 17, the two pipes, one dry one trembling — I got the whole crime in one image, no explanation needed.

### I slowed down / skimmed
Pages 11–12. Bi11bot explains the Water Wars, Alpha, the five streams, the Measure, "one of three, in sequence." I read it because I had to, not because I wanted to. It felt like the part of the game where the NPC talks too long. Page 13 is more table-and-map talk and I started reading faster.

### I was confused
Page 10. The pulse climbs the pipe, a woman we never see the face of presses TRACK instead of REPORT. Okay, mysterious. But then page 12 panel 4 the same hand moves Alex's name to Site 6, and I couldn't tell if she was helping him or hunting him. Confused but intrigued — I kept it as a question, not a leak. Page 16 panel 4: Ada is suddenly inside the mine with them. Last I saw she was at the apartment. The parenthetical explains it but I tripped on it for a second — "wait, when did she get here?"

### Things I was expected to already know
- "Alpha" — used on page 2's scanner before anyone says what it is. I guessed "the system that runs everything" and page 11 confirmed it, so fine, but for nine pages it was just a word.
- The casino chip. Page 2 panel 4 he's turning it, page 6 it matches an equation, page 12 "Grandma said he wasn't gambling." Nobody ever says plainly what the chip *is* or where he got it. I assumed it's his dad's. Never confirmed in this issue.
- "He" in Ada's line page 3 — "don't spend it proving he was right about people leaving." I figured out it means the dad, but only after page 4.
- The water-drop mark. On the book, the key, Tomas's wrist guard, the gauge. I noticed it every time and nobody explains it in this issue. I was okay waiting, but it's a tab the book kept open.

### Lines I read twice
- "Then don't spend it proving he was right about people leaving." (p3) Read it three times. Still not 100% sure what it means. Something like: don't leave just to spite your dad? It's the kind of line that sounds deep and dissolves when you press it.
- "Both were true. The research was real. So was the cost." (p12) Read twice. Got it the second time — he was gambling AND researching. First time I went "both of what?"
- "Nobody checks what everybody trusts." (p17) Got it in one, but it's the kind of aphorism this book keeps handing out and I started noticing the habit.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
Alex, Ada, Grandmother, Bi11bot, Tomas — all clear, all distinct voices. Foreman-7 is a faceless robot, fine. The hologram dad is clear. The only blur: the nameless supervisor page 1 and the guard page 2, but they don't matter.

### I felt something
Page 4, Grandmother asking "Did your father come home yet?" — that landed. Page 8, the hologram saying "question everything... especially me" — I actually said "okay, that's good" out loud. Page 18, Tomas on a bandaged leg hauling the wheel open — I was in it by then.

### I predicted
The dad is dead or "dead." The grandma knows way more than she shows — page 4 panel 3 is basically a neon sign. The woman in the control room is important and probably connected to the dad. All three paid off later, which felt satisfying, not cheap.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
A kid inherits his missing father's debt and his secret fight against the system that decides who gets water.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 11, if anywhere — the history-lesson hologram. But by then the bookshelf and the robot had me, so honestly: none.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Yes. Page 18 ends with them climbing inside a pipe with a wall of water behind them and the truth already broadcast — that's a real cliffhanger about people I know, not a loud noise. I'd read issue 2 tonight.

VERDICT Issue 01: put down: none; next issue: yes

## ISSUE 02 — HALYARD (Pages 19–36)

### Pulled me forward
Page 23, Lina Vey: "I've been waiting twenty years for someone to ask." Good line, and the river-failing problem gave this city its own stakes fast. Page 27, the Seal waking up and Bi11bot's "Low bar. Took twenty years to clear" — I laughed. Page 32, "Someone both of them agreed the world should forget" — that's a hook. Page 36, the half-corrupted photo and "Ada's hair" / "Not yet" — best page-turn in the issue, and it's the last page of the *next* chunk, which annoyed me.

### I slowed down / skimmed
Pages 19–22 are rough. The captions and dialogue are doing all the work and the work is explaining: three Houses, the Registry, the intellectual estate, the collaborators, the erased names. I skimmed page 20's dialogue chain — "What does 'intellectual estate' mean?" — because it read like a worksheet. Page 25's montage of Tomas Reed, Suri, Jalen each saying what Adrian didn't understand — I got the idea in one panel and there were four. Pages 28–31 are the Seal doing registry things and I was reading captions, not a story. Page 31's caption is a paragraph of summary that felt like the book telling me what to feel about Halyard.

### I was confused
Page 19's caption says "From the train, it looked like that might even be true." What train? They climbed out of a conduit with a flood behind them last issue, and the staging note itself says they come out of a storm drain. I noticed the contradiction cold, no notes needed. Page 24: Alpha says two lines about probability and ownership and then the page ends — I didn't know who Alpha was talking *to* until I inferred Lock from page 26. Lock himself: he appears on page 20's layout description, speaks nowhere clearly until page 26, and I never learned what he wants beyond "the consequence." He's a shape, not a person. Page 29, "Request denied. Foundational instrument has priority" — denied whose request? I had to reconstruct it.

### Things I was expected to already know
- The Maker's Seal. It "awakens" on page 27 and drives everything through page 33, and I never got a clean statement of what it physically is or why Adrian could leave a backdoor in a citywide registry. "One of three" from issue 1 is doing a lot of unpaid labor here.
- The Covenant. Name-dropped page 32 as if I know it. I don't until page 35.
- "Aqualis" (p23) — a company? A House? Lina's employer? Never nailed down.
- Mera, page 32: "So did Mera." First time her name is spoken aloud, and I only know her as the faceless hand from issue 1. The book treats her as established.

### Lines I read twice
- "She keeps the experience. They keep everything else." (p19) Got it, but it's another aphorism.
- "They didn't steal the Seal. They stole its purpose." (p28) Read twice. Still think it's a poster, not a sentence.
- "Only those the system was taught to forget." (p28) Read twice, gave up on parsing it precisely, moved on.
- "It would require trust." (p33) The caption literally announces the theme. I felt managed.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
Lina — clear. Lock — a suit and a desk, no voice. Tomas Reed vs. Tomas from Keel: I genuinely stopped on page 25 — "wait, the miner is here?" No, it's a different Tomas Reed. Two Tomases, one of them a Reed, and then issue 3 gives me Frank Reed. Bad idea, whoever named these. Suri and Jalen are one line each and never became people.

### I felt something
Page 27, Alex refusing to sign — "I'm not signing away people I just found." That worked because issue 1 earned his stubbornness. Page 30, pressing RESTORE CONTRIBUTOR OWNERSHIP — I felt the click of the theme completing, though a little like watching a lesson conclude.

### I predicted
The third custodian is Ada's mother. Called it at page 32, confirmed by the photo at 36. The prediction felt good, not spoiled.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
Same fight, new arena: who gets credit for what people make, and whether one person should own it.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 20. The Registry dialogue is the exact moment the book stopped being a story and started being a syllabus. If issue 1 hadn't bought credit, I'd be gone here.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Page 36 — yes, completely. The photo of Ada's mother and Bi11bot's "Not yet" is a real cliffhanger about someone I care about. But note: the actual Halyard story ended on page 33 with a caption telling me the theme, and pages 34–36 are the real issue-3 hook smuggled in. The 33 ending alone would have gotten a shrug; 36 gets a yes.

VERDICT Issue 02: put down: page 20; next issue: yes

## ISSUE 03 — OASIS (Pages 37–54)

### Pulled me forward
Page 38, "That's my mother" — and then the book does the right thing: page 39, "You let me believe you were dead." / "Yes." No speech, no excuse. I was locked in through the whole Leona sequence. Page 49, the playback of Adrian dying while Alpha logs the rejected override — the dad looking into the camera at water up to his chest is the best image in the book. Page 50, "The override was mine" — Mera finally becoming a person instead of a hand. Pages 51–52, the offer and the refusal — I genuinely didn't know which way Alex would go for one panel, and the book had earned that.

### I slowed down / skimmed
Pages 43–47. The negotiation. Mara, Frank, Elias each get an entrance panel, then it's several pages of people at a table being brave about ledgers. I understood every beat and I still skimmed, because the outcome was never in doubt — Ada asks one question on page 44 and the whole conflict unwinds on schedule. It reads like a model-UN exercise where everyone does the right thing in turn.

### I was confused
Page 48: the three components authenticate because of "three recorded human actions" — I had to think hard about which actions counted (Elias's data release? Mara's water? Frank's units?) and I'm still not sure that's the intended mapping. Page 51: Alpha offers Alex permanent override because "succession conditions" are satisfied — satisfied by what, exactly? The wager logic from Bi11bot ("your father built the test") is doing the explaining, and it's thin. I accepted it on momentum, not understanding.

### Things I was expected to already know
- "Adrian's transfer" (p48) — first mention, treated as known. I inferred "the thing that wakes when the three safeguards are satisfied" but the book never says it straight.
- The Charter (p52) — appears fully formed in the final governing line. New word, last act, no setup.
- Mera's whole deal — contact restrictions, association risk, the suppression of Ada's file — is explained in fragments across pages 41, 50, 53 and I assembled it, but a colder reader than me-by-page-40 would be lost.

### Lines I read twice
- "I thought she left after she died." (p38) Read three times. I think it's a typo or a riddle — she thought her mother left, and also thought she died? It stopped me cold in the middle of the best scene in the issue, which is a shame.
- "No one in that crowd was wrong. That was the problem." (p40) Read twice, liked it both times.
- "That is why it counts." (p46) Got it, good line.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
Leona — instantly vivid, best new character since Bi11bot. Mera — finally a person. Mara — the finger-counting and "Tell me about Tuesday" made her real in one panel. Frank and Elias — I kept having to check which one was which; "ink-stained founder" and "soft-spoken administrator" blur when they're both men in their 50s at the same table. And Frank Reed vs. Tomas Reed vs. Tomas the miner — three names colliding, still mad about it.

### I felt something
Page 39–41, the whole mother-daughter reckoning — the book's emotional peak, and it's quiet. Page 49, Adrian's death — I put the script down for a second, and I was reading because I had to. Page 54 panel 3, the empty chair and "That was the point" — the ending landed.

### I predicted
Alex refuses the override. Obvious from page 52 panel 2's setup, and honestly from issue 1 — the book never seriously entertained him taking it, which is the one place the ending felt safe. Mera's guilt — predicted at issue 1 page 10, confirmed. The grandmother promise callback — predicted, satisfying.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
Whether people can be trusted to govern themselves when a system offers to do it for them — and the answer is a wager, not a guarantee.

### Where I would have put it down
Nowhere in this issue — it's the strongest of the three. Closest is page 44, where Ada's one question starts fixing everything and I felt the author's hand.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Last page of the book. Would I buy the next book? Yes, but for the people, not the question. The final caption — "Can people accept responsibility for a future no system can guarantee?" — is the book telling me its essay question on the last page, and I didn't need it. The empty chair and Alex going back to his grandmother said it better. I'd read a sequel about Ada and Leona; I'd skip one about the Charter.

VERDICT Issue 03: put down: none; next issue: yes

## Whole book

### The page that would have lost my whole class
Page 20. The Registry scene — "intellectual estate," "in exchange for what?" — is homework dressed as dialogue, arriving exactly where the book should be cashing issue 1's momentum. Half the class never comes back from it. Page 11 is the runner-up.

### What I would tell a friend it was about
A kid in a dying mining town inherits his dad's debt, finds his dad's secret robot, and climbs the mountain city by city exposing how the system that runs the water rigs everything — and at the top he's offered the keys to the whole system and has to decide whether to take them. It's good. The middle sags. The mom stuff in the last one is the best part.

### One-sentence aftertaste of each issue
- Issue 01: A birthday scanner reading "28.7 years" and a bookshelf that opens — the issue that made me forget I didn't choose this book.
- Issue 02: A city where they steal credit instead of water, told too often in captions — the issue I read out of loyalty.
- Issue 03: A mother who let her daughter believe she was dead, and a dead father looking into a camera as the water rises — the issue that paid for the other two.
