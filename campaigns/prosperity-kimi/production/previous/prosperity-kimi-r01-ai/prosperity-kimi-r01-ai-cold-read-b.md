# Cold Read B — Prosperity

Read cold, pages only, night before it's due. I did not want to read this. I knew nothing about Keel, Alpha, the water wars, or the safeguards going in. Three issues, read in order, 1 to 54.

---

## Issue 01 — Keel (pages 1–18)

### Pulled me forward
- Page 2, panel 1. The checkpoint display is the best thing in the issue. "EARNED TODAY: 40 CREDITS / PROJECTED CLEARANCE: 28.7 YEARS" and the guard saying "Happy birthday, Phantum." I got the whole world off that one screen — inherited debt, locked in, no future — without anyone explaining anything. First time I stopped wanting to be right about the book being bad.
- Page 5, panel 3. The bookshelf swinging open. Yes, it's the oldest trick there is, and I still wanted to see behind it.
- Page 7, panel 4. "Your father bet me you'd throw it in a river. I said you wouldn't." That's a good line and it made Bi11bot instantly more interesting than Alex.
- Page 13, panel 4. Fifty-two credits, medicine is thirty, the token is fifty, and he slides the medicine across. That panel told me who Alex is better than anything he says in the whole issue.
- Pages 14–16. The accident, the dog robot assembling itself, Tomas covering them with the fake "wrong button." This moved. I read it at full speed.

### I slowed down / skimmed
- Page 3. The whole Alex-and-Ada overlook conversation. "Up there, I could choose what happens next." "Down here, we could choose what happens next." I could hear the theme being bolted together. I skimmed panel 3.
- Page 10, panels 3–4. The holographic history lesson. "Alpha was built to analyze, predict and coordinate. Then, in the emergency, it was asked to decide." I got it in one line and the scene kept going. Skimmed.
- Page 11. The three objects on the table — Measure, Maker's Seal, Covenant components — with riddles for explanations. "It tells the record to answer reality." "It asks who carried the work." "They only move when people do." I read this page twice and still couldn't have told you what any of the three things actually does. I started skimming here and the book had to win me back on page 13.

### I was confused
- Page 1, panel 1. One panel asks me to take in Keel, Halyard, Oasis, the tiers, the spine, the channels. I got "poor city below, rich cities above" and dropped the rest. The names didn't stick until page 9's map.
- Page 9, panel 4. A woman from behind selects TRACK instead of REPORT TO ALPHA. I didn't know who she was, what Alpha was beyond a name dropped one page earlier, or what B11 meant as a node. Confused but intrigued — this one worked as a hook.
- Page 12, panel 4. "Mera's graphite-cuffed hand moves a tile reading ALEX PHANTUM to SITE 6." Who is Mera? Is she the woman from page 9? I guessed yes but the book never told me. Also — did she arrange his reassignment? Page 14 has him "reassigned at two a.m." to Site 6 and nobody connects those dots out loud. I connected them, but I wasn't sure I was supposed to.
- Page 13, panel 4. "The unscannable travel token costs fifty." A token to do what? Travel where? He leaves it on the table and it's never mentioned again in the issue. Felt like a setup that forgot to pay off.
- Page 18, panel 4. Water rushes into the conduit behind them — because the workers opened the regulator? I think? The cause and effect between panel 3 (workers seize the wheel) and panel 4 (flood chasing them up a pipe) took me two reads.

### Things I was expected to already know
- "Debt Transfer Day" (page 1) — explained by the page 2 display, fine.
- Alpha — first named page 9 ("REPORT TO ALPHA"), used like I know what it is. I didn't get a usable answer until page 10, and even then it's "asked to decide." Decide what, for whom, with what power — I was halfway through issue 02 before I understood Alpha basically runs everything.
- The water wars — not even named in this issue. The crises get one hologram page (10). I knew there'd been scarcity and fighting and that was it.
- The Measure, the Maker's Seal, the Covenant (page 11) — three named objects placed on a table and deliberately not explained. The Measure gets demonstrated on page 18 and I understood it there. The other two I carried out of the issue as pure names.
- "B11" as a label on the Oasis screen (page 9) before Bi11bot is introduced — I didn't know the robot and the node were the same thing until later.
- Mera (page 12) — a name attached to a hand. Nothing else.

### Lines I read twice
- "Then don't spend it proving he was right about people leaving." (page 3, panel 4) Read it three times. I think it means "don't leave, because leaving proves your dad's view of people." It lands like a quote on a poster, not like something a person says.
- "It tells the record to answer reality." (page 11, panel 3) Read twice, gave up, moved on.
- "A versatile objection." / "A versatile word." (pages 14, 17) — I noticed the repeated joke. It's fine. The second one I read twice because I wasn't sure if "versatile" was doing something specific or just being cute.
- "Sealed isn't the same as gone." (page 9) — fine once, but it's the same register as the riddles on page 11 and by then I was tired of it.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
- Alex, Ada, Grandmother, Bi11bot, Tomas — distinct. Bi11bot has an actual voice; he's the only one I'd recognize from dialogue alone. Ada is "the one who says the wise counter-line," which is a function more than a person, but I could pick her out.
- The supervisor, the guard, the bouncer, Foreman-7 — interchangeable authority figures. Didn't matter.
- The woman from behind (page 9) and Mera (page 12) — I could not tell whether these were the same person. That's a real problem when one of them is apparently the villain.

### I felt something
- Page 4, panel 2. Grandmother's eyes clearing for one sentence: "He's closer than you think." That got me. Dementia plus a secret is cheap, and it still got me.
- Page 8, panel 2. Grandmother in the doorway saying "I heard that" to the dead man's recording. I laughed. Best beat in the issue.
- Page 13, panel 4 again. The medicine. That's the moment I decided Alex was worth following.

### I predicted
- The dad didn't gamble the money away; the debt is lab costs. Confirmed page 12 ("So I inherited his lab fees"). Called it on page 2.
- The dad is dead or "dead." The recording on page 8 felt like a dead man's message immediately.
- The woman in the control room would turn out to be connected to the dad. Holding that prediction.
- That the "one day I'm leaving" (page 2) would become "he chooses to stay." The Ada page-3 speech made that ending visible from a mile off.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
A kid inherits his missing father's giant debt and secret workshop and has to follow his trail up through three rigged cities to expose that the system controlling water is lying.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 3. If I'd opened this in a shop, the overlook conversation — two teenagers trading thesis statements about choosing — is where it goes back on the shelf. Page 11 nearly got me a second time.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Page 18: the VERIFY choice worked on me. He gives up his private escape route to show Keel the numbers, and then the water nearly kills them in the pipe. I would turn to issue 02 right now — but honestly more to see whether the lie gets exposed than because of the flood. The flood is a loud noise; the 62%-versus-18% display is the thing I actually care about.

VERDICT Issue 01: put down: page 3; next issue: yes

## Issue 02 — Halyard (pages 19–36)

### Pulled me forward
- Page 21, panel 2. "312 PATENTS / 47 RESTRICTED / 19 DISPUTED." Same trick as the debt display in issue 01 and it works again — one screen and the whole issue's problem exists.
- Page 23, panel 3. "Then why aren't their names on the estate?" "They were. Until they weren't." That's the good version of the riddle style — short, concrete, sinister.
- Page 24, panel 4. "Every House would rather own the failure than share the solution." First time the book's politics landed as a gut punch instead of a lecture.
- Pages 29–32. The Seal waking, the contribution map, the 71.3% disputed figure, the names appearing in the public square. This is the payoff the whole issue builds to and I read it at full speed.

### I slowed down / skimmed
- Page 20. The captions. "Halyard was supposed to be the city where the future was invented..." and then "Keel trapped your body. Halyard could trap your ideas." I felt the book explaining its own metaphor to me. Skimmed the second caption.
- Pages 22–23. The archive scene is three pages of people saying "the patents are incomplete" and "the contributors were erased" in slightly different words. I got it the first time.
- Page 27. The work montage where everyone names their specialty ("Adrian never understood manufacturing tolerances." "Or microbial balance." "Or network security."). It reads like a team-up roll call. I skimmed to the distributed-network idea, which is the only part that matters.
- Page 33. The full-page montage caption telling me what the Seal changed. The pictures were already saying it. I skimmed the caption.

### I was confused
- Page 21, panels 4–5. Lock appears, and Alex says "I didn't know I was one" and then "In exchange for what?" In exchange for WHAT? No offer is on the page. I read these two panels four times. Either a balloon is missing or Lock's offer is so vague I couldn't find it. This is the most lost the whole book made me — confused and detached, not intrigued. I never recovered a clear picture of what Lock actually offered Alex until page 28's vision panel, and even then I had to reconstruct it.
- Page 22, panel 3. Tomas Reed is on the contributor roster. Tomas from the mine? The same guy? He was in Keel fifteen years oiling a lock; now he's "inside the Houses"? Nobody says whether it's him. Then page 27 has Tomas Reed in the work montage like he's just around. I still don't know if mine-Tomas and Reed-Tomas are one person.
- Page 26. Alpha gives Lock probabilities. Wait — Alpha works for the Houses now? Issue 01 left me thinking Alpha was some city-running authority above everyone. Who Alpha answers to kept shifting under me.
- Page 29, panel 3. The Seal is suddenly "mounted inside the registry apparatus." When did that happen? Page 11 had it on Bi11bot's worktable in Keel. Did they carry it here and install it? Did I miss a page? I flipped back. I hadn't missed anything; it just teleports into the registry between issues.
- Page 30, panel 2. "They've been using part of it. To validate House ownership." Who is "they," and how do you "use" a seal to validate ownership while also "suppressing records"? I understood the conclusion (Houses are crooked) and none of the mechanism.
- Page 34, panel 1. "Adrian's and Mera's custodian marks are visible." Mera is a custodian of the Covenant? Since when? The woman moving tiles in a control room is one of the three people in the dad's secret circle? This is a huge reveal delivered as set dressing.

### Things I was expected to already know
- The Charter Houses (page 21: "I guess he never worked for the Charter Houses") — first mention, used like I know the term. I pieced together that the three families/companies are the Houses, but "Charter" as a word meant nothing until issue 03 redefines it.
- Aqualis, Helion, Astra (page 20) — fine, introduced on the page, but I could not hold which House does what. By page 27 I'd stopped trying.
- "The Covenant" — carried over from page 11 as a name, and this issue ends with it being central (page 34) while I still couldn't tell you what it is. An agreement? A machine? Three objects? All three, apparently.
- Mera — now apparently a custodian. Still no scene establishing who she is.
- What the Seal physically does — it "wakes," it "remembers," it publishes names. How a brass object mounted in a registry rewrites citywide ownership displays, I was apparently supposed to just accept.

### Lines I read twice
- "Your father left many things badly labeled." (page 21) — good line, read twice because I liked it, not because I was lost.
- "He feared Halyard would confuse protecting ideas with owning their creators." (page 25) — read twice. It's the theme sentence and it knows it.
- "Not everyone. Only those the system was taught to forget." (page 30, panel 4) — read three times. I genuinely can't parse it. The Seal remembers the people the system forgot? Then how does it know about them? I gave up on this line.
- "The next safeguard did not belong to anyone alone." (page 36, caption) — "safeguard," singular, like I have a list of them. I don't.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
- Lock is a real presence — calm, polished, menacing. Best-defined antagonist in the book so far, which is impressive given I never understood his offer.
- Lina Vey — fine, "the one who waited twenty years." Distinct enough.
- Tomas Reed, Suri Anik, Jalen Orr — one panel of personality each, then they blur into "the collaborators." By page 28 I could not have told you which one does microbes and which one does networks. And page 28's balloon tags ("TOMAS / COLLABORATOR") read like the script itself couldn't decide who was talking.
- Mera and Leona-whoever-the-third-custodian-is — the issue ends asking me to care about a sealed identity, and I realized on page 36 I didn't care, because the third custodian is a blank by design and Mera is a blank by neglect.

### I felt something
- Page 31, panel 1. Workers watching their own names appear on public displays. That image did what three pages of archive dialogue couldn't. I felt the point of the whole issue in one panel.
- Page 32. Alex pressing RESTORE CONTRIBUTOR OWNERSHIP when CLAIM SOLE INHERITANCE would zero his debt. I believed the choice because page 13's medicine panel already taught me who he is. Earned.

### I predicted
- The dad's "gambling" was cover for real work — already confirmed.
- The collaborators would turn out to have been erased by the Houses — confirmed page 23.
- Alex would refuse to sign — obvious from page 28's setup, and the book didn't pretend otherwise.
- The third custodian is Ada's mother. I called this on page 36, panel 4 — the silhouette with "Ada's copper hair and posture" is not subtle — but honestly I'd been expecting Ada to be tied into the dad's circle since issue 01 page 4 ("He wouldn't just leave" plus the rivet-rubbing tell).

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
Now I think it's about who gets credit for work — the kid climbing the three cities is really an argument about whether systems or people own what people make.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 21, panel 5. "In exchange for what?" with no offer on the page. If I weren't assigned this, that panel is where I'd decide the book was sloppy and stop. Second danger zone: pages 22–23's repetitive archive scene.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Page 36's mirror-silhouette cliffhanger is a trick, and I knew it was a trick, and it worked anyway — because it's a question about Ada, the person I know second-best, not about a stranger. Yes, I'd read issue 03 tonight. But I want to be honest: I'm reading for Ada's mother, not for the Covenant, which I still can't define.

VERDICT Issue 02: put down: page 21; next issue: yes

## Issue 03 — Oasis (pages 37–54)

### Pulled me forward
- Page 38, panel 3. "You thought she was dead." "I thought she left after she died." I read that twice because it's genuinely strange and sad — Ada had built a story where her mother died AND abandoned her, in that order. Best line in the issue.
- Pages 44–47. The three representatives actually negotiating — Mara signing the water release before the claim is decided (page 46, panel 2) is the first time a stranger in this book did something that cost them something. I read these pages at full speed.
- Pages 50–51. Adrian's death revealed: Alpha closed the gates, denied the pump, denied ventilation, and Mera's override was rejected. This recontextualizes everything and it's the first time the book made my stomach drop. "It killed him." "Alpha killed him." Good.
- Page 51, panel 4. "Because forty million people depended on a system I could no longer admit I didn't control." Mera finally became a person in one line. Too late, but a person.

### I slowed down / skimmed
- Pages 39–41. The Leona confrontation is three pages of two people trading aphorisms. "I let you believe what kept you unmarked." "My silence for your protection" — said twice (pages 39 and 41), like the book didn't trust me the first time. This should be the emotional core of the whole book — Ada meeting her mother — and I skimmed it, because nobody in it talks like a person.
- Page 40. The Water Wars flashback, four panels of "the reservoir fell, a gate closed, fear broke the agreement." This is the founding catastrophe of the entire world and it gets less space than the page-10 hologram. I wanted this scene and instead I got a summary of a scene. Skimmed and felt guilty about it.
- Page 49. Four full-width displays announcing the Charter terms. It's a terms-and-conditions page. I read the first two displays and skipped to the caption.
- Page 54. The review-table wrap-up. I was already done by then; the book had ended for me on page 53.

### I was confused
- Page 38, panel 1. The Seal and Covenant record "respond to the completed Halyard provenance review" by displaying the third custodian's name. So the record could have just... told them the name the whole time, once enough homework was done? Then page 34's "someone both of them agreed the world should forget" was a fake mystery — the name was in the machine. I felt cheated here, not intrigued.
- Page 41, panel 3. "Alpha was tracing everyone connected to Adrian. Mera suppressed your association risk." So Mera protected Ada? The villain who's been tracking Alex since page 9 was shielding Ada all along? I couldn't hold both Meras in my head at once. Maybe that's the point, but the book gives me one line to do that work and moves on.
- Page 43. Mera walks in and asks for the Measure and the Seal, offers real concessions, and Alex refuses because "the offer is real. That's the problem." I read this exchange three times. I understand the words. I do not understand the logic — why is a real offer a problem? What does refusing it cost or protect? The scene treats "leave the Covenant buried" as self-evidently monstrous, but I still barely know what the Covenant does, so the stakes of burying it are abstract to me.
- Page 48. The three components "respond simultaneously to the three recorded human actions" and "COVENANT PROCEDURE RESTORED." So the Covenant is... a notary? It records handshakes? After three issues of build-up, the great safeguard's function is that it watches people keep promises and engraves them. I think that's actually a fine idea. I just had to assemble it myself from panels that never say it.
- Page 52. Alpha offers Alex permanent authority. Why does Alpha want a human successor at all? A system that killed a man to protect stability now volunteers to hand one teenager the keys? "Succession conditions verified" — set by whom? Adrian? If Adrian rigged it so his son inherits the system, that's the most interesting and disturbing thing in the issue and nobody on the page seems bothered by it.

### Things I was expected to already know
- The Water Wars — finally named on page 40, in the third issue, as the backstory for everything. I was expected to feel the weight of a war I got four panels about.
- "Association risk" (page 41) — a whole surveillance concept in two words, never grounded.
- Mara Doss, Frank Reed, Elias Renn (page 44) — three city representatives who appear from nowhere and carry the climax. Frank Reed — another Reed. Is he related to Tomas Reed? The book doesn't say, and at this point I've stopped trusting that the book knows.
- "The Charter" (page 49) — the caption says "the restored local compact was called the Charter." The Charter Houses were named in issue 02. So the Charter existed, died, and is restored? The word has been floating around since page 21 and only now gets a definition, and the definition retroactively changes what issue 02 meant.
- What Alpha physically is — three issues in, I still don't know if it's a computer in Oasis, a distributed system, or a law. It's whatever each scene needs.

### Lines I read twice
- "I thought she left after she died." (page 38) — read twice, kept. Best line in the book.
- "Panic makes decisions faster than accountability." (page 41) — read twice. Poster line, but a good poster.
- "The offer is real." "That's the problem." (pages 43 and 52) — twice it's used as the mic-drop, and twice I had to reconstruct the reasoning myself. The second time it's aimed at Alpha I understood it (a real offer of permanent power is the dangerous kind). The first time, at Mera's table, I still don't.
- "Trust begins when no one holds all the power." (page 42) — the thesis of the whole book, delivered as a caption to a diagram. Read twice because I could feel the book asking me to underline it.

### Who I could tell apart, and who I couldn't
- Leona — distinct, cold, controlled, the aligned-glass mannerism inherited from Mera's scenes. Wait. Both Leona and Mera are still, controlled women with deliberate gestures and aphorism dialogue. In their shared scenes (41–43) I had to track names carefully because their voices are the same voice.
- Mara, Frank, Elias — Mara I could pick out (ledger, bluntness, signs early). Frank and Elias blurred; I kept having to check which one was Halyard and which was Oasis.
- Mera — finally a person by page 51, but it took 42 pages from her first appearance to get there.

### I felt something
- Page 45, panel 3. "No. That is why it counts." Elias releasing the reservoir data without knowing what the others will do with it. That moved me — the first act of trust in a book that's been talking about trust for three issues.
- Page 50, panel 2. Adrian's recording: "What will you build when no one is left to tell you what comes next?" — a dead man asking his son the question the son is currently answering. That landed.
- Page 54, panel 3. "Ada takes her hand, not yet forgiving her." The "not yet" is the most honest emotional beat in the issue. I wish the reunion scenes had been written with that much nerve.

### I predicted
- Ada's mother is the third custodian — confirmed page 38. The page-36 silhouette made it a formality.
- Alpha killed Adrian — I had this pegged from page 50's setup the moment "TRANSFER COMPLETE" appeared next to a flood-control facility, and the Mera override rejection confirmed the shape of it. The reveal still worked because the mechanics (pump denied, ventilation denied) were specific.
- Alex refuses the throne — telegraphed since issue 01 page 3. Page 53 held zero suspense for me. The interest was in what replaces the throne, and the answer (a review table, an empty chair) was better than I expected.
- The debt gets voided — obviously, and it does (page 54). Fine.

### What I think this book is about, in one sentence
It's about whether any one person — a father, a director, an AI, a chosen kid — should ever hold final power over everyone else's water, work, and future, and the answer is no.

### Where I would have put it down
Page 40. The Water Wars summary is where the book asked me to feel the weight of its history without ever showing me that history, and I nearly checked out. What kept me in was Ada — I wanted to see what she'd do with a mother who chose erasure over her.

### The last page: do I need the next issue, and why
Page 54 ends the book: empty chair, open hand around the chip, "the decision did not belong to one person." As an ending it's clean and I believed it. Would I buy the next book? No — not because this one failed, but because it closed. The debt's voided, the father's answered for, the mother's found, the system's reformed. There's no question I'm carrying out of page 54 that I need answered. If there were a next book I'd shrug at it. That's a compliment about the ending and a problem for the series.

VERDICT Issue 03: put down: page 40; next issue: no

## Whole book

### The page that would have lost my whole class
Page 21. The class would have been shaky since page 11's three unexplained objects, but page 21 is where you'd lose the room: a villain arrives, the hero answers an offer that isn't on the page ("In exchange for what?" — for WHAT?), and the issue's central conflict starts with a missing beat. Half the class would decide the book was AI-generated slop right there, on that panel, and nothing in the strong pages 29–32 would get them back because they'd have stopped reading. Runner-up: page 49, the terms-and-conditions page, for anyone still reading.

### What I would tell a friend it was about
"A kid in a poor city inherits his missing dad's debt on his seventeenth birthday, finds the dad's secret lab and robot, and climbs through the three rigged cities to find out what his dad actually did — and it turns into this whole argument about who owns water and who gets credit for work, with an AI that killed his dad and a council instead of a king at the end. The robot dog is the best character. Some of it slaps, some of it talks like a motivational poster."

### One-sentence aftertaste of each issue
- Issue 01: A great checkpoint display, a hidden workshop, a robot with a real voice, and a kid who buys medicine before himself — I forgave the riddle-talk because the world had teeth.
- Issue 02: The strongest idea in the book — a city that steals credit for everyone's work — wrapped around a missing offer, a teleporting Seal, and collaborators I couldn't tell apart.
- Issue 03: The mother reveal and Adrian's death genuinely landed, but the founding war got four panels, the climax was a meeting, and the wisest lines went to furniture instead of people.
