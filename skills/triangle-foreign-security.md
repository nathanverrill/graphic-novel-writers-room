# Foreign Security in the Lithium Triangle

Uses the same plausibility labels as the companion skills.

**"Lithium triangle" is only the name of the region here.** This skill is not about lithium. Treat lithium as one activity among many (copper, silver, tin, gold, borates, herding, farming, tourism, astronomy, trade, cities) and bring it in only when the writer's story does.

**This is a setting kit, not a plot.** It describes an environment, its constraints, and categories of things that can happen there. Do not steer toward any particular storyline, theme, protagonist, or political reading, and do not assume the writer wants conflict, villains, or a message. Serve whatever story the writer brings: a romance, a comedy, a procedural, a family saga, a thriller, a children's book. Offer options across the range rather than a favorite, and use only the parts that the writer's story touches.

## Plausibility labels

Everything here is labelled with the vocabulary defined in `skills/hard-sf-rules.md`: **T** truth,
**EG** educated guess, **S** speculation, **L** license, and **Cut** for what does not fit. The
rules for a license, the license log and the Thorne and Tyson tests are in that file; read it
before inventing anything the story then has to live with.

Here the "science" is the region's physics, law, economics, and politics.
Baseline is September 2026. Private security law and foreign deals change quickly. Search before treating specifics as fact.

## The honest picture first

If the image in your head is armed foreign mercenaries patrolling a mine or a salt flat in plate carriers, that is mostly **Cut**. These are functioning states with gun laws, licensing, nationalist politics, and press. Foreigners with rifles on Bolivian or Argentine soil is a scandal that ends careers.

What foreign security really looks like here is quieter and more interesting:

- **Foreigners manage, locals stand post.** The guard at the gate is a local on low wages, usually unarmed. The foreigner is the security manager, the consultant, the trainer, the analyst, or the vendor.
- **The guns belong to the state.** When force is used, it is police, gendarmes, or soldiers. The contractor's job is to make sure they show up, know where to go, and are fed, fueled, and housed. That relationship is where the real story is.
- **Most of the work is information.** Risk maps, community "stakeholder" files, social media monitoring, drone patrols, cameras, access control, convoy tracking, background checks, and who is planning the next blockade.
- **The tech is the foreign presence.** Chinese cameras and command centers, Israeli analytics and counter drone gear, US satellite and tracking services. Hardware outlasts any contract.

## What the law allows (the constraint that shapes everything)

- **Chile.** A new private security law tightened the sector: licensing, training, oversight by the police, and guards generally unarmed. Armed private protection is a narrow, specially authorized category. Foreigners need residency and credentials. Chile is where a contractor behaves most like a compliance officer. Organized crime in the north (cathode robberies, extortion, gangs) is the legitimate driver of demand.
- **Argentina.** Private security is licensed province by province. Firearms are tightly controlled. In practice the provincial police and the governor decide what happens around a mine, and the national Gendarmería covers borders and gets sent to blockades. A foreign firm works through a local licensed company, usually one with friends in the provincial capital.
- **Bolivia.** Private security is supervised by the police; guards are unarmed. The constitution bans foreign military bases, and anti foreign feeling around resources is strong and easily mobilized. The army guards strategic sites and has been posted at mines against ore thieves. Anything that looks like foreign armed men near Uyuni or Potosí becomes a national crisis within a day.
- **All three.** Companies that want Western financing or buyers sign up to the Voluntary Principles on Security and Human Rights and the international code of conduct for security providers. This creates paperwork, training sessions, grievance hotlines, and a gap between the binder and the road.

## How it really works: the public force arrangement

The key regional precedent is **Peru**, where mining companies have signed paid agreements with the national police: the company provides money, vehicles, fuel, food, and lodging, and police units guard the mine and respond to protests. Chinese owned Las Bambas is the best known example. Deaths of protesters at the hands of police housed on company property are documented.

In the triangle, expect versions of this (T to EG):
- Company builds or donates a police post, vehicles, radios, drones, or a camera network to the province or municipality.
- Gendarmes or police lodge and eat at the mine camp "for logistical reasons."
- The company's security manager sits in the police operations room during a blockade, or the police commander sits in the company's.
- Intelligence flows both ways: company files on community leaders reach prosecutors; police informants' reports reach the company.
- After something goes wrong, everyone points to the other's chain of command.

This is the plausible machinery of repression: legal on paper, deniable in practice, and foreign only at the level of who pays and who advises.

## What contractors actually do day to day

- Perimeter, gates, badges, vehicle searches, alcohol and drug tests at shift change.
- Convoy security for product, reagents, fuel, and explosives: GPS tracking, jamming detection, escort cars, route variation, panic protocols. This is the most real physical risk (hijacking).
- Theft control: copper cable, fuel, cathodes, concentrate, lab samples, vehicles. Most theft is inside assisted.
- Camp order: fights, drink, drugs, sex trade at the fence line, contractors' disputes.
- Travel security for executives and visiting engineers. Evacuation plans for altitude illness, earthquakes, floods, and unrest.
- Community "monitoring": who attends assemblies, who is funding the NGO, which leader might sign, what is said on WhatsApp and Facebook, drone footage of blockades.
- Liaison with police, prosecutors, the governor's office, the army, embassies.
- Investigations: fraud, leaks, activist networks, rival bidders.
- Crisis response: a blockade, an occupation, a death, a kidnapping, a spill. Lawyers and public relations are part of the security team.
- For Chinese firms specifically: compound management, staff movement rules, curfews, keeping personnel away from locals, and reporting to headquarters and the embassy.

## Surveillance and tech (T to EG)

- Fixed thermal and radar perimeters, long range cameras on towers, drone patrols on schedules, tethered drones at gates, counter drone jammers because communities and thieves fly drones too.
- License plate readers on the only road. Every truck and pickup logged for years.
- Facial recognition at gates and in the provincial capital's camera network, often the same vendor.
- Social media scraping, phone number mapping, informant payments. Spyware on activists' and journalists' phones is documented elsewhere in Latin America and plausible here (EG to S).
- Satellite imagery and AI alerts for new tracks, camps, and gatherings on open ground around a site.
- Worker wearables for fatigue and altitude that double as location tracking.
- Private Starlink type links so the site works when the state cuts or loses coverage.
- The counter side: communities with their own drones, lawyers filing data access requests, hackers and leakers, journalists matching shell companies to ex officers, and NGO security trainers teaching activists to check their phones.

## Escalation ladder

How a quiet arrangement gets worse, each step with precedent somewhere in the region:

1. **Compliance security** (T). Unarmed local guards, foreign manager, human rights training binder.
2. **Embedded police** (T to EG). Paid or in kind agreement. Public force lives on site.
3. **Intelligence operation** (EG). Files on leaders, infiltration of assemblies, smear campaigns, criminal complaints used as a weapon.
4. **Criminal threat changes the rules** (EG). Hijackings, extortion, or a gang killing gives the company and the state a reason to arm up. Licensed armed escorts for convoys. A state of emergency in the mining corridor. Soldiers on the road.
5. **Gray operators** (EG to S). A distressed owner or a contractor hires a cheap outfit. Ex police with personal weapons. Foreign supervisors who are tourists on paper. Beatings, burned blockade camps, threats. Deniable.
6. **Closed compound** (S). A state linked foreign project runs a sealed site with its own guards, its own rules, and local police who do not enter. Most plausible in Bolivia under a government to government deal, and it lasts only until national politics turns on it.
7. **The contractor becomes the authority** (S). See next section.

Beyond step 7 is **Cut**.

## How a security contractor ends up running a place

This is the plausible local form of the "abandoned site falls to a strongman" trope. See the companion skills for who else fills that vacuum.

- A major mothballs a project. The only continuing contract is site security, renewed yearly by someone in a distant office who never visits.
- The security lead controls the gate, the road, the fuel tank, the water bore, the generator, the vehicles, the airstrip, and the camp. He knows every local official and every local grievance.
- He starts small: charging herders and tour operators to cross, renting camp beds to another company's crews, selling fuel and water, letting a smuggler use the strip at night, hiring only from one family.
- His guards are locals who depend on him. His weapons are few, legal or nearly so. His real power is the phone: the police chief, the mining secretary, the union delegate, the ore buyer.
- The owner changes twice. Each new owner finds it easier to keep him.
- If he is foreign (a Colombian ex sergeant, a Chilean ex marine, a South African or British ex soldier who stayed, a Chinese supervisor who married locally), he is more exposed, not less. He needs a local partner as the legal face, and that partner can turn on him. That tension is good drama.
- He ends when national attention arrives: a death, a journalist, an election, a new owner with Western lenders. Then he is a scandal, a scapegoat, or gone overnight.

Hard rock variant: the same figure at a vein deposit also takes a cut from the informal miners who move in, which is more money and more violence.

## Abuses that are plausible

- Beatings, illegal detention, and handing people to police after.
- Filming, profiling, and criminal complaints against community leaders. Years of court dates as punishment.
- Sexual exploitation around camps.
- Guards firing on thieves or trespassers, then the scene being arranged.
- Police or gendarmes killing protesters while logistically supported by a company.
- Spyware and doxxing of journalists and lawyers.
- Dividing communities with selective contracts and jobs, managed by the security and community relations teams together.
- Local guards themselves abused: unpaid wages, altitude shifts without acclimatization, blamed when things go wrong.

## Characters that ring true

The British or Australian country security manager on his fifth posting, tired, honest within limits. The Chilean ex Carabinero who knows every prosecutor. The Colombian ex soldier sending money home, overqualified and underpaid, the only one who has seen real combat and the least eager to see more. The Chinese security supervisor who speaks no Spanish and whose job is mostly keeping his own engineers inside the wire. The Israeli vendor demonstrating a counter drone system to a governor. The local ex colonel on a consulting retainer whose only deliverable is answering his phone. The guard from the village, related to half the people on the blockade. The gendarme commander eating in the company canteen. The analyst in a capital city building network charts of grandmothers. The human rights auditor with two days on site. The gray operator with a pickup, a shotgun, and an invoice that says "logistics." The community's own security: young men with radios and drones who watch the watchers.

## Visual details

Unmarked white pickups with whip flags and roll cages. Guards in fleece and hi vis, not camouflage. Radios, body cameras, breathalyzers. A container office with a wall of screens and a printed map stuck with pins. Police trucks parked inside the company fence. A drone landing pad beside a Pachamama offering. Camera masts with solar panels on the only road for 200 km. A sealed Chinese compound with its own kitchen, basketball hoop, and red banners. Laminated human rights posters in Spanish nobody reads. A lone checkpoint at 4,300 m that is a chain, a hut, and a bored man who decides everything.

## Cut list

These fail the Thorne test for this setting. Any of them can still be used as a declared **License** under the rules above, never by accident.

- Foreign mercenary companies openly armed and patrolling.
- Wagner style deployments with heavy weapons, or foreign troops garrisoning mines. Advisors, trainers, equipment, and sealed compounds are the ceiling.
- Firefights between rival contractors, or US versus Chinese proxies shooting at each other. The contest is contracts, lawsuits, leaks, financing, and influence.
- Private armies defeating state forces, or holding territory against them.
- Contractors assassinating presidents or running coups from a mine camp. Lawfare, funding, and media campaigns are the realistic tools of political pressure.
- Large Chinese armed guard forces. Chinese firms stay unarmed and partner locally, by their own law and by local law.
- Foreign contractors invisible to locals. In places this empty, everyone knows who the outsider is within a week.
- Omniscient surveillance. Coverage is a few roads and gates, connectivity fails, sensors ice over, and people talk around it.
