# Graphic Narrative Storycraft & Visual Storytelling

## Why this skill exists

Use this skill when an LLM is helping a creator conceive, write, script, storyboard, lay out, draw, critique, revise, or finish a comic, manga, graphic novel, sequential illustration, or other page-based visual narrative.

The model must not behave like a prose writer who later decorates the prose with pictures. It must reason like a hybrid of:

- story architect,
- character writer,
- comic-script writer,
- storyboard artist,
- page designer,
- sequential storyteller,
- production-minded illustrator,
- letterer-aware composer,
- and self-editor.

The target is not merely a “good-looking page.” The target is a page or sequence that makes the reader experience the intended story clearly, emotionally, rhythmically, and efficiently.

This skill deliberately preserves detailed lessons and creator-specific heuristics from the source material. When two source workflows differ, do not flatten them into a fake universal rule. Preserve them as alternatives and choose according to the project.

---

# PART I — THE MEDIUM: WHAT A GRAPHIC NARRATIVE IS DOING

## 1. Comics are sequential, not merely illustrated

A graphic narrative gives the reader selected fragments:

- an image,
- then another image,
- a speech balloon,
- a reaction,
- a change in framing,
- a sound effect,
- a gutter,
- a page turn,
- a reveal.

The reader mentally creates continuity between those fragments.

Therefore, when designing a comic, always reason about **what the reader must infer between images**, not only what is literally drawn inside each image.

The blank interval between two panels is not nothing. It is a place where the reader constructs motion, elapsed time, causal connection, impact, or emotional transition.

### Operational rule

For every adjacent pair of panels, be able to answer:

```text
What changed between Panel A and Panel B?
What does the reader have to infer?
Is that inference easy enough to make?
Is the omitted action interesting to imagine, or confusing to reconstruct?
```

Do not draw every animation frame. Select the moments that allow the reader to build the scene.

---

## 2. Text and image form one system

Do not treat dialogue, captions, balloons, and art as independent layers of meaning.

In a good comic:

- the words affect the framing,
- the framing affects the words,
- balloons affect eye movement,
- expressions alter how dialogue is interpreted,
- images can replace exposition,
- captions can add information the drawing cannot efficiently show,
- sound effects can contribute to worldbuilding and physicality.

One source workflow explicitly places text into the thumbnail before finalizing the panel composition because the creator wants to see what the reader will read while deciding what the reader will see.

Another source notes that some sequences may work even better with little or no dialogue.

Treat both as valid.

### LLM behavior

When scripting or storyboarding, do not write the words first and forget the visual cost of those words. Do not draw the page first and squeeze lettering into whatever empty space remains.

Plan them together.

---

## 3. Comics are not movies

Borrow useful concepts from cinema—camera angle, establishing shot, cutting, visual continuity, lens-like perspective—but do not reduce comics to frozen film frames.

A comic page can do things a film frame cannot:

- show multiple moments simultaneously,
- make one moment physically larger than another,
- place images in deliberate spatial relationships,
- use the gutter as time,
- repeat nearly identical images for rhythm,
- compress a long event into one image,
- stretch a tiny action across many panels,
- hide a reveal behind a physical page turn,
- make typography part of the composition,
- use symbolic or abstract backgrounds,
- break or preserve panel borders according to emotion,
- turn an entire spread into one visual event.

Therefore, design for the **page as a medium**, not merely for imaginary camera cuts.

# PART II — THE FULL CREATIVE PIPELINE

## 4. Default process

Use this as the detailed default pipeline:

```text
1. Feed the mental library
2. Capture ideas
3. Identify premise / genre / audience
4. Map the story destination
5. Build the story spine
6. Define characters
7. Establish character-driven causality
8. Set format + page budget
9. Script the story
10. Break the script into pages / spreads
11. Thumbnail / storyboard
12. Revise script from storyboard discoveries
13. Design page turns, pacing, balloon flow, and panel rhythm
14. Build construction drawings / pencils
15. Solve anatomy, acting, perspective, environment, and continuity
16. Ink / finish lines
17. Letter / place balloons / captions / SFX
18. Add flat color / tone
19. Add atmosphere, depth, color grade, and texture when appropriate
20. Review the page in sequence, not in isolation
21. Revise only what matters enough to change
22. Finish and ship
```

This is not a one-way assembly line.

The loop may become:

```text
SCRIPT → STORYBOARD → SCRIPT CHANGE → NEW STORYBOARD
```

or:

```text
ROUGH DRAWING → BETTER VISUAL IDEA → PAGE RESTRUCTURE → DIALOGUE CHANGE
```

The important principle is to make expensive decisions late and cheap decisions early.

# PART III — IDEATION: BUILD SOMETHING TO THINK WITH

## 5. Read → Play → Write

One source frames ideation as a three-part cycle:

### Read = data input

Build a large mental library.

Useful inputs include:

- novels,
- comics,
- manga,
- newspapers,
- essays,
- mythology,
- history,
- biographies,
- visual culture,
- people,
- conversations,
- technical material,
- places,
- images,
- games,
- films,
- animation.

The underlying lesson is not “copy other stories.” It is that creative output needs stored material to recombine.

Short, disposable entertainment can offer references, but it should not become the only input. Depth increases the number and quality of associations available during writing.

### Play = data processing

“Play” includes literal games, but the deeper function is experimentation and synthesis.

Examples:

- tabletop roleplay,
- video games,
- sports strategy,
- card games,
- scenario simulation,
- genre inversion,
- role reversal,
- changing tone,
- recombining motifs,
- asking “what if?” repeatedly.

Use play to test combinations without needing them to become canon.

### Write = data output

Capture fragments before they vanish.

The fragment can be:

- one line of dialogue,
- a setting,
- an image,
- a character flaw,
- a possible ending,
- a strange object,
- a conflict,
- a joke,
- a visual reveal,
- a page-turn idea.

Do not demand a complete story before recording an idea.

### LLM instruction

When brainstorming with the user, maintain an **idea bank** rather than prematurely forcing every fragment into one polished pitch.

A useful internal or visible structure is:

```text
IDEA FRAGMENT
SOURCE / INSPIRATION
WHAT MAKES IT INTERESTING
POSSIBLE CONNECTIONS
QUESTIONS IT OPENS
```

---

## 6. Remixing can begin with a genre shift

The source gives an example of taking mythology normally presented through horror or tragic framing and moving it into fantasy-comedy-adventure while preserving recognizable mythological material.

The transferable lesson:

When looking for a new angle, preserve one layer and change another.

Possible transformations:

```text
same mythology → different genre
same plot type → different cultural setting
same role → opposite personality
same event → different point of view
same character desire → different social world
same world → different emotional tone
```

Do not mistake originality for having no influences. Originality often emerges from an unusual relationship among existing elements.

# PART IV — STORY MAPPING: KNOW WHERE YOU ARE GOING

## 7. Start point and end point

One source uses a navigation analogy: if you know where you are and where you are going, you can reason about the route.

Apply that to narrative.

Before committing to detailed production, define:

```text
STARTING STATE
ENDING STATE
WHAT CHANGES BETWEEN THEM
WHO CAUSES THE CHANGE
WHAT THE STORY COSTS THE CHARACTERS
```

Do not make “I’ll see where it goes” the only plan once the work reaches production.

Exploratory writing is allowed. But once the story becomes a graphic-production project, uncertainty has a cost in pages, art, lettering, continuity, and revision.

---

## 8. Know the ending before production

Another source elevates this into a working commandment: know the ending before you start the full comic.

Why?

Because the ending changes how you understand:

- setup,
- foreshadowing,
- early character behavior,
- thematic echoes,
- midpoint choices,
- the “mushy middle,”
- the meaning of visual motifs.

If you are illustrating another writer’s completed script, read the entire script before drawing the early pages.

The first pages may contain gestures or objects whose importance is only obvious after the ending is known.

# PART V — THREE-ACT STORY MAP, IN THE SOURCE’S OWN LOGIC

## 9. Act I: setup

The source’s Act I map contains five useful concepts.

### 9.1 Balance

Show what “normal” means in this story world.

Balance does not need to mean happiness. It means the existing condition before the central disruption.

Questions:

```text
What is ordinary here?
What do the characters accept as normal?
What social / emotional / political arrangement is stable enough to be disrupted?
```

### 9.2 Imbalance

Introduce the force that breaks the existing condition.

The imbalance should alter the story’s trajectory rather than merely add decoration.

### 9.3 Introduce the protagonist

The protagonist can appear before becoming heroic.

The source explicitly distinguishes **introducing the hero** from the moment the character actually chooses to act like one.

### 9.4 Catalyst

The protagonist encounters the problem in a way that produces personal understanding or pressure.

A catalyst can create the thought:

> Something is wrong. Something has to be done.

The protagonist does not yet need to know exactly what they will do or believe they are capable of doing it.

### 9.5 Call to action / commitment

The character crosses from awareness toward decision.

Do not require the commitment to be grand.

The important shift is from:

```text
I see the problem.
```

to:

```text
I will take some responsibility for what happens next.
```

---

## 10. Worked example: Lakan / Mai-style setup

Preserve this reasoning pattern as an example for the model.

The source describes:

1. A world in which different races have an existing balance.
2. A dark force disrupts that balance.
3. Lakan is introduced; he is not yet functioning as a fully committed hero.
4. He personally experiences capture and witnesses civilian suffering.
5. He reaches the internal conclusion that the situation cannot simply continue.
6. He also feels weak and inadequate: he recognizes the problem before believing he can solve it.

The value of this example is the order.

The catalyst is not merely “something explodes.” It changes the protagonist’s relationship to the problem.

### LLM check

When proposing an inciting incident, ask:

```text
Does this merely happen near the protagonist?
Or does it alter what the protagonist now knows, wants, fears, or feels responsible for?
```

---

## 11. Act II: confrontation, despair, aid, decision, action

The source gives Act II a very character-centered progression.

### 11.1 Despair / low state

The protagonist may want to help but feel unable to act.

Sources of low state can include:

- guilt,
- failure,
- injury,
- lack of power,
- ignorance,
- imprisonment,
- shame,
- fear,
- consequences of an earlier mistake.

### 11.2 Aid

Help appears.

Crucially, **aid does not have to be a person**.

It can be:

- a mentor,
- an ally,
- an object,
- a weapon,
- a place,
- a plant,
- information,
- a route,
- a discovery,
- a coincidence that creates an opening.

### 11.3 Decision

Aid creates possibility; the protagonist still has to choose.

Do not let the helper solve the protagonist’s moral or narrative problem for them.

### 11.4 Action

The protagonist takes the risk.

The source emphasizes that the first real act does not have to be enormous.

A small action can matter because it proves agency.

---

## 12. Worked example: rescue as first meaningful agency

The source’s example puts the protagonist in captivity and despair. A rescuer creates an opportunity. The protagonist then has a choice: stay passive or help despite being weak.

Later, when someone is in immediate danger, the protagonist dives in to save them.

The important lesson is not the literal rescue. It is:

```text
DESPAIR
→ AID CREATES POSSIBILITY
→ CHARACTER CHOOSES
→ CHARACTER RISKS SOMETHING
→ AGENCY IS PROVEN THROUGH ACTION
```

When the model writes Act II, use this causal structure rather than treating the middle as “more obstacles happen.”

---

## 13. Act III: outcome, closure, new balance

The source explicitly allows multiple kinds of outcome:

- protagonist wins and gains the desired thing,
- protagonist wins but loses the desired prize,
- protagonist fails but gains something else,
- protagonist loses,
- protagonist dies,
- antagonist wins.

Do not default every story to conventional victory.

### Closure = new balance

After the climax, show what “normal” becomes.

Ask:

```text
What has permanently changed?
What does the character now understand?
What does society now do differently?
Which relationship has changed?
Which old behavior is no longer possible?
```

One source prefers the new balance to represent growth relative to the old one—for example, formerly passive people becoming proactive or more self-aware. Treat that as a preferred progression, not a universal law for tragedy or horror.

---

## 14. Cliffhanger after closure

The source treats the cliffhanger as something that can appear **after the current story has earned closure**.

Use it to create anticipation, not to avoid resolving the story.

The source also frames continuation demand as a kind of audience test: if readers care about the final unresolved element, it can indicate appetite for more.

### Distinguish

```text
BAD:
The current story simply stops before resolving its central problem.

BETTER:
The current story resolves, establishes a new balance, then introduces a fresh uncertainty.
```

# PART VI — STORY ANCHORS AND LENGTH CONTROL

## 15. Anchor beats

One source shows a more granular map using anchor points such as:

- beginning,
- inciting incident,
- second thought / hesitation,
- obstacle one,
- obstacle two,
- midpoint twist,
- later obstacle,
- disaster,
- crisis,
- climax,
- descending action,
- ending.

Use anchor beats to answer:

```text
What must happen?
In what rough order?
How much page-space can each beat afford?
Where does the story accelerate or breathe?
```

Do not treat labels as mandatory. Their function is navigational.

# PART VII — CHARACTERS: PERSONALITY MUST GENERATE PLOT

## 16. Character design includes personality, not only appearance

A character sheet can include:

```text
Name
Age
Height / build
Silhouette
Front view
Side view
Back view
Costume
Color tendencies
Distinct visual markers
Personality
Values
Fears
Desires
Habits
Strengths
Weaknesses
```

The source uses a 360-degree visual model plus personality details.

The important principle: the design should help the reader recognize who the person is, not merely what clothes they wear.

A smiling, brightly colored, open posture may support an outgoing personality; a closed silhouette may support another temperament.

Do not force visual symbolism when it is unnecessary, but exploit it when useful.

---

## 17. Expression library

The source recommends designing major characters in six basic emotional expressions:

- happy,
- sad,
- fearful,
- angry,
- disgusted,
- surprised.

Use this as a practical acting reference.

The deeper lesson is to know **how this specific character looks when feeling each emotion**.

A restrained character’s “angry” face should not automatically look like an explosive character’s angry face.

---

## 18. Protagonist and antagonist drive the story

This is one of the most important lessons in the material.

The plot should behave like a snowball of choices and consequences.

Use:

```text
CHARACTER DECIDES X
→ X produces CONSEQUENCE Y
→ other people react to Y
→ protagonist / antagonist reacts to those reactions
→ that new decision creates CONSEQUENCE Z
```

Avoid:

```text
The author needs the characters at Location B,
so a random event forces them to Location B.
```

Repeated author-forced movement produces a passive cast and a mechanical plot.

### LLM diagnostic

For every major beat ask:

```text
Whose decision caused this?
Why would that character make this decision?
What new consequence follows specifically from that decision?
```

If no character can answer, the beat may be plot puppetry.

---

## 19. Beginner antagonist contrast

The source suggests a beginner exercise: make protagonist and antagonist strongly opposed in personality or moral position.

This is useful because it makes conflict legible.

After the writer gains confidence, introduce complexity:

- good character with real faults,
- antagonist with genuine virtues,
- overlapping motives,
- moral compromise.

Do not confuse moral ambiguity with characters who have no clear motivation.

---

## 20. Supporting-role vocabulary from the source

### Deuteragonist
A second major character who accompanies, assists, contrasts, or complicates the protagonist.

### “Muse” / object of desire
The source uses “muse” for the thing around which desire revolves. It may be a person, object, place, feeling, goal, or even something the protagonist wants to destroy.

Use the term only if it helps the user; the functional question is:

> What does this character long for, seek, protect, obtain, destroy, or understand?

### Mentor
A guide who helps the protagonist discover a path, ability, calling, or destination.

The mentor can guide; the protagonist should still have to achieve the goal.

### Narrator
Use narration sparingly. Do not narrate visible information redundantly.

### Secondary characters
They can create subplots and world depth, but do not expand every secondary character before the core story has enough demand to support it.

---

# PART VIII — SHOW, DON’T DUPLICATE

## 21. Narration must add something the art does not already say

If the panel clearly shows two characters overlooking rice terraces at sunset, a caption that says “they looked over the rice terraces at sunset” wastes space.

Use captions instead for:

- internal thought,
- time jump,
- location not obvious from art,
- contradiction,
- thematic commentary,
- memory,
- information outside the frame,
- voiceover with additional meaning.

### LLM test

For every caption:

```text
If this caption disappeared, would the image already communicate the same information?
```

If yes, cut or transform it unless repetition is intentionally rhetorical.

---

# PART IX — AUDIENCE, FORMAT, PAGE BUDGET

## 22. Know who the story is for

Page count, density, text load, subject matter, and pacing depend partly on audience and format.

Do not assume one length fits children, teens, and adults equally.

Before detailed scripting, identify:

```text
Audience
Format
Chapter / issue length
Print vs digital
Reading direction
Page dimensions
Color vs monochrome
Production schedule
```

---

## 23. Page limits can improve storytelling

One source explicitly uses page limits to force better decisions.

If an event “should not take more than six pages,” and it grows beyond that, ask whether:

- the scene is dragging,
- two beats can combine,
- dialogue repeats information,
- a panel is unnecessary,
- the event truly needs more space.

Sometimes the correct solution is to add a page.

Sometimes the correct solution is to cut.

The important thing is to **notice the cost** rather than let the sequence expand accidentally.

---

# PART X — THINK IN SPREADS AND PAGE TURNS

## 24. A spread is a storytelling unit

For a printed comic, two facing pages are experienced together.

Therefore, track:

```text
LEFT PAGE
RIGHT PAGE
CENTER GUTTER
PAGE-TURN AFTER RIGHT PAGE
```

Do not plan only one isolated page at a time.

A page can read well alone but fail when paired with its facing page.

---

## 25. Build tension toward the page turn

One source recommends structuring a left-to-right spread so tension can develop across the left and right page, then release or transform after the turn.

Example technique from the source:

```text
panels progressively become smaller / denser
→ tension increases
→ reader turns page
→ large reveal panel appears
```

This is not a formula to repeat constantly.

Use it when justified.

---

## 26. Right-page cliffhangers

For left-to-right print reading, the source recommends placing important end-of-spread hooks on the right-hand page so the physical act of turning reveals the answer.

Possible hook types:

- someone starts running,
- a character sees something off-panel,
- a hand reaches toward something,
- a threat appears,
- dialogue raises a question,
- a character is about to enter,
- an action begins but the result is withheld.

Next page:

- answer,
- escalate,
- reverse,
- or complicate.

---

## 27. Page-turn example: “Where are they?” → running

A source page analysis provides a useful micro-model.

Previous page ends with the question: where did the main characters go?

The next page answers by showing them running far away.

That page then has additional jobs:

1. confirm they escaped,
2. establish where they now are,
3. show speed and distance,
4. allow the characters to begin interacting after the chase,
5. move them toward a descent into another part of the station,
6. hand the story into the next page, where pursuit continues.

The point is that the page is not “a page with people running.”

It is a **bridge with multiple narrative responsibilities**.

### LLM page-intent template

```text
QUESTION / ACTION ENTERING PAGE:

PRIMARY ANSWER:

SECONDARY STORY JOB:

CHARACTER / RELATIONSHIP JOB:

SPATIAL JOB:

HOOK / TRANSITION OUT:
```

# PART XI — SCRIPT WRITING

## 28. Scripting comes after ideation, map, and character work

The source explicitly warns against treating scripting as the first step.

Without premise, destination, and character understanding, the writer is more likely to stall while asking:

- what happens next?
- why is the character here?
- what is the point of this page?

Therefore, build enough upstream structure before generating panel-by-panel script.

---

## 29. What a comic script needs to communicate

At minimum, a practical comic script should make these clear:

```text
PAGE NUMBER
PANEL COUNT
PANEL NUMBER
WHAT THE READER SEES
WHO IS PRESENT
IMPORTANT ACTION / EXPRESSION
DIALOGUE
CAPTION
SOUND EFFECT
SPECIAL BALLOON OR LETTERING NOTE, IF NEEDED
```

The script is not the final artwork.

Its job is to transfer intent into something the artist and letterer can interpret.

---

## 30. There is no single universal comic-script format

One source emphasizes that comic scripting does not have the same universal format discipline as film screenwriting.

Different publishers, writers, artists, and teams may use different conventions.

What matters consistently is that the script is:

- clear,
- concise,
- organized,
- readable,
- internally consistent,
- easy to collaborate from.

Do not switch formatting conventions halfway through one script.

---

## 31. Collaboration changes how much description is needed

If writer and artist are the same person, the creator may use extremely abbreviated notes because the missing visual information already exists in their own head.

If the writer is handing the work to another artist, descriptions need to transfer enough information that both people are imagining compatible scenes.

### LLM behavior

Before generating a script, infer or ask when necessary:

```text
Is this script for the user alone?
For an artist collaborator?
For a publisher submission?
For a team pipeline?
```

Then adjust specificity.

---

## 32. Formal-format example: publisher-style page/panel script

A source uses a Dark Horse-style example to explain one professional format.

The relevant transferable conventions include:

- cover page for submission information,
- title / issue / writer information in the script header,
- explicit page number,
- explicit panel count,
- numbered panels,
- visual description,
- named speaker,
- numbered captions when several appear,
- layout clarity for letterers.

Do not claim these are universal requirements unless the target publisher actually requires them.

---

## 33. Numbering captions can help lettering order

If a panel has several captions, numbering them can clarify intended reading sequence.

For example:

```text
CAPTION 1
...

CAPTION 2
...

CAPTION 3
...
```

This is especially useful when the letterer is a separate collaborator.

---

## 34. “~25 words” as a practical density warning, not a universal law

One source cites a publisher-style guideline of keeping an individual caption / thought balloon / speech balloon around 25 words or fewer, splitting longer material into additional balloons when necessary.

Preserve the lesson correctly:

- long balloons can visually clog the page,
- text has physical area,
- multiple shorter units can improve flow,
- exact limits depend on format and publisher.

Do **not** enforce 25 words as a global rule.

---

## 35. Draft first, format later

One source describes writing a first pass quickly—page, panel, ideas—then later cleaning typography, bolding, spacing, labels, and organization.

Use this as a productivity pattern:

```text
PASS 1: get the narrative and panel logic out
PASS 2: revise story
PASS 3: clean formatting / collaboration readability
```

Do not interrupt every creative sentence to perfect document formatting.

---

## 36. Canonical LLM comic-script format

When no publisher-specific format is requested, use this consistent internal format:

```text
PAGE 7 — 5 PANELS
PAGE INTENT: The chase stops; mistrust becomes dialogue; Tempo offers a route downward.

PANEL 1 — WIDE / ACTION
Three characters sprint through the station concourse. Strong depth: front runner large, rear runner small. Background cues identify the station but remain subordinate.

SFX: THUD THUD THUD

PANEL 2 — MEDIUM
They skid to a halt. [Character] turns on [Character], frustrated.

CHARACTER A
Dialogue...

PANEL 3 — CLOSE REACTION
...
```

Descriptions should be visual and actionable, not novelistic unless the prose conveys an important feeling the artist needs to understand.

---

# PART XII — BALLOON TYPES AND LETTERING ELEMENTS

## 37. Useful source vocabulary

The source distinguishes:

- narration box,
- speech / talk balloon,
- thought balloon,
- shout balloon,
- telepathic / disembodied speech treatment.

The exact graphic style is flexible.

The functional requirement is that readers can distinguish modes of communication.

Do not add five balloon styles merely because they exist. Use them when the narrative has distinct communication states to encode.

---

# PART XIII — STORYBOARDING: THE BLUEPRINT

## 38. Storyboard before expensive art

The storyboard can be ugly.

That is a feature.

It exists to test:

- panel sizes,
- reading order,
- staging,
- camera choices,
- balloon placement,
- emphasis,
- page turns,
- spreads,
- rhythm,
- reveal logic.

If an angle fails, redraw a scribble—not six hours of finished anatomy and rendering.

---

## 39. Storyboards are page architecture

One source defines comic storyboards as blueprints for:

- how panels stack on a page,
- how pages sit beside each other,
- what is left vs right,
- where the page turn happens.

Therefore, a storyboard is more than a set of miniature illustrations.

It is a **map of reader movement**.

---

## 40. Storyboard fidelity should stay low until decisions stabilize

Use:

- stick figures,
- boxes,
- arrows,
- silhouettes,
- simple perspective,
- balloon placeholders,
- short notes.

Do not reward the model for producing pretty thumbnails that hide unresolved storytelling.

---

## 41. Storyboard ↔ script feedback loop

One source describes changing entire dialogue passages or fight scenes because the storyboard revealed a better way to stage the sequence.

Treat that as normal.

### Review loop

```text
1. Script scene.
2. Thumbnail it.
3. Notice dead space, excess dialogue, confusing action, bad page turn, or better visual idea.
4. Revise the script.
5. Thumbnail again.
6. Repeat until the story and page architecture support each other.
```

Do not protect the script from improvement merely because it was written first.

---

# PART XIV — PRINT-AWARE PAGE PLANNING

## 42. Know left/right placement before final art

For print work, establish whether the chapter begins on:

- left page,
- right page,
- or after an intentionally blank page.

This affects every later spread and reveal.

---

## 43. Live area, trim, bleed, and gutter

One source explicitly plans pages around printer boundaries.

### Live area
Keep essential text and must-see visual information safely inside.

### Trim
The physical cut may vary slightly. Do not place essential information on the trim line.

### Bleed
Art can extend beyond the trim so no accidental white edge appears after cutting.

### Gutter
The inner meeting area of facing pages can swallow information, especially in bound books.

### LLM rule

If generating print-layout guidance, never position essential words or critical facial details near trim or gutter without a deliberate reason.

Printer specifications vary; use the actual printer/publisher template when available.

---

## 44. Sound effects may tolerate risk differently from dialogue

A source notes that decorative sound-effect lettering may sometimes extend closer to bleed/trim than essential dialogue, depending on the design and production rules.

This is not permission to ignore print safety.

It is a hierarchy:

```text
critical dialogue / essential information → safest
important art → safe
decorative / sacrificial edges → may bleed
```

---

# PART XV — PANEL DESIGN AND PACING

## 45. Panels are units of perceived time

A source calls panels the comic’s unit of time measurement.

Use panel structure to alter pace.

### Many small panels can:

- break one action into micro-beats,
- create hesitation,
- slow a tiny moment,
- produce rapid rhythm,
- create comedy through timing,
- increase tension through incremental revelation.

### Few large panels can:

- create spectacle,
- create stillness,
- emphasize a moment,
- give environment room,
- make the reader linger.

There is no universal “large = slow” formula independent of content, but physical size and count strongly influence reading experience.

---

## 46. One artist’s panel-count heuristic

One source says they become cautious around six panels on a page and strongly question the page if it approaches ten.

Preserve this correctly as a **personal production heuristic**, not a law.

The valuable reasoning is:

```text
If panel count is very high, ask whether:
- two panels can combine,
- the comic is accidentally becoming an animation storyboard,
- each panel captures a meaningful beat,
- the page has enough room for readable text and art.
```

Exceptions are valid, including comedy, stylistic repetition, or deliberate keyframe-like sequences.

---

## 47. Do not storyboard every footstep

Comics usually need the important action beats, not every intermediate pose.

Weak sequence:

```text
hand reaches
hand moves closer
hand touches handle
hand grips handle
hand turns handle
hand pulls
```

unless the story needs extreme suspense or timing.

Stronger default:

```text
character reaches
→ next panel: door opens / consequence appears
```

The reader performs closure.

---

## 48. Add panels when the sequence is moving too fast

The source also gives the reverse lesson: sometimes an event needs **more** panels to breathe.

Examples:

- show the alarm affecting the whole dorm rather than only one room,
- add an exterior / wider context image,
- return to the character after establishing larger consequence.

The rule is not “fewer panels.”

The rule is “the right number of meaningful beats.”

---

## 49. Panel shape can reflect emotional order

Source-derived options:

### Calm / orderly scene
Use:

- regular grid,
- stable borders,
- predictable spacing,
- restrained composition.

### Chaotic / intense scene
Possible tools:

- diagonal cuts,
- uneven panels,
- border breaks,
- bleed,
- overlapping elements,
- compressed rhythm.

Do not distort the page merely to demonstrate design skill.

Formal chaos should express narrative chaos.

---

# PART XVI — READING ORDER

## 50. Respect the reading convention

For a conventional Western left-to-right comic, the source describes a horizontal-first, then vertical reading flow—often summarized as a Z-like movement.

For manga / right-to-left reading, reverse accordingly.

Never make the reader decode the basic order for novelty.

### Diagnostic

Show a thumbnail to an imagined first-time reader and ask:

```text
Where does the eye go first?
What does it read second?
Could two panels plausibly compete for “next”?
Could a balloon pull the eye into the wrong panel?
```

If yes, fix hierarchy.

---

# PART XVII — BALLOONS ARE COMPOSITIONAL OBJECTS

## 51. Word balloons are high-contrast anchors

One source ranks balloons as a major tactical priority because they strongly attract the eye.

Use that attraction deliberately.

Plan:

- balloon position,
- tail direction,
- sequence,
- empty space,
- interaction with faces,
- interaction with panel borders,
- relation to reading flow.

A reader should not have to reread a panel because the dialogue order is unclear.

---

## 52. Text-first thumbnailing as one workflow option

One creator’s workflow:

1. place final or near-final text,
2. position balloons,
3. build panels and action around that text,
4. draw expressions while seeing the actual lines being spoken.

This can work especially well for writer-artists.

Do not force it when:

- an artist receives a locked script from someone else,
- the sequence is primarily silent,
- exploratory visuals need to precede dialogue.

---

# PART XVIII — CAMERA AND SHOT CHOICE

## 53. Every angle needs a reason

Do not fill a conversation with repeated headshots.

Ask for every panel:

```text
Why this distance?
Why this angle?
Why this body part?
Why this background?
Why this moment?
```

Possible functions:

### Wide / establishing
- locate characters,
- show relation to environment,
- show scale,
- reset geography.

### Medium
- show body language,
- show interaction,
- balance acting and context.

### Close-up
- emphasize expression,
- emotional detail,
- important object,
- information restriction.

### Extreme close-up
- isolate a clue,
- intensify sensation,
- control attention.

### High angle
- vulnerability,
- geography,
- surveillance,
- pattern.

### Low angle
- dominance,
- threat,
- monumentality.

### Distorted / wide-angle perspective
- speed,
- exaggeration,
- energetic depth.

Do not assign emotional meaning mechanically. Context determines interpretation.

---

## 54. Talking scene visual variety

A source gives a useful coffee-shop example.

Two characters can continue the same dialogue while the images move through:

- establishing view of the coffee shop,
- headshot,
- reaction,
- eye close-up,
- mouth close-up,
- coffee cup with steam,
- background patron,
- relevant destination / object being discussed.

The visual does not need to show the speaker’s face every time.

But it should remain connected to:

- dialogue,
- atmosphere,
- character psychology,
- or story information.

Avoid random B-roll.

---

# PART XIX — ESTABLISHING SHOTS AND ORIENTATION

## 55. Establish where the scene occurs

One source makes this a strong rule: a scene needs spatial context unless the lack of context is itself an intentional reveal.

Reader confusion about geography is usually not productive mystery.

Ask:

```text
Where are they?
How are they arranged?
Where can they move?
What is the environment doing to the scene?
```

Establish early enough that later close shots make sense.

---

## 56. Connector panels

A page may contain several tight shots that individually lack context.

Insert one panel that reconnects:

- a character,
- an action,
- and a recognizable environmental element.

This “connector panel” can carry spatial continuity for surrounding close-ups.

---

# PART XX — BACKGROUNDS: COMMUNICATE, DON’T COMPETE

## 57. Think theater stage, not endlessly rendered movie set

A source recommends adding enough detail to sell the setting, then allowing the reader to remember it.

Once the environment is established, every panel does not require maximal rendering.

Prioritize:

- location clarity,
- action clarity,
- depth,
- recognizable motifs,
- interaction with environment.

Do not let a background steal attention from the actual narrative focal point unless the environment is the subject.

---

## 58. Visual shorthand for location

A professional page example repeats a small set of iconic environment cues—specific shapes, greenery / planter-like elements, canopy, space view—to tell the reader “we are still on this station.”

The lesson:

Create a **visual vocabulary** for each location.

Possible shorthand:

```text
architecture silhouette
window geometry
signage
vegetation type
floor pattern
lighting
machines
color family
sky / exterior condition
```

Reuse enough of that vocabulary to preserve continuity without redrawing the entire environment.

---

## 59. Foreground, middleground, background

Even simple drawings can feel spatially rich when they include layered depth.

Use layers to create:

- overlap,
- scale change,
- separation,
- environmental immersion.

Do not interpret this as “every panel needs three literal layers.”

Use it as a depth-building tool when scenes feel flat.

---

# PART XXI — ACTION AND DYNAMISM

## 60. Sell an idea with several reinforcing signals

A professional example tries to sell “these characters are running fast.”

Instead of depending on perfect anatomy, it combines:

- front character much larger than rear character,
- strong depth,
- exaggerated perspective,
- wide-angle feeling,
- dynamic poses,
- capes / tentacles / loose elements flying backward,
- speed lines,
- environmental movement,
- diagonals.

The important meta-lesson:

If a panel must communicate a concept strongly, do not rely on one fragile cue.

Use multiple compatible signals.

---

## 61. Character design can support motion readability

Movable costume or body elements—cape, coat, hair, ribbons, tentacles—can visibly react to motion.

They can:

- clarify direction,
- amplify speed,
- show wind,
- dramatize gesture,
- make silhouettes more expressive.

This does not mean every character needs a cape.

It means design can anticipate sequential storytelling needs.

---

# PART XXII — CONSTRUCTION DRAWING: WHERE THE HEAVY LIFTING HAPPENS

## 62. The construction stage converts intent into solvable drawing problems

Use the rough/pencil phase to solve:

- anatomy,
- pose,
- facial expression,
- foreground / background relationships,
- perspective,
- ellipses,
- props,
- balloon space,
- staging,
- environment interaction,
- continuity.

The source emphasizes that much of the real work happens here, not in polishing the line.

---

## 63. Construction detail depends on confidence and difficulty

The amount of underdrawing can vary panel by panel.

Use more construction when:

- unfamiliar anatomy,
- difficult hand,
- complex foreshortening,
- new costume,
- difficult perspective,
- important expression,
- important mechanical object.

Use less when:

- the pose is familiar,
- the object is simple,
- the creator can reliably finish from a loose indication.

Do not impose one construction density on the whole book.

---

## 64. Accuracy has a hierarchy

A professional page may tolerate a slightly imperfect leg but fail if a major ellipse or perspective structure is visibly wrong.

Ask:

```text
What will the reader actually notice?
What has to be structurally convincing?
What can stay loose without harming the story?
```

Examples of high-value accuracy:

- major ellipses,
- strong perspective lines,
- eye direction,
- silhouette,
- face readability,
- object interaction,
- location continuity.

If a ruler / ellipse tool solves an obvious geometric problem, use it.

Do not waste hand-drawing purity on an error the tool can remove quickly.

---

## 65. Interaction with the environment sells the scene

A small detail—such as a cape catching or resting on the edge while a character climbs down—can make a figure feel physically connected to the environment.

Look for contact:

- feet on surfaces,
- hands gripping objects,
- clothing reacting to edges,
- shadows connecting figure and ground,
- debris reacting to movement.

These cues can be more convincing than polishing isolated anatomy.

---

# PART XXIII — PRODUCTION EFFICIENCY

## 66. Be brutally selective under schedule

Professional comics often operate under deadlines.

The source repeatedly asks:

> What does this panel need to communicate, and what is the fastest reliable way to communicate it?

This is not permission for carelessness.

It is hierarchy.

Spend time where the reader receives value.

Possible hierarchy:

```text
story clarity
character acting
readability
important geometry
focal object
continuity
supporting background
micro-detail
```

---

## 67. “Clarity over cool”

A complex composition that confuses the sequence is worse than a simple composition that tells the story.

Before approving an experimental page, verify:

- panel order is obvious,
- focal point is obvious,
- speakers are obvious,
- geography is understandable,
- action is understandable,
- page turn still works.

If the experiment passes, keep it.

If it fails, simplify.

---

# PART XXIV — COLOR, TONE, ATMOSPHERE

## 68. Color is storytelling

Color can control:

- mood,
- atmosphere,
- environmental depth,
- scene transition,
- character emphasis,
- emotional progression.

Do not treat color as decoration applied after storytelling is complete.

---

## 69. Build color in layers when useful

One professional workflow described in the source uses a simple sequence:

```text
line drawing
→ flat colors
→ atmospheric separation
→ color grade / gradients
→ texture
```

This is one workflow, not a requirement.

The deeper lesson is to separate functions so they can be adjusted independently.

---

## 70. Atmospheric perspective

The source uses color gradients to separate distant elements from nearer ones.

Use atmospheric separation to help:

- depth read quickly,
- background recede,
- characters remain focal,
- large space feel large.

Do not overcomplicate the physics if the stylized visual system communicates clearly.

A rationalized visual language can be more valuable than literal simulation.

---

## 71. Page-level color progression

A gradient or color shift can help the page move emotionally from top to bottom or from one beat to another.

For example:

```text
cooler / safer
→ warmer / more dangerous
```

Do not apply a gradient just because the page looks empty.

Tie the change to story intent.

---

## 72. Texture is subordinate

Noise and paper texture can help a digital page feel less sterile, but they are finishing tools.

They do not repair:

- bad pacing,
- unclear anatomy,
- confusing reading order,
- weak staging.

Do not let surface treatment masquerade as storytelling.

---

# PART XXV — SOUND EFFECTS

## 73. SFX are not an afterthought

Sound effects can contribute to:

- force,
- rhythm,
- material quality,
- scale,
- machinery,
- environment,
- comedy,
- impact,
- worldbuilding.

Plan their visual character and placement.

A small whispering SFX should not look like an explosion unless the mismatch is intentional.

---

# PART XXVI — WORKED PAGE-TURN / STORYBOARD EXAMPLE

## 74. Hidden listener reveal

One storyboard source describes a sequence in which two less-important characters talk while a known character is secretly listening.

The storyboard decisions include:

- keep the conversation visually varied,
- show environment and destination imagery related to what is being discussed,
- place the hidden listener small in the background before full reveal,
- use the page turn to reveal that the listener is a known character,
- then use a clearer larger image to identify them,
- continue the panels so the reveal generates the next action.

The lesson:

A reveal can be prepared at several scales:

```text
background clue
→ page turn
→ identification shot
→ consequence
```

This is stronger than introducing the reveal with no visual setup.

---

# PART XXVII — COMEDY AND SPECIAL RHYTHMS

## 75. Small repeated panels can create gag timing

One source sometimes uses tiny side-by-side panels and simplified / chibi acting for comedy.

This can mimic keyframe-like timing deliberately.

Use it when the repetition itself is funny.

Do not copy the device into serious action automatically.

---

# PART XXVIII — STUDY OTHER MEDIA BY DECONSTRUCTING DECISIONS

## 76. Do not consume references passively

A source recommends comparing scripts with finished media and asking why the creator made specific decisions.

When studying another work, ask:

```text
Why this page break?
Why this shot?
Why hide the background here?
Why reveal the character now?
Why use one large panel instead of three?
Why does the dialogue continue over an object shot?
Why is this sequence silent?
```

The goal is not imitation. It is learning decision logic.

---

# PART XXIX — THE 12 SOURCE-DERIVED COMMANDMENTS, EXPANDED

## 77. Commandment 1 — Finish the comic

The source treats this as the only commandment that is essentially non-negotiable.

A finished imperfect comic teaches:

- pacing,
- continuity,
- production reality,
- endurance,
- what readers actually notice,
- what takes too long,
- what can be simplified.

An unfinished masterpiece plan teaches less about completion.

### LLM instruction

Do not keep escalating scope when a simpler solution would allow the user to finish.

---

## 78. Commandment 2 — Word balloons are king

Because balloons often have strong contrast and attract the eye, use them to reinforce flow rather than fight it.

---

## 79. Commandment 3 — Respect the reading path

Default conventions exist because readers process them automatically.

Break them only with a compelling, readable reason.

---

## 80. Commandment 4 — Clarity over cool

Never sacrifice comprehension merely to show off page design.

---

## 81. Commandment 5 — Use whatever tool works

Traditional vs digital purity is secondary to:

- finishing,
- consistency,
- clarity,
- appropriate quality.

---

## 82. Commandment 6 — Know the ending before production

Already detailed above. This is especially important for visual foreshadowing and pacing.

---

## 83. Commandment 7 — Panels control pace

Already detailed above. Treat page architecture as time architecture.

---

## 84. Commandment 8 — Sound effects are storytelling

Already detailed above. Integrate them with tone and world.

---

## 85. Commandment 9 — Establish the scene

Do not let the reader become spatially lost unless disorientation is the intended experience.

---

## 86. Commandment 10 — Comics are not movies

Exploit page-native possibilities.

---

## 87. Commandment 11 — Think theater stage, not movie set

Show enough environment to make the scene function; let reader memory and imagination carry the rest when appropriate.

---

## 88. Commandment 12 — Color controls mood

Already detailed above. Make palette decisions narratively.

# PART XXX — SOURCE HEURISTICS THAT MUST NOT BE TURNED INTO FALSE LAWS

## 89. Preserve specificity and caveats

The model must distinguish among:

### Strong cross-source principle
Example: clarity matters; page turns matter; storyboards reduce expensive revision.

### Creator preference
Example: one artist prefers fewer than six panels and becomes cautious above that.

### Publisher / format guideline
Example: an example script system recommends roughly 25 words or fewer per text unit.

### Context-specific production method
Example: one writer-artist places text first in Photoshop; another may work from a separate script.

### Optional structural model
Example: three-act mapping.

Do not convert all five categories into “rules.”

# PART XXXI — LLM CREATION PROCEDURE

## 90. When asked to develop a new graphic novel concept

Do not jump directly to page one.

Use:

### Stage A — idea material

```text
PREMISE FRAGMENTS
GENRE / TONAL TRANSFORMATIONS
VISUAL MOTIFS
CHARACTER SEEDS
CONFLICT SEEDS
ENDING POSSIBILITIES
```

### Stage B — destination

```text
STARTING BALANCE
IMBALANCE
ENDING
NEW BALANCE
```

### Stage C — characters

```text
PROTAGONIST
ANTAGONIST / OPPOSING FORCE
DEUTERAGONIST IF NEEDED
OBJECT OF DESIRE / MUSE
MENTOR IF NEEDED
```

### Stage D — causality

Write the main chain as:

```text
decision → consequence → reaction → next decision
```

### Stage E — story map

Build acts / anchors.

### Stage F — page budget

Estimate how many pages each major beat receives.

Only then move into detailed scripting.

---

## 91. When asked to write a scene

First define:

```text
SCENE PURPOSE
ENTERING QUESTION / PRESSURE
CHARACTER OBJECTIVE
CONFLICT
CHANGE BY END OF SCENE
EXIT HOOK
```

Then convert to page beats.

---

## 92. When asked to script pages

For each page, include:

```text
PAGE NUMBER
LEFT/RIGHT POSITION IF KNOWN
PAGE INTENT
PANEL COUNT
PANEL-BY-PANEL VISUALS
DIALOGUE / CAPTIONS / SFX
PAGE-TURN FUNCTION
```

Do not describe invisible internal emotion as though the artist can literally draw it. Translate internal state into:

- expression,
- posture,
- action,
- environment,
- or caption/dialogue.

---

## 93. When asked to storyboard

For each spread, reason about:

```text
READING DIRECTION
LEFT PAGE / RIGHT PAGE
DOMINANT PANEL
BALLOON PATH
PANEL RHYTHM
ESTABLISHING INFORMATION
WHERE BACKGROUND IS REQUIRED
WHERE BACKGROUND CAN DROP OUT
PAGE-TURN REVEAL
```

Do not spend tokens describing surface rendering before the layout works.

---

## 94. When asked to critique a page

Use this order:

### 1. Narrative job
What is the page supposed to do?

### 2. Sequence clarity
Can the reader follow the action and reading order?

### 3. Character causality
Do actions arise from characters or author convenience?

### 4. Page-turn logic
Does the page enter and exit effectively?

### 5. Balloon flow
Do words guide or obstruct reading?

### 6. Pacing
Are panel number, size, and rhythm appropriate?

### 7. Spatial context
Do we know where we are?

### 8. Acting
Do expression and body language support the dialogue?

### 9. Camera / composition
Are angles purposeful and varied?

### 10. Background hierarchy
Enough context? Too much competition?

### 11. Motion / impact
Do several visual signals reinforce the intended action?

### 12. Color / tone / SFX
Do finishing systems support story?

### 13. Production efficiency
Is effort being spent where it matters?

### 14. Polish
Only now discuss small anatomy, rendering, texture, or stylistic cleanup.

# PART XXXII — DETAILED FAILURE MODES

## 95. Plot puppetry

Symptom: characters go wherever the outline needs them to go.

Fix: rebuild the chain of decisions and consequences.

---

## 96. “Event soup” middle

Symptom: Act II is only a series of unrelated obstacles.

Fix: make each obstacle change what the protagonist decides next.

---

## 97. Redundant narration

Symptom: caption describes visible action.

Fix: remove it or add nonvisual meaning.

---

## 98. Talking-head grid

Symptom: six nearly identical faces during a conversation.

Fix: vary scale and subject; show relevant environment, gestures, objects, reactions, or destination information while keeping dialogue connected.

---

## 99. Unmotivated camera variation

Symptom: random angles added only for “cinematic” look.

Fix: assign each shot a storytelling function.

---

## 100. No establishing context

Symptom: characters appear in disconnected close-ups.

Fix: establish or deliberately delay establishment as a reveal.

---

## 101. Animation-board syndrome

Symptom: a panel for every physical micro-step.

Fix: choose meaningful action beats and trust closure.

---

## 102. Hypercompressed page

Symptom: too many panels and balloons compete for area.

Fix: combine beats, move content to another page, or expand page budget.

---

## 103. Empty spectacle

Symptom: splash page is large but narratively minor.

Fix: reserve scale for importance, emotion, place, or deliberate aesthetic experience.

---

## 104. Pretty but unreadable

Symptom: experimental composition makes sequence unclear.

Fix: clarity over cool.

---

## 105. Background vanity

Symptom: environment is rendered beyond what the story needs while characters remain weak.

Fix: establish, use shorthand, prioritize focal information.

---

## 106. Balloon afterthought

Symptom: text covers faces or forces awkward order.

Fix: plan lettering at thumbnail stage.

---

## 107. Wrong page for reveal

Symptom: twist is visible before reader reaches the intended setup.

Fix: move the reveal across the page turn when format supports it.

---

## 108. Script inconsistency

Symptom: page / panel / dialogue labels change mid-document.

Fix: choose one internal format and stick to it.

---

## 109. Overpolished storyboard

Symptom: time wasted rendering an idea that has not been structurally approved.

Fix: return to rough thumbnails.

---

## 110. Endless perfection loop

Symptom: anatomy and surface detail are revised indefinitely while book remains unfinished.

Fix: define “professional enough for this project,” correct high-value errors, move forward, finish.

# PART XXXV — FINAL OPERATING PRINCIPLE

The LLM should always remember the hierarchy:

```text
STORY PURPOSE
→ CHARACTER CAUSALITY
→ SEQUENCE CLARITY
→ PAGE / SPREAD RHYTHM
→ READER ATTENTION
→ VISUAL STAGING
→ PRODUCTION EFFICIENCY
→ FINISHING TECHNIQUE
→ SURFACE POLISH
```

A comic is not successful because every drawing is perfect.

It succeeds when the reader knows what matters, feels the intended progression, understands the characters’ choices, moves through the pages naturally, experiences the reveals at the right time, and wants to continue.

And the production process succeeds when the creator can carry that intent all the way to a finished work.
