# The room's craft

What every agent in the room knows about the medium.

# Storycraft core

Shared by every creative role.

## The medium

A graphic novel is not illustrated prose. Meaning comes from the interaction of image, words, sequence, page architecture, and the reader's act of connecting panels.

The page and the page turn are storytelling units. A panel is a unit of attention. A balloon is physical space.

## Core principles

1. Every page must earn its existence.
2. Prefer causality over chronology: one choice should create the next problem.
3. Character is revealed by consequential action under pressure.
4. Visual information should do work that dialogue would otherwise have to do.
5. Words and images should complement each other rather than redundantly narrate the same fact.
6. Enter scenes late and leave when the dramatic transaction is complete.
7. Escalation means changing the problem, not merely increasing volume.
8. Plant information before the reader needs it; pay it off after they have nearly forgotten it.
9. Preserve orientation unless confusion is intentional and useful.
10. Build memorable contrasts: scale, pace, silence/noise, public/private, hope/fear, order/chaos.
11. Page turns are opportunities for surprise, revelation, reversal, or a changed question.
12. Clarity is not the enemy of complexity. The reader can handle difficult ideas if the immediate dramatic objective is legible.

## Scene test

For any scene or sequence, identify:

- Who wants what right now?
- What prevents them from getting it?
- What choice do they make?
- What changes because of that choice?
- What does the reader now know, feel, or anticipate that they did not before?
- Why is this better as comics than as a paragraph of explanation?

## Information hierarchy

Separate:
- what the character knows,
- what the reader knows,
- what other characters believe,
- what is objectively true in project canon.

Dramatic irony is useful only when those differences are intentional.

## Exposition

Prefer evidence, behavior, environment, conflict, artifacts, and consequences. Dialogue may explain what cannot reasonably be shown, but characters should not tell one another things they both obviously know just for the reader's benefit.

## New concepts

When introducing a new institution, technology, location, rule, or social idea:
1. Show it doing something.
2. Give the reader one concrete anchor.
3. Delay secondary terminology.
4. Demonstrate the consequence of misunderstanding it.
5. Repeat the concept later in a changed context.

## Caveat

Rules are tools, not commandments. Break them when the effect is deliberate and the reader benefit is greater than the cost in clarity.

## Final principle

The reader should experience the story, not the writers' room.

# Methods from comics and creative practice

Use documented methods as craft prompts, not as style-imitation personas.

## Useful methods

### Whole-page thinking
Treat the complete page as a unit of rhythm and composition before polishing individual panels. Useful for the Layout Agent.

### Rough layouts first
Resolve staging, scale, reading order, and page turns with crude thumbnails before detail. Useful for the Layout Agent and the writers.

### Spatialized time
Explore whether the arrangement of panels can make time, repetition, parallel action, or memory visible as structure rather than explained text. Useful for the Plotter and Layout Agent.

### Dense panel specification when necessary
When story meaning depends on a precise visual fact, state it clearly in the script. Density should serve production, not literary display. Useful for the writers.

### Structural symmetry
Mirrors, reversals, repeated layouts, and transformed callbacks can create meaning when tied to story change. Use sparingly and intentionally.

### Image-first prompts
Begin from concrete images, remembered objects, places, gestures, or sensory fragments, then discover story associations.

### Cut-up / recombination
Reorder fragments from existing beats or lines to discover unexpected adjacency. The result is raw material, not automatically good story.

### Random-entry constraints
Use externally selected words, images, objects, or cards to break habitual associations.

## Guardrail

Do not ask an agent to imitate a living or historical creator's distinctive voice. Borrow transferable methods, constraints, and workflow principles.

# Graphic Narrative Storycraft & Visual Storytelling

## Why this skill exists

Use this skill when an LLM is helping a creator conceive, write, script, storyboard, lay out, draw, critique, revise, or finish a comic, manga, graphic novel, sequential illustration, or other page-based visual narrative.

The model must not behave like a prose writer who later decorates the prose with pictures. It must reason like a hybrid of:

- story architect,
- character writer,
- comic-script writer,
- storyboard artist,
- page designer,
- sequential storyteller,
- production-minded illustrator,
- letterer-aware composer,
- and self-editor.

The target is not merely a “good-looking page.” The target is a page or sequence that makes the reader experience the intended story clearly, emotionally, rhythmically, and efficiently.

This skill deliberately preserves detailed lessons and creator-specific heuristics from the source material. When two source workflows differ, do not flatten them into a fake universal rule. Preserve them as alternatives and choose according to the project.

# PART I — THE MEDIUM: WHAT A GRAPHIC NARRATIVE IS DOING

## 1. Comics are sequential, not merely illustrated

A graphic narrative gives the reader selected fragments:

- an image,
- then another image,
- a speech balloon,
- a reaction,
- a change in framing,
- a sound effect,
- a gutter,
- a page turn,
- a reveal.

The reader mentally creates continuity between those fragments.

Therefore, when designing a comic, always reason about **what the reader must infer between images**, not only what is literally drawn inside each image.

The blank interval between two panels is not nothing. It is a place where the reader constructs motion, elapsed time, causal connection, impact, or emotional transition.

### Operational rule

For every adjacent pair of panels, be able to answer:

```text
What changed between Panel A and Panel B?
What does the reader have to infer?
Is that inference easy enough to make?
Is the omitted action interesting to imagine, or confusing to reconstruct?
```

Do not draw every animation frame. Select the moments that allow the reader to build the scene.

---

## 2. Text and image form one system

Do not treat dialogue, captions, balloons, and art as independent layers of meaning.

In a good comic:

- the words affect the framing,
- the framing affects the words,
- balloons affect eye movement,
- expressions alter how dialogue is interpreted,
- images can replace exposition,
- captions can add information the drawing cannot efficiently show,
- sound effects can contribute to worldbuilding and physicality.

One source workflow explicitly places text into the thumbnail before finalizing the panel composition because the creator wants to see what the reader will read while deciding what the reader will see.

Another source notes that some sequences may work even better with little or no dialogue.

Treat both as valid.

### LLM behavior

When scripting or storyboarding, do not write the words first and forget the visual cost of those words. Do not draw the page first and squeeze lettering into whatever empty space remains.

Plan them together.

---

## 3. Comics are not movies

Borrow useful concepts from cinema—camera angle, establishing shot, cutting, visual continuity, lens-like perspective—but do not reduce comics to frozen film frames.

A comic page can do things a film frame cannot:

- show multiple moments simultaneously,
- make one moment physically larger than another,
- place images in deliberate spatial relationships,
- use the gutter as time,
- repeat nearly identical images for rhythm,
- compress a long event into one image,
- stretch a tiny action across many panels,
- hide a reveal behind a physical page turn,
- make typography part of the composition,
- use symbolic or abstract backgrounds,
- break or preserve panel borders according to emotion,
- turn an entire spread into one visual event.

Therefore, design for the **page as a medium**, not merely for imaginary camera cuts.

# PART XXIX — THE 12 SOURCE-DERIVED COMMANDMENTS, EXPANDED

## 77. Commandment 1 — Finish the comic

The source treats this as the only commandment that is essentially non-negotiable.

A finished imperfect comic teaches:

- pacing,
- continuity,
- production reality,
- endurance,
- what readers actually notice,
- what takes too long,
- what can be simplified.

An unfinished masterpiece plan teaches less about completion.

### LLM instruction

Do not keep escalating scope when a simpler solution would allow the user to finish.

---

## 78. Commandment 2 — Word balloons are king

Because balloons often have strong contrast and attract the eye, use them to reinforce flow rather than fight it.

---

## 79. Commandment 3 — Respect the reading path

Default conventions exist because readers process them automatically.

Break them only with a compelling, readable reason.

---

## 80. Commandment 4 — Clarity over cool

Never sacrifice comprehension merely to show off page design.

# PART XXX — SOURCE HEURISTICS THAT MUST NOT BE TURNED INTO FALSE LAWS

## 89. Preserve specificity and caveats

The model must distinguish among:

### Strong cross-source principle
Example: clarity matters; page turns matter; storyboards reduce expensive revision.

### Creator preference
Example: one artist prefers fewer than six panels and becomes cautious above that.

### Publisher / format guideline
Example: an example script system recommends roughly 25 words or fewer per text unit.

### Context-specific production method
Example: one writer-artist places text first in Photoshop; another may work from a separate script.

### Optional structural model
Example: three-act mapping.

Do not convert all five categories into “rules.”

# PART XXXV — FINAL OPERATING PRINCIPLE

The LLM should always remember the hierarchy:

```text
STORY PURPOSE
→ CHARACTER CAUSALITY
→ SEQUENCE CLARITY
→ PAGE / SPREAD RHYTHM
→ READER ATTENTION
→ VISUAL STAGING
→ PRODUCTION EFFICIENCY
→ FINISHING TECHNIQUE
→ SURFACE POLISH
```

A comic is not successful because every drawing is perfect.

It succeeds when the reader knows what matters, feels the intended progression, understands the characters’ choices, moves through the pages naturally, experiences the reveals at the right time, and wants to continue.

And the production process succeeds when the creator can carry that intent all the way to a finished work.
