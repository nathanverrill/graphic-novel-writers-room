# Money in the Lithium Triangle

Uses the same plausibility labels as the companion skills.

**"Lithium triangle" is only the name of the region here.** This skill is not about lithium. Treat lithium as one activity among many (copper, silver, tin, gold, borates, herding, farming, tourism, astronomy, trade, cities) and bring it in only when the writer's story does.

**This is a setting kit, not a plot.** It describes an environment, its constraints, and categories of things that can happen there. Do not steer toward any particular storyline, theme, protagonist, or political reading, and do not assume the writer wants conflict, villains, or a message. Serve whatever story the writer brings: a romance, a comedy, a procedural, a family saga, a thriller, a children's book. Offer options across the range rather than a favorite, and use only the parts that the writer's story touches.

## Plausibility labels

Everything here is labelled with the vocabulary defined in `agents/skills/hard-sf-rules.md`: **T** truth,
**EG** educated guess, **S** speculation, **L** license, and **Cut** for what does not fit. The
rules for a license, the license log and the Thorne and Tyson tests are in that file; read it
before inventing anything the story then has to live with.

Here the "science" is the region's physics, law, economics, and politics.
Baseline is September 2026. Exchange rates, inflation, and subsidies here change by the month. **Never hardcode a price or rate in a story without searching first**, and for near future stories prefer ratios ("a month's rent," "three shifts' pay," "a llama") over numbers.

## The big truths

1. **Three countries, three money cultures.** Chile: stable currency, formal banking, heavy consumer debt. Argentina: generations of inflation, everyone thinks in dollars, distrust of banks. Bolivia: long fixed peg that broke, dollar shortage, cash and QR and stablecoins side by side, huge informal economy. A character's relationship to money depends on which side of the border they grew up on.
2. **The mine pays far better than anything else nearby, and everyone knows it will end.** That produces both splurging and frantic saving, often in the same household.
3. **Who signs your paycheck matters more than what you do.** Direct employee of the major, contractor, subcontractor, community company, cooperative member, day laborer: same road, same dust, wildly different money.
4. **The shift roster shapes the wallet.** Days of no spending at camp, then days home with a lump of cash and a list of people who need it.
5. **Boom town prices punish everyone not on a mine wage.** Teachers, nurses, herders, pensioners, and police pay mining rents on normal salaries.
6. **Trust is local.** People trust dollars, bricks, vehicles, animals, land, and relatives before banks or governments. Near future tech rides on top of that, it does not replace it.

## Currency and payment by country

**Chile (T).** Peso is stable by regional standards. Big contracts, rents, and mortgages are indexed to the UF, an inflation linked unit. Almost everyone is banked (the state bank's basic account is universal). Debit cards and phone transfers everywhere, even at market stalls. Household debt is high: department store credit cards, supermarket cards, payday style advances, car loans. Pensions are individual accounts that mostly disappoint. A Chilean miner's money story is salary, bonus, and debt.

**Argentina (T).** Decades of high inflation and repeated currency crises. Wages are renegotiated several times a year through union bargaining (paritarias). People convert savings to physical dollars and keep them at home; big purchases (houses, cars, land) are priced in dollars and often paid in cash. Official and parallel exchange rates have come and gone for decades, and even in calm periods nobody believes the calm. Digital wallets (Mercado Pago and others) are universal for daily payments and pay interest on balances to keep up with inflation. Stablecoins are common among the young and freelancers. Foreign mining companies work under 30 year stability deals that ordinary people do not get. The 13th month salary (aguinaldo) paid in two halves is a fixed point of the year. An Argentine's money story is speed: spend or convert before it melts.

**Bolivia (T).** The boliviano was pegged to the dollar from 2011 until the reserves ran out. A parallel market opened around 2023 and the street rate went to more than double the official one at the worst point. Dollars vanished from banks, card limits abroad were tiny, and fuel queues lasted days. People and importers moved into stablecoins to keep trading; dealerships and shops began accepting them openly. In 2026 the new government cut fuel subsidies, faced weeks of blockades, floated the currency, and went to the IMF. The official and street rates have converged for now, at a far weaker level, with inflation feeding through. Day to day: cash is king, QR code payments from bank apps are everywhere down to street vendors, and most work is informal with no payslip. The year end bonus (and in good years a second one) matters. Fuel price and supply is the thing everyone tracks. A Bolivian's money story is improvisation and distrust of any promise that a number will hold.

**Border logic (T).** Any price gap between the three countries becomes a business within days: fuel, flour, cooking oil, electronics, cars, coca, cigarettes, dollars themselves. Border towns (Villazón and La Quiaca, Ollagüe, Pisiga and Colchane, Bermejo) live on it. Which direction the goods flow flips when exchange rates flip.

## Who earns what (relative, not absolute)

Think in tiers. The exact numbers move; the ratios are stable.

| Who | Pay and terms | Notes | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Expat manager or specialist** | Home country salary in hard currency, housing, flights, hardship uplift | Spends little locally. Invisible money | T |
| **Direct employee of a major** (operator, technician, lab) | Several times the local average wage. Health cover, bonuses, union contract, training | The golden ticket. In Chilean copper, the bonus for signing a multi year union contract can equal a year's pay for an ordinary worker, paid as a lump sum | T |
| **Professional** (engineer, geologist, hydrologist) | High, often partly dollar linked in Argentina and Bolivia | Mobile. Will leave for Australia or Canada | T |
| **Contractor employee** (maintenance, construction, drilling) | Roughly half to two thirds of direct staff for similar work. Short contracts, fewer benefits | Most of the people on site. Most of the resentment | T |
| **Subcontractor and camp services** (catering, cleaning, laundry, security guard) | Near minimum wage plus site allowance. High turnover. Often women and migrants | The guard at the gate earns a small fraction of the truck he is waving through | T |
| **Community company worker or owner** | Workers get contractor wages. Owners and the family that controls the company can become locally rich | The main route by which royalty and contract money becomes private money | T |
| **Trucker, owner operator** | Good gross, brutal costs: fuel, tires, tolls, bribes, debt on the truck | One breakdown from ruin. Organized, can shut a road | T |
| **State mine worker** (Bolivia) | Decent, unionized, politically protected, partly tied to metal prices | Envied and resented by cooperative miners | T |
| **Cooperative partner (socio)** (Bolivia) | Owns a share of a vein. Income swings with ore grade and metal price. Can be very good or nothing | Hires laborers. Pays little tax. Buys a pickup in a good year | T |
| **Cooperative day laborer (peón)** | Paid per day or per sack, cash, no contract, no insurance. Sometimes paid in ore | Teenagers among them. Takes the physical risk | T |
| **Palliri** (women sorting dumps) | Sells small lots of scavenged ore by the kilo. Bottom of the ladder | Often widows of miners | T |
| **Juku** (ore thief) | A good night can beat a month's legal wage. Splits with insiders and buyers | High risk, young men | T |
| **Herder, quinoa farmer, salt cutter** | Low and seasonal cash, wealth held in animals and land. Quinoa had its own boom and bust | The baseline everyone is compared to | T |
| **Tourism worker** (guide, driver, hostel) | Seasonal, tips in dollars or euros, vulnerable to blockades and border closures | Competes with mines for vehicles, beds, and water | T |
| **Public employee** (teacher, nurse, police) | Fixed salary that boom town prices eat alive. Some get zone bonuses | Moonlights, or leaves | T |

## How the roster shapes money

- **On shift (7, 10, or 14 days):** food, bed, transport, and gear are provided. Nothing to spend on. Pay accumulates. Phone transfers home from the camp lounge.
- **Shift change day:** buses arrive in town or the regional city. Bars, restaurants, motels, electronics shops, and car dealers time themselves to it. So do thieves, lenders collecting, and partners waiting.
- **Off days:** many workers do not live near the mine at all. They commute by bus or plane from Antofagasta, La Serena, Santiago, Salta, Jujuy, Tucumán, Cochabamba, or Tarija, so the wages leave the region. Local mayors complain the money flies over them.
- **Two households:** a family home in the city and a life at site. Secrets, second families, and child support orders garnishing wages are common enough to be a cliché.
- **Payday rhythm:** monthly in Chile and Argentina's formal sector, with advances mid month. Cooperatives and informal work pay on sale of ore, weekly or whenever the buyer pays.

## What people do with mine money

- **Build, floor by floor.** Brick and rebar is the savings account. The unfinished second story with rods sticking up is wealth stored for the next good year.
- **Buy a vehicle.** Pickup, minibus, or truck, because a vehicle is also a business: transport contracts, taxi runs, tourism, contraband. In Bolivia many are cheap smuggled cars without papers.
- **Educate a child in the city.** Rent a room in Salta, Sucre, Antofagasta. Engineering, nursing, law.
- **Carry relatives.** A mine wage supports parents, siblings, godchildren. Refusing is not an option.
- **Sponsor a fiesta.** Being the pasante of a patron saint festival, or dancing in Oruro's carnival, can cost a year's savings: band, costumes, beer, food for hundreds. It buys standing that money alone cannot. Outsiders call it waste. Locals call it how a community stays a community.
- **Convert to dollars** (Argentina, Bolivia) or to animals and land (countryside).
- **Drink it.** Boom towns have more bars than shops. So do some payday budgets.
- **Start the side business** for after the mine: a shop, a hostel, a truck, a water tank lorry.

## Credit, debt, and saving

- **Fiado:** the corner shop's notebook. Buy now, pay on payday. Runs on reputation. Universal (T).
- **Rotating savings circles:** called pasanaku in Bolivia and the Andean northwest, similar circles elsewhere. Ten people put in a fixed amount each week or month and take turns receiving the pot. No paperwork, enforced by shame. Used to buy stock, a vehicle, or pay for a fiesta (T).
- **Retail cards and payday advances** (Chile): easy credit, high interest, debt collectors, blacklists that block future jobs and loans (T).
- **Gota a gota:** "drop by drop" loan sharks, a Colombian model spread across Latin America. Small cash loans to vendors and workers, daily collection by motorbike, interest around twenty percent a month, violence for non payment. Present in northern Chile, Bolivia, and Argentina, tied to transnational gangs (T, growing EG).
- **Ore buyer advances** (Bolivia): the buyer fronts cash, tools, or dynamite against future ore, then sets the price. Debt peonage with a friendly face (T).
- **Microfinance and cooperatives' credit unions** (Bolivia especially): widespread, legitimate, and a lifeline. Also a way to be over indebted to five lenders at once (T).
- **Company store logic returns** (EG): camp apps, wage advances through the employer's fintech partner, deductions at source for uniforms, lodging, or loans.
- **Pawn and sell:** gold jewelry, phones, tools, the pickup. First things to go in a bust.

## Boom town prices (T)

- Rents double or triple when a project is building. Whole hostels and family houses are leased by contractors. Locals rent out their own rooms and sleep in the kitchen, or are priced out to the edge of town.
- Food, vehicles, and mechanics cost more than in the capital because everything is trucked in and demand does not haggle.
- Some prices quietly go dollar, especially rent, vehicles, and anything sold to companies.
- Water by truck costs several times piped water. The poor pay the most per liter.
- Two price lists: one for people in company jackets, one for neighbors.
- Construction phase peak, then a drop. A build employs thousands for two or three years; operation employs a few hundred. Towns that borrowed and built for the peak are left with empty rooms.

## Community money: royalties and agreements

- **Forms:** a percent of sales to a community council or trust, fixed annual payments, funding for projects, jobs quotas, preferential contracts for community owned companies, scholarships, a share in a provincial company's dividends (T).
- **Where the fights are:** who counts as a member (residents versus those who left for the city, families with old surnames versus newcomers), who signs for the community, whether to pay out cash per head or fund collective works, audits, and leaders who acquire new trucks (T).
- **What it does:** new local elites, new offices, consultants and lawyers on retainer, young people returning, and neighbors who no longer speak. Communities without an agreement watch those with one (T).
- **State side:** mining royalties go to regional and municipal governments, which spend them on visible works of mixed usefulness: a coliseum, a plaza, a paved street to nowhere. In Bolivia, fights over how a department's royalties are split can shut down a city for weeks (T).
- **Near future (EG):** payments on transparent ledgers or apps demanded by lenders and auditors; per capita digital payouts; trust funds invested abroad; and the same old argument about who is on the list.

## The supporting economy

Truckers and mechanics. Tire shops. Fuel sellers, legal and not. Water tank lorries. Caterers, laundries, camp cleaners. Bus companies. Hostel owners. Market women who are often the real financiers of their families. Money changers on a plastic stool with a calculator. Phone and Starlink resellers. Safety gear shops. Assay labs and ore buyers' yards. Dynamite and coca stalls at the miners' market. Lawyers, notaries, and "gestores" who make paperwork happen. Evangelical churches taking tithes. Sex workers, many of them migrants, some trafficked, working bars timed to shift change. Healers and yatiris paid for offerings. Contraband haulers (chuteros, bagayeros) moving cars, fuel, and goods across the line. Coca growers and traders upstream. All of it rises and falls with the mine's payroll.

## Bust, in a wallet

- Contractors are cut first, overnight, often with wages owed. Direct staff last, with severance.
- Local suppliers hold unpaid invoices from a subsidiary that no longer answers the phone.
- Rents collapse, the half built second floor stays half built, the pickup bought on credit is repossessed or sold cheap.
- The fiado notebook fills, then the shop closes.
- Men leave for another mine, another country, or the coops. Women hold the household with market trade.
- In Bolivia, laid off workers and the newly poor flow into cooperatives and informal gold. Ore theft rises.
- Lenders, legal and gota a gota, keep collecting.
- When the price returns, the company comes back under a new name and hires at lower rates.

## Near future extrapolations

| Element | Label |
|---|---|
| Wages paid to phone wallets as default, even for day labor. Cash survives for anything people do not want recorded | EG |
| Dollar stablecoins as the normal savings and cross border tool in Bolivia and Argentina, tolerated or regulated. Ore buyers, smugglers, remittance senders, and ordinary savers all using the same rails | EG |
| Chinese payment apps and yuan settlement inside Chinese run compounds and with Chinese ore buyers; ordinary locals still think in dollars | EG to S |
| Central bank digital currencies announced, piloted, distrusted | S |
| Automation shrinks direct jobs, so the fight shifts from jobs to royalties, contracts, and cash transfers. Communities demand a per tonne payment rather than employment quotas | EG |

| Algorithmic rostering and gig style contracts for maintenance and transport crews, paid per task through a platform | EG |

## Scene details that ring true

A roll of dollars in a sock inside a bag of rice. A QR code taped to a market woman's umbrella pole. A money changer's stool outside the bus terminal. A worker doing mental arithmetic in three currencies. A payslip with more deduction lines than pay lines. A laminated price list with the numbers on stickers so they can be changed weekly. The shop's fiado notebook with the surname everyone knows. The pasanaku envelope handed over at a Sunday lunch. A new pickup parked outside an adobe house with no plumbing. Rebar sprouting from every roof. A brass band hired for three days. A motorbike collector stopping at each stall at dusk. Fuel queue stretching out of town with people sleeping in cabs. A contractor's WhatsApp group the night the layoffs come. The community assembly where the only agenda item is the list of names.

## Cut list

These fail the Thorne test for this setting. Any of them can still be used as a declared **License** under the rules above, never by accident.

- A cashless, frictionless future. Cash, barter, favors, and animals persist because trust in institutions is low and connectivity fails.
- Locals uniformly impoverished or uniformly enriched by mining. The point is the spread, inside one town and inside one family.
- Workers paid in company scrip or literal lithium. The modern company store is an app and a payroll deduction.
- Everyone fluent in crypto. Stablecoins as dollars, yes. Speculative tokens as daily money, no.
- Stable prices over a long story in Argentina or Bolivia. If years pass, numbers change, and characters notice.
- Communities receiving royalties without conflict, or all leaders being corrupt. Both happen; neither is universal.
- Cooperative miners as poor victims only. Partners can be wealthy and exploit their own laborers.
- Dollarization or a shared currency solving anything cleanly in the story's time frame. It may be tried (S). It will be argued about regardless.
- Subsidized fuel lasting forever in Bolivia, or its removal going smoothly. It is always a fight.

## Output formats

Same as the companion skills (**world brief**, **location sheet**, **faction sheet**, **plausibility review**, **timeline**). Add:

**Character ledger:** for any character, list who pays them and how, how often, in what currency or form, what it buys locally (rent, food, fuel as ratios), who depends on them, what they owe and to whom, where they keep savings, what they are building toward, and what happens to them in a bust. One short paragraph is enough, but every main character should have one.

**Price board:** for a scene's town and year, a dozen everyday prices expressed as ratios to a contractor's daily wage, with the local and the company jacket price where they differ.

**License log:** a running list for the project. For each L: what it is, the story reason, the nearest T, EG, or S version, what must stay rigorous around it, and whether it is the premise, a small license, or L clarity. Show the log whenever a plausibility review is given, and warn when the count is getting high.
