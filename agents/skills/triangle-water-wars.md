# Water Wars in the Lithium Triangle

Uses the same plausibility labels as the companion skills.

**"Lithium triangle" is only the name of the region here.** This skill is not about lithium. Treat lithium as one activity among many (copper, silver, tin, gold, borates, herding, farming, tourism, astronomy, trade, cities) and bring it in only when the writer's story does.

**This is a setting kit, not a plot.** It describes an environment, its constraints, and categories of things that can happen there. Do not steer toward any particular storyline, theme, protagonist, or political reading, and do not assume the writer wants conflict, villains, or a message. Serve whatever story the writer brings: a romance, a comedy, a procedural, a family saga, a thriller, a children's book. Offer options across the range rather than a favorite, and use only the parts that the writer's story touches.

## Plausibility labels

Everything here is labelled with the vocabulary defined in `agents/skills/hard-sf-rules.md`: **T** truth,
**EG** educated guess, **S** speculation, **L** license, and **Cut** for what does not fit. The
rules for a license, the license log and the Thorne and Tyson tests are in that file; read it
before inventing anything the story then has to live with.

Here the "science" is the region's physics, law, economics, and politics.
Baseline is September 2026. Search before relying on a specific precedent's details.

## What "war" means here

People in this region use the word war for things that are not wars. Bolivia's Guerra del Agua (Cochabamba, 2000) lasted about four months from first protests to victory, peaked in one week of street fighting, and killed one teenager. It expelled a multinational, reversed a national law, and reshaped the country's politics for twenty years. That is the scale and the model.

A water war, as locals mean it:

- Lasts **days to weeks** at its peak, inside a conflict that simmers for **years**.
- Is fought with **bodies on roads**, stones, slings, dynamite noise, tear gas, rubber and sometimes live rounds, fire, lawsuits, and livestreams. Not armies.
- Kills **zero to a handful**, occasionally a dozen. Each death has a name everyone knows, a mural, and an anniversary.
- Is about **who decides**, as much as about liters. The spark is usually an insult: a contract signed in secret, a permit issued over a holiday, a minister who would not come.
- **Ends in a paper**: a law repealed, a permit suspended, an agreement signed at 4 am, a company "pausing" a project. Then the fight over whether the paper is honored begins.
- Gets a **name and a date**, and becomes a founding story. The next generation's leaders are the ones who were teenagers on the barricade.

Write these as events with protagonists on every side, not as apocalypse.

## Precedent bank (all T)

Use these to calibrate. Do not copy them; rhyme with them.

**Bolivia**
- **Cochabamba Water War, 2000.** City water privatized to a foreign led consortium, tariffs jumped, even wells and rainwater seemed to fall under the concession. A coalition of irrigating farmers, factory workers, coca growers, students, and neighbors (the Coordinadora) shut the city. Martial law. A seventeen year old shot dead by an army sniper on camera. Government backed down, company left, then sued for millions abroad and eventually dropped it under pressure.
- **El Alto, 2005.** Second water war against another foreign concessionaire. Won.
- **La Paz water crisis, 2016.** Reservoirs fed by shrinking glaciers ran dry. Rationing for weeks in a capital city. Residents briefly held water officials until trucks arrived. State of emergency.
- **Lake Poopó, 2015 onward.** Bolivia's second largest lake dried up from drought and diversion. Fishing communities dissolved. A warning everyone cites.
- **Nor Lípez versus the Uyuni lithium contracts, 2024 onward.** Assemblies voting to expel foreign firms, lawsuits over consultation and freshwater sourcing, contracts suspended by a court.

**Chile**
- **Quillagua.** Oasis town on the Loa River, often called the driest place on Earth. Mining contamination and upstream extraction in the late 1990s and 2000 killed its farming. Now lives on trucked water. The ghost at every meeting.
- **Pampa Colorada, 2007.** A giant copper mine's plan to pump high Andean groundwater was rejected after Atacameño opposition. Proof communities can win.
- **El Tatio, 2009.** A geothermal exploration well blew out beside the famous geysers. Project cancelled after protests.
- **Caimanes versus Los Pelambres.** A decade of blockades, hunger strikes, and court rulings over a tailings dam above a village's water.
- **Petorca.** Avocado exporters with private water rights and illegal drains while villagers got water by truck. Gave the country the slogan "it's not drought, it's plunder."
- **Salar de Atacama blockades, 2018 and 2024.** Atacameño communities blocked the roads to the lithium operations for days over state deals made without them. Won a seat in the talks.
- **Freirina, 2012.** A town rose against a stinking industrial pig plant, fought police for weeks, and closed it. The template for a small town beating a big company.

**Argentina**
- **Famatina, 2007 onward.** "El Famatina no se toca." A small La Rioja town blockaded a mountain road for years and drove off four mining companies in a row.
- **Andalgalá, 2010 and 2021.** Police repression of a blockade against a copper project turned into a town uprising. In 2021 the company's offices were burned. Weekly protest walks have continued for many hundreds of Saturdays.
- **Mendoza, December 2019.** The legislature loosened a law banning cyanide and other chemicals in mining. Tens of thousands marched for days under "the water of Mendoza is not negotiable." Repealed within a week. Known as the Mendozazo.
- **Chubut, December 2021.** A mining zoning law passed at night. Government buildings burned. Repealed in days. The Chubutazo.
- **Jáchal and Veladero, 2015.** A cyanide solution spill into rivers from a gold mine. The company first understated it. A permanent protest camp followed.
- **Jujuy, 2023.** A fast tracked provincial constitutional reform touching land and protest rights set off weeks of blockades across the lithium roads, harsh repression, eye injuries from rubber bullets, and an indigenous march to Buenos Aires.
- **Río Los Patos, Catamarca.** A wetland dried after a lithium company's aqueduct. Years later a provincial court froze new permits in the basin.
- **Salinas Grandes.** Thirty some communities have refused lithium entry for over a decade with their own consultation protocol, road control, and court cases.

**Neighbors worth knowing**
- **Conga and Tía María, Peru.** "Water yes, gold no." Multiple protesters killed across several years, states of emergency, multibillion dollar projects frozen. Shows the upper bound of violence for this kind of conflict in the Andes.
- **La Boquilla, Mexico, 2020.** Farmers seized a dam from the National Guard to stop water being released to pay a treaty debt. One woman killed. Precedent for civilians taking water infrastructure.

## Who uses the water

Conflict can start from any of these, and most real ones have not been about lithium.

- **Farming and herding:** the largest user across the region. Irrigators with canal turns measured in hours, export fruit and avocado growers with bought water rights, quinoa, wetlands for llamas.
- **Copper and other hard rock mining:** by far the thirstiest industry, plus tailings dams and acid drainage that threaten what water is left.
- **Cities:** Antofagasta, Calama, Copiapó, La Paz, El Alto, Potosí, Oruro, Jujuy, Salta. Rationing, tariffs, private concessions, and neighborhoods served by truck.
- **Old mine contamination:** rivers such as the Pilcomayo and the basin of Lake Poopó carrying centuries of tin and silver waste to downstream farmers and fishers.
- **Tourism:** hotels and pools in oasis towns that ration water to residents.
- **Energy:** hydropower dams, geothermal wells, solar plants that need washing, proposed green hydrogen plants that need pure water.
- **Data centers** where they arrive.
- **Brine and lithium operations:** a newer and smaller user than copper or farming, but placed exactly at the fragile edge of salt flats.
- **Glaciers and rock glaciers:** mining near ice is its own legal and physical fight, especially in Argentina.
- **Across borders and between provinces:** shared rivers, aquifers, and transfers.

## Triggers

Rank in rough order of how often they start things.

1. **A decision made in secret or in a hurry.** Contract, permit, law, or constitutional change passed at night, over a holiday, or without consultation.
2. **A visible loss.** The spring stops. The lagoon is gone in the dry season for the first time. Llamas die. The municipal well goes salty. Taps run two hours a day.
3. **A spill or accident.** Brine, acid, tailings, cyanide, diesel into a river or wetland. Worse if first denied.
4. **An insult.** An official who says the communities are manipulated, a company video, a minister who flies over and does not land, a leader arrested.
5. **A pipeline, dam, or well field aimed at someone's water.** New boreholes beside a wetland, an aqueduct from a river, a dam above a valley, a transfer from one basin or province to another.
6. **Drought emergency allocation.** Rationing that spares the mine, or is believed to.
7. **A boom.** Five companies arrive in one year, all drilling. Or a crop boom, with new wells for every export orchard.
8. **A death.** A protester, a herder hit by a company truck, a child sick downstream.
9. **Betrayal inside.** A leader or a neighboring community signs an agreement.
10. **Tariffs and privatization.** A private operator or desalination concession raises town water prices.

## Actors

**Community side:** assemblies and traditional authorities; irrigators' associations (canal users are the most organized people in any valley); herders; women's organizations (often the backbone and the front line); young people home from university with drones and law degrees; mine workers from the same families, torn; teachers; the parish priest or evangelical pastor; regional civic committees (very powerful in Potosí); transport unions whose trucks make or break a blockade; cooperative miners, who may join for their own reasons and bring dynamite; tour operators; NGOs, lawyers, hydrologists, journalists, and visiting foreigners with cameras.

**Company side:** site manager, community relations team, security manager, lawyers, a public relations firm in the capital, head office on another continent watching the share price, contractors who want the road open, workers' unions who want their shifts, and the communities that already signed.

**State side:** mayor (caught in the middle), governor (usually pro project), provincial or national police, gendarmerie or carabineros special forces, army in Bolivia and under emergency decrees elsewhere, prosecutors, the environmental regulator, the water authority, judges, the ombudsman, national ministers, and eventually the president.

**Outside:** embassies of the owner's country, lenders and battery buyers with audit clauses, international courts and arbitration panels, the Catholic Church or the UN as mediator, organized crime using the chaos, and political parties trying to ride it.

## Tactics

**Community**
- Assembly, vote, written resolution, deadline. Always first.
- Road blockade: stones, burning tires, parked trucks, a rope and fifty women. Rotating shifts by community, a common kitchen, coca and soup at 3 am. Let ambulances through. Decide about tourists.
- Vigil at the gate, permanent protest camp, weekly walk.
- March to the provincial or national capital, days on foot, picking up support.
- Occupation: a well field, a pump station, the company's town office, the municipal building.
- Closing valves, switching off pumps, cutting power to a well.
- Hunger strike. Crucifixion style protest. Sewing lips (rare, remembered).
- Citizen science: their own sampling, sensors, drone footage of a dry vega set beside old photos.
- Courts: injunctions, constitutional claims, complaints to regional human rights bodies.
- Livestream everything. Hashtag. Get a national celebrity or a foreign parliamentarian to say it.
- Pressure buyers: letters to car and battery makers, audit complaints.
- Sabotage at the edge: slashed pond liners, a burned pickup or drill rig, a cut pipeline, a toppled monitoring mast, sugar in a tank. Usually at night, usually disowned by the assembly (T rarely, EG more often later).
- Holding people: officials, engineers, or police detained for hours until a minister comes. Released unharmed, almost always (T).
- Community justice threats against insiders who signed.

**Company and state**
- Divide: sign with one community, hire from one family, fund one leader's project.
- Delay: dialogue tables (mesas de diálogo) that meet for years.
- Criminalize: charges for blocking roads, damage, kidnapping, terrorism in extreme cases. Court dates for a decade.
- Flood the zone with police before an eviction at dawn. Tear gas, rubber bullets aimed at faces, motorbike units, arrests, phones seized.
- State of emergency or siege. Army on the roads. Curfew.
- Infiltrate assemblies, monitor phones and social media, leak a leader's private life.
- Media: "a violent minority," "foreign funded," "anti development," "manipulated by politicians."
- Offer: a water plant, a hospital wing, a percent, jobs, a new road.
- Suspend the project loudly and wait for the cameras to leave.
- Sue the state abroad for lost profits if the permit is pulled.

## Anatomy of an event

Use this as the default arc. Bend it, do not skip steps.

1. **Slow grievance (years).** Water table dropping, studies contested, promises unmet. Meetings. Letters unanswered.
2. **Spark (a day).** One of the triggers.
3. **Assembly and ultimatum (days).** A deadline. Officials miss it or send a deputy.
4. **Blockade (day 1 to 5).** Roads close. Mine supply and product stop. Tourists stranded. Fuel and food get short in town. Truckers furious, then sometimes allied.
5. **Spread.** Other communities, the city, students, unions with their own demands. A coordinating body forms with a name.
6. **First clash.** Police try to clear the road. Injuries. A video goes national.
7. **Peak (days 5 to 15).** Bigger marches, a building burned, officials held, army deployed or threatened, a death or near death. Church or ombudsman offers mediation. Company stock falls. The capital notices.
8. **Negotiation.** All night sessions. Walkouts. A document.
9. **Paper.** Law repealed, permit suspended, independent study ordered, prisoners released, company "withdraws for now."
10. **Aftermath (years).** Trials of leaders. Compensation fights. The study is delayed. The project returns under a new name. The coalition splits over candidates and money. Some leaders enter politics, some are bought, some are broken.
11. **Memory.** A name, a date, a mural, a song, a yearly march, a school named for the dead. Children who were there are the next leaders.

## How events get named

- **Bolivia:** "Guerra del" plus the thing (Agua, Gas, and whatever comes next). Also by month: "Octubre Negro," "Febrero Negro."
- **Argentina:** place plus "azo": Mendozazo, Chubutazo, Jujeñazo, Andalgalazo. Also by slogan: "El Famatina no se toca."
- **Chile:** "el estallido," "la toma de" plus place, "el levantamiento de" plus town, or by date: "el 18 de octubre."
- **Everywhere:** by the name of the dead, by the bridge or kilometer marker where it happened ("lo del kilómetro 40"), by a slogan on a wall ("El agua vale más que el oro," "No es sequía, es saqueo," "Agua sí, mina no," "El agua no se negocia"), or in Quechua, Aymara, or Kunza by young activists reclaiming language. Yaku and uma mean water in Quechua and Aymara; puri in Kunza.
- The state and the company use different names for the same event: "the incidents," "the disturbances of March," "the illegal blockade."
- Give each event two or three names depending on who is speaking.

## How it is remembered

Murals of the dead with water flowing from their hands. A cross or apacheta at the spot. An annual march that gets smaller, then bigger again when the project returns. A song by a local band. A documentary that plays at European festivals. Court cases that outlive witnesses. A leader who became a deputy and is now called a traitor. A company history that does not mention it. Grandmothers who were on the rope. Police who were twenty years old and still dream about it. Children's drawings of tear gas. The phrase "like in the water war" used as both a threat and a promise.

## Cut list

These fail the Thorne test for this setting. Any of them can still be used as a declared **License** under the rules above, never by accident.

- Armies fighting over water. Militias holding reservoirs. Mad Max.
- Interstate war over an aquifer. Notes, courts, and border theater only.
- Death tolls in the hundreds. The upper bound with regional precedent is the low dozens in a national crisis, and single digits is far more typical.
- A decisive, final victory for anyone. Projects return; laws get rewritten; coalitions fracture.
- A unified "community." Every event includes insiders who signed, workers who need the shift, and families split down the middle.
- Purely spontaneous riots. These are organized societies with assemblies, shifts, kitchens, and minutes.
- Eco terrorism campaigns with bombs and casualties as a norm. Property sabotage is plausible; killing campaigns are not the regional pattern.
- Communities as always right, companies as always lying. Sometimes the hydrology really is ambiguous, and both sides' experts are sincere.
- Water literally running out for everyone at once. Scarcity arrives unevenly: by neighborhood, by hour of the day, by who can pay for a truck.
- The mine simply closing forever because of one protest. Pauses, sales, redesigns, and relocations are the realistic outcomes.

## Output formats

Same as the companion skills (**world brief**, **location sheet**, **faction sheet**, **plausibility review**, **timeline**). Add:

**Event sheet:** names (community, state, company versions), date and duration, place and scale, slow grievance, spark, coalition and its coordinating body, demands, tactics used by each side day by day, peak moment, casualties with names, who mediated, the paper that ended it, what was and was not honored, trials and careers after, how it is remembered, plausibility label, nearest real precedent.

**Chronicle:** the same event as a dated log, one to three lines per day, suitable for a wall of text in a comic panel, a news crawl, or a chapter opener.

**Voices:** the event told in short first person accounts from five to eight people on different sides, ten or twenty years later. Contradictory on purpose.

**History of the region:** for a story set in 2045 or 2055, a list of four to eight remembered events since 2026, spaced by boom and bust, each with a name, so characters have a shared past to argue about.

**License log:** a running list for the project. For each L: what it is, the story reason, the nearest T, EG, or S version, what must stay rigorous around it, and whether it is the premise, a small license, or L clarity. Show the log whenever a plausibility review is given, and warn when the count is getting high.
