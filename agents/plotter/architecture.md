# Story architecture


## Story map

Build from decisions and consequences, not a list of events.

For each major beat record:
- dramatic question,
- character objective,
- obstacle,
- choice,
- consequence,
- new information,
- emotional movement,
- page budget.

## Three-act thinking without formula worship

Use acts as changes in the kind of problem:
- **Act I:** establish the world, desire, pressure, and irreversible commitment.
- **Act II:** attempts create consequences; understanding deepens; alliances and assumptions change.
- **Act III:** the protagonist must act with the knowledge and costs accumulated earlier.

Do not force exact percentages when the story's rhythm argues otherwise.

## Anchor beats

Useful anchors include:
- image or event that establishes the story's governing tension,
- inciting disruption,
- first irreversible choice,
- midpoint change in understanding or strategy,
- consequence that removes the easy path,
- final meaningful choice,
- image or action that proves what changed.

## Page budget

Budget pages early. A sequence that is emotionally or visually important needs room. Exposition that can be understood in one image should not occupy three pages of dialogue.

Track:
- total target pages,
- pages per act/section,
- major spreads,
- reveals requiring right-hand page turns,
- quiet pages,
- compressed transitions.

## Character causality

A character is structurally useful when their values, fears, blind spots, relationships, and skills alter what happens.

For each principal character define:
- public role,
- immediate want,
- deeper need or unresolved contradiction,
- leverage or capability,
- vulnerability,
- mistaken belief or incomplete model of the world,
- boundary they will not cross at the start,
- decision that only this character would make,
- relationship that pressures change,
- proof of change or refusal to change.

Avoid biography that never affects a scene.

## Ensemble test

Every principal character should differ in at least three useful ways:
- what they notice,
- what they value,
- what they can do,
- what they fear losing,
- what they think the problem is,
- what solution they reach for first.

If two characters repeatedly make the same dramatic choice, combine them or sharpen the difference.
