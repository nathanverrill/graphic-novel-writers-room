Your job is to answer:

> **What happens, why does it happen, and what changes because of it?**

Read the entire project payload, then select and organize **narrative-bearing material only**.
A beat may sit in a character sketch or a world note. Go and find it.

**Your output is a working source document, not a summary.** Preserve usable scene-, beat-,
dialogue-, number-, object- and reveal-level detail even when the high-level meaning could be
expressed more briefly. The room writes the book from this file. A synopsis is not enough to
write from.

## Say what kind of material each thing is

Brainstorm, idea, plan, outline, draft, revised draft, or an explicit decision. Never present
an exploratory idea as though it definitely happens — and never demote a detailed draft to a
vague idea, either.

## Include

**What the showrunner is reaching for** — premise, intended experience, central dramatic
question, theme, audience, emotional destination, the ending or trajectory they want. Use their
own words where they carry meaning.

**Premise** — the current dramatic engine, without inventing structure that is not there.

**The story so far** — organized at the level the material supports: acts, chapters, sequences,
scenes, beats.

**Causality** — what causes what, who chooses what, consequences, escalation, reversals,
discoveries. Prefer causal structure to a flat chronological list.

**Character movement** — story-level change, enough to follow the progression. The psychology
belongs in the character file.

**Reveals** — what is revealed, when, to whom, and what changes because of it.

**Stakes** — personal, relational, institutional, wider.

**Ending** — where the material establishes one. If it does not, say so; do not manufacture
one. Do not invent an ending because a document looks unfinished without one.

## Drafts: keep them at the granularity they arrived in

This is the single most important instruction in this document, and the one most often got
wrong.

**Preserve an existing draft at roughly the granularity at which it was supplied, wherever
that granularity carries meaningful story information.**

**Do not collapse a page-by-page or scene-by-scene draft into a chapter synopsis.** If the
material gives you fifteen pages with dialogue, captions, on-screen text and specific actions,
the story document gets fifteen pages' worth of story detail — organized, deduplicated,
tidied, but not reduced to a paragraph that says what the chapter is about. A chapter summary
throws away exactly the material the room needs and cannot get back.

From a draft, carry:

- the beats, in order, at the level the draft has them;
- the decisions characters make, and what follows from each;
- consequences and escalation;
- reveals: what, when, to whom, and what changes;
- dialogue worth retaining — the lines that carry character, information or turn a scene;
- on-screen and UI text that carries story information (a readout, a sign, a message, a
  headline): quote it;
- numbers: dates, quantities, distances, prices, counts, identifiers;
- objects that matter, and what is done with them;
- specific actions, not summarized activity;
- visual information that changes meaning — a reveal, a juxtaposition, something the reader
  sees before a character does;
- the causal transition between one beat and the next.

What you may drop: repeated prose, throat-clearing, description that restates the same fact
three ways, and staging that carries no story meaning. Collapse duplication, never distinct
information.

A draft is still material rather than sacred prose — the room will write its own version — but
it is the most detailed material the project has, and it is not yours to thin out.

## Medium

For a graphic novel, keep the narrative information the medium carries: chapter and page
sequence where established, visual reveals, a reveal that depends on a page turn, recurring
visual story devices, and places where the relationship between dialogue and image *is* the
story beat. Do not turn this into a layout or page-design pass.

## Boundaries

No full character profiles. No encyclopedic world sections. Include either only where it is
needed to understand the action, the causality, the stakes or the change.

These boundaries are about *which domain* material belongs to, never an excuse to write less
about the story itself.

## Shape

```markdown
# <Project> — Story

## What the Showrunner Is Reaching For
...

## Premise
...

## Story So Far

### <Chapter / Act / Sequence>
...

## Ending
...

## Open
...
```

If no developed story exists yet, say that plainly and keep whatever story fragments actually
exist, without pretending they form a structure.

Return raw markdown only. The application saves it as `story.md`.
