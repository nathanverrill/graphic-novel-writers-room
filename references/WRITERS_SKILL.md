description: >
A detailed screenwriting and dialogue-writing skill for LLMs. Use it to develop,
write, diagnose, or rewrite scenes, dialogue, character behavior, action description,
and screenplay story structure. It prioritizes truthful human behavior, character
intention, obstacles, tactics, scene turns, subtext, distinct voice, economical visual
description, progressive change, and rigorous rewriting.
Actual Script Writing Skill 0. What This Skill Is For
Use this skill when the task involves any of the following:

writing or rewriting screenplay scenes
writing dialogue
improving character voice
fixing flat or “on-the-nose” dialogue
generating scene conflict
developing characters for drama
turning exposition into drama
diagnosing why a scene feels dead
creating scene turns and escalation
writing screenplay action/description
improving pacing inside a dialogue-heavy scene
distinguishing characters who sound alike
building subtext
revising a weak first draft
critiquing a screenplay or scene
studying why an existing scene works
converting a story idea into playable dramatic behavior
This is not a generic “make dialogue witty” prompt.

The core task is to make characters feel like real, specific human beings who want things, encounter resistance, take actions, conceal things, reveal themselves through behavior, and cause change.

The LLM should behave like a writer who alternates between:

CREATE
→ STEP OUTSIDE THE WORK
→ CRITIQUE
→ REWRITE
→ TEST
→ REWRITE AGAIN
Do not confuse fluency with quality.

A smooth first draft is still a first draft.

1. The Highest-Level Standard: Do Not Lie About Human Behavior
   One of the strongest lessons in the source material is that weak writing often comes from two related pressures:

fear of departing from familiar successful patterns
desire to please the audience
These pressures create:

clichés
recycled antagonisms
sentimental behavior
convenient reactions
false conflict
characters who behave the way the plot requires rather than the way a person would
Fiction is invented, but the human behavior inside it should feel truthful.

Working test
For every important beat, ask:

Would this person really do this?
Would this person really say this?
What would they actually want here?
What are they afraid will happen?
Why are they choosing this tactic?
What does this cost them?
What are they refusing to admit?
Is this behavior psychologically credible?
Or am I forcing it because I need the plot to move?
Genre does not excuse false behavior.

An action film can be emotionally truthful. A comedy can be psychologically truthful. A science-fiction story can be relationally truthful. A stylized movie can still contain honest motives.

Anti-cliché rule
Do not ask:

What usually happens in this kind of movie?

Ask:

Given this genre, this situation, and this particular human being, what is the most truthful and specific version of what could happen?

Conventions can be used.

Clichés should be interrogated.

2. Audience Pleasing Is Not the Same as Storytelling
   Do not write in an attempt to please every possible viewer.

A story has:

a sensibility
an audience
a genre
a worldview
a level of complexity
a taste profile
Trying to offend nobody and satisfy everybody often flattens the work.

The writer should understand who the work is for without converting the story into audience-service calculations.

LLM behavior
When asked to make something “more commercial,” do not automatically:

simplify every moral ambiguity
add a generic villain
force a romance
add quips everywhere
create a conventional happy ending
remove discomfort
Instead distinguish:

ACCESSIBILITY
from
GENERICITY
The work can become clearer, faster, more legible, and more dramatically compelling without becoming false.

3. Genre Helps Locate Conflict — It Does Not Write the Story for You
   Genre can supply some expected sources of pressure.

For example, a thriller may imply threat, pursuit, danger, secrecy, or a dangerous antagonist.

But other genres require deeper investigation.

For a family drama, the genre “family” does not tell you what is wrong.

You must ask:

mother vs daughter?
father vs son?
siblings?
money?
loyalty?
grief?
inheritance?
shame?
favoritism?
culture?
generational values?
intimacy?
control?
The answer comes from understanding people.

Comedy-specific principle
The source material frames comedy as rooted in anger or irritation.

A useful starting question is:

What is pissing this character—or the writer—off?

Possible targets:

hypocrisy
bureaucracy
etiquette
politics
social convention
romantic behavior
work culture
technology
status games
institutional absurdity
Comedy can arise when an idealistic expectation collides with how ridiculous reality is.

Do not make the comedy merely “amusing.”

Give the comic conflict a target.

4. Structure Means Change, Not Formula
   Do not interpret structure as a rigid page-number template.

The source’s deeper definition is:

Structure is the organized progression of change.

A scene should usually involve a character entering with a desire and leaving in a different condition.

That change may be:

external
relational
informational
emotional
strategic
moral
psychological
status-based
A simple scene may contain a minor change.

A sequence may produce a moderate change.

An act climax should create a major change.

The story builds toward an increasingly consequential, ultimately irreversible change.

Scene structure model
CHARACTER WANTS SOMETHING
→ SOMETHING RESISTS
→ CHARACTER ACTS
→ RESISTANCE RESPONDS
→ CHARACTER CHANGES TACTIC
→ PRESSURE INCREASES
→ SOMETHING TURNS
→ CHARACTER LEAVES IN A DIFFERENT CONDITION
This is structure.

It can occur inside:

quiet drama
comedy
action
romance
thriller
courtroom drama
workplace drama
a two-person conversation 5. The Change Test
After writing a scene, answer:

STATE AT ENTRY:
What does the character believe, possess, expect, want, fear, or control?

TURN:
What changes the direction of the scene?

STATE AT EXIT:
What is now different?
If the answers are effectively identical, investigate whether the scene is dramatically necessary.

Three scenes in a row should not produce the same degree and type of change.

Repetitious change creates boredom.

Progression requires variation in:

magnitude
direction
stakes
cost
revelation
reversibility 6. Writing Is Create → Critique, Repeated Constantly
Do not romanticize writing as continuous inspired flow.

A practical writing cycle is:

IMAGINE
WRITE
READ
JUDGE
REWRITE
READ AGAIN
REWRITE AGAIN
After creating a page or scene, ask:

Does this work?
Would the character say that?
Would the character do that?
Is the reaction credible?
Is this repetitive?
Does it make sense?
Is there a better way?
Is a beat missing?
Is the scene doing two things when it should do one?
Is it doing only one thing when it needs another layer?
The quality of rewriting depends on the quality of diagnosis.

“Make it better” is not a diagnosis.

Identify the failure.

7. First Drafts Are Material, Not Verdicts
   A first draft is allowed to be:

obvious
over-explained
too loud
too long
over-designed
rhythmically awkward
emotionally blunt
One useful way to think of it:

The first draft is a note to yourself about the effect you are trying to create.

Then revision hides the scaffolding.

If the first version says the intention too clearly, revise until the audience experiences the intention without seeing the machinery.

Revision sequence
PASS 1 — What am I trying to do?
PASS 2 — What actually happens on the page?
PASS 3 — What is redundant?
PASS 4 — What is false?
PASS 5 — Where is the character's intention unclear?
PASS 6 — Where is the obstacle weak?
PASS 7 — Where can behavior replace explanation?
PASS 8 — Where can subtext replace text?
PASS 9 — Does each character sound like themselves?
PASS 10 — Does the scene turn?
PASS 11 — Can the language become more vivid and economical? 8. Character Begins with Want, Obstacle, and Tactics
A powerful character-development model in the source comes from Aaron Sorkin:

Do not begin by telling the audience who the character is.

Show what they want.

Then place something formidable in the way.

Then observe how they try to overcome it.

Character engine
INTENTION

- OBSTACLE
- # TACTICS
  DRAMATIC CHARACTER REVELATION
  Demographics and surface characteristics can come later.

A character is not made alive merely by giving them:

an age
a job
a hairstyle
a hometown
a wardrobe
a favorite food
Those may be useful.

But behavior under pressure reveals character more powerfully.

Example logic
Two characters can want the exact same thing:

Get promoted.

One:

charms
flatters
bargains
builds alliances
The other:

intimidates
outworks everyone
exposes rivals
threatens
Their tactics tell us who they are.

9. Make the Want Specific
   Avoid weak objectives such as:

“wants respect”
“wants love”
“wants validation”
These may be long-term psychological needs, but they are often too vague to write a playable scene.

Convert them into immediate objectives.

Instead of:

She wants respect.

Use:

She wants him to stop interrupting her in front of the board.

Instead of:

He wants love.

Use:

He wants her to stay for dinner.

Instead of:

He wants approval.

Use:

He wants his father to sign the application tonight.

Specific wants generate behavior.

10. Want vs Need
    A want is what the character is pursuing.

A need may be deeper and sometimes unrecognized.

The source material repeatedly emphasizes that the most interesting characters may not understand what truly drives them.

Track:

CONSCIOUS WANT:
What does the character say they are trying to get?

DEEPER NEED:
What would actually resolve or transform them?

UNCONSCIOUS DRIVE:
What pressure are they not yet able to name?
These do not need to match.

The distance between them creates drama.

11. Public Objective vs Private Objective
    For an important scene, distinguish:

Public objective
What the character could plausibly admit they are doing.

Private objective
What they are actually trying to accomplish emotionally or psychologically.

Example:

PUBLIC:
Convince the boss to reconsider the assignment.

PRIVATE:
Force the boss to admit that class prejudice—not performance—caused the decision.
A character may not consciously recognize the private objective.

That is useful.

12. Add Two Questions: What Are They Hiding?
    For deep scenes, ask:

WHAT ARE THEY HIDING FROM THE OTHER CHARACTER?

WHAT ARE THEY HIDING FROM THEMSELVES?
This creates:

secrecy
deception
denial
subtext
delayed revelation
inner conflict
The second question is often more powerful.

A character may believe they are arguing about money while actually fighting to avoid admitting abandonment.

13. Push the Character to the Wall
    A character becomes dramatically interesting when pressure forces them to choose between values.

Ask:

What would this person hate to do?

Then ask:

What circumstance could make doing it necessary?

Examples:

an honest person must lie
a pacifist must use violence
a loyal friend must betray someone
a proud person must ask for help
a controlling parent must let a child leave
a compassionate doctor must choose who does not receive treatment
This produces internal conflict.

The drama is not only:

Can they do it?

It is also:

Can they live with themselves after doing it?

14. Three Large Arenas of Conflict
    The source’s Richard Powers material distinguishes several levels of dramatic pressure.

A. Person against self
Psychological / internal conflict.

Examples:

shame vs desire
fear vs duty
love vs resentment
principle vs survival
B. Person against person / society
Interpersonal, social, political conflict.

Two sympathetic people can hold incompatible values.

The author does not always need to declare one side evil.

A useful example pattern:

CHARACTER A'S CORE VALUE = EQUALITY
CHARACTER B'S CORE VALUE = FREEDOM
EVENT REQUIRES ONE PRIORITY
→ COLLISION
C. Person against environment / life
The human intention meets a world that may be:

indifferent
hostile
larger than the individual
ecologically incompatible
physically uncontrollable
A story can contain all three levels at once.

15. Roles Are Generic; Characters Are Specific
    “Hero,” “villain,” “victim,” “sidekick,” “cashier,” “doctor,” and “mother” are roles.

A role is a slot in a story.

A character is the specific human being who inhabits it.

Even a one-scene cashier should have a behavioral trait.

When a protagonist’s credit card fails, the cashier can be:

sympathetic
amused
contemptuous
distracted
bored
impatient
embarrassed for them
delighted by the power imbalance
That tiny choice creates playable behavior for the actor.

16. Dimensionality Comes from Contradiction
    A dimensional character contains contradictory tendencies.

Examples:

kind / cruel
brave / avoidant
generous / controlling
loyal / jealous
confident / approval-seeking
Do not merely list contradictions.

Design situations and relationships that activate them.

17. Cast Design: Every Relationship Should Reveal a Different Person
    A powerful ensemble principle:

Different people bring out different sides of us.

The protagonist should not behave identically with:

spouse
boss
child
rival
stranger
parent
subordinate
mentor
If every relationship produces the same behavior, the character feels flat and false.

Cast matrix
For each major pair, define:

A WITH B:
What side of A appears only here?
What side of B appears only here?
What do they want from each other?
What do they refuse to give each other?
What history shapes the exchange?
What behavior would neither show with anyone else?
Remove redundant relationships.

If two supporting characters bring out the exact same side of the protagonist, consider combining them or differentiating their functions.

18. Antiheroes and Antagonists Require Empathy
    Do not write a difficult character from a position of contempt.

The source’s useful formulation is to write even the antagonist as though they are:

making their case for why they deserve to enter heaven.

That does not mean approving of them.

It means understanding:

how they justify themselves
what they believe
what wound or value organizes their behavior
why their actions make sense from inside their worldview
Judgment produces cardboard villains.

Empathy produces characters.

19. Dialogue Is Not Decoration
    Dialogue should not exist merely because:

the writer enjoys banter
the scene needs words
the writer wants to sound clever
the audience needs an information dump
Dialogue performs dramatic work.

The source identifies multiple functions, including:

exposition
action
pleasure / verbal spectacle / beauty
Use all three intelligently.

20. Dialogue Function 1: Exposition
    Dialogue can reveal:

history
relationship
profession
worldview
social background
education
previous events
unseen context
But good exposition should feel like part of what the character is doing.

Vocabulary itself is exposition
A carpenter may know multiple specific kinds of nails.

A sailor may use nautical language.

A programmer may use technical shorthand.

That vocabulary tells us about the character’s life without a biography speech.

Tiny-word exposition
One word can imply history.

Example pattern:

“You’re not leaving me again.”

The important exposition is hidden in “again.”

The audience immediately understands that this has happened before.

21. Dialogue Function 2: Action
    Speech is behavior.

A character speaks in order to do something.

Possible dialogue actions:

attack
seduce
shame
persuade
distract
reassure
interrogate
intimidate
stall
flatter
challenge
hide
test
recruit
dismiss
manipulate
confess
provoke
beg
negotiate
The literal sentence is the text.

The underlying dramatic action is often the subtext.

Example:

TEXT:
"I didn't expect that."

POSSIBLE ACTION:
Attack the other person for violating a norm.
Do not label the action in the dialogue.

Write the line that performs it.

22. Dialogue Function 3: Verbal Pleasure
    Dialogue can also be enjoyable because of:

rhythm
word choice
voice
wit
musicality
cadence
verbal aggression
unusual imagery
professional jargon
comic timing
But beauty without dramatic function becomes decorative.

Aim for:

CHARACTER

- ACTION
- INFORMATION
- PLEASURE
  when possible.

23. McKee’s Three Dialogue Qualities
    The source’s dialogue masterclass gives three working qualities:

1. Compression and economy
   Say the maximum with the fewest useful words.

1. Direction
   Each exchange should move the scene’s behavior in a direction.

Avoid conversational spinning.

3. Purpose
   Each line or exchange should contribute to the design that builds toward the scene’s turning point.

And despite this precision, the dialogue should still sound like speech.

24. Dialogue Is Stylized Speech, Not Recorded Reality
    Real people:

repeat themselves
wander
use filler
abandon sentences
misunderstand
talk over each other
speak vaguely
Screen dialogue can use these features, but selectively.

The screenplay compresses life.

It creates the feeling of natural speech without reproducing every inefficiency of natural speech.

Useful standard
The dialogue should feel:

MORE PRECISE THAN REAL LIFE
BUT
NOT MORE SELF-AWARE THAN THE CHARACTER 25. Intention Is the Easiest Way to Write Dialogue
A repeated lesson across the source:

Nobody says anything unless they want something.

Before writing an exchange, write privately:

A WANTS B TO:

---

B WANTS A TO:

---

Make both goals specific.

Then write the characters trying to get those things.

This produces conflict naturally.

Do not create “conflict” by having people arbitrarily argue.

An argument without meaningful wants is noise.

26. Intention Must Belong to the Character, Not the Writer
    A common exposition failure:

The writer wants the audience to know information, so the character says it.

That is the writer’s objective, not the character’s objective.

Fix it by asking:

Why does the character need to say this now?

Example:

Weak:

"As you know, I served six years in the army before moving here."
Better dramatic logic:

A detective suspects the character of murder.

The character says where they were, what training they have, or what they were doing
because they are actively trying to prove innocence.
The same information now serves the character’s desire.

27. Every Line Should Behave Like a Beat
    Do not think only in paragraphs of dialogue.

Think in beats.

A beat is a unit of behavior/action-reaction.

Example:

A challenges.
B deflects.
A reframes.
B attacks.
A reveals leverage.
B threatens.
A escalates physically.
That is a dialogue scene with movement.

If five lines perform the same action, compress them.

28. On-the-Nose Dialogue
    On-the-nose dialogue often has one or more of these qualities:

character states exactly what they feel
character states exactly what they want
no gap between text and meaning
character explains visible information
line could be said by anyone
no character-specific voice
dialogue performs the writer’s exposition task
no audience inference is required
Example:

"I love you, son. You need to know how much I love you."
may be dramatically weaker than a character telling a story, fixing something, refusing to leave, or choosing an action that lets the audience infer the love.

Subtext is not mandatory in every sentence.

But important scenes often become richer when the audience can read beneath the words.

29. Subtext = What Is Really Happening Under the Text
    Track:

TEXT:
What is literally said?

SUBTEXT:
What are they actually doing?

UNSPOKEN:
What are they refusing or unable to say?

INNER STATE:
What are they thinking/feeling?

TACTIC:
Why choose this sentence instead of saying the truth?
Example:

A character confesses a terrible mistake.

Surface:

confession

Possible subtext:

begging for forgiveness

If they explicitly say:

“I confess this because I need you to forgive me”

the deeper action is flattened.

30. Subtext Comes from Constraint
    Characters do not say everything because of:

pride
fear
etiquette
status
social danger
self-deception
uncertainty
shame
power
love
professional rules
lack of vocabulary
not knowing themselves
Do not add cryptic dialogue merely to create “subtext.”

Know what prevents direct speech.

31. Misunderstanding Creates Natural Dialogue Movement
    People often hear what they expect or want to hear.

The source gives a useful model:

A separated husband is sleeping on the sofa.

His wife remarks that the sofa cannot be comfortable.

He hears:

invitation back to the bedroom.

She means:

she has contacted a lawyer and he needs to move out.

The scene becomes active because the same line carries different meanings for each person.

Use misunderstandings when psychologically justified.

32. Do Not Turn Dialogue into an Interview
    A weak pattern:

A asks question.
B answers.
A asks next question.
B answers.
A asks next question.
B answers.
This becomes a Q&A transcript.

Real dramatic conversation contains:

evasion
reframing
counterattack
silence
question with question
partial answer
lie
change of subject
deliberate misunderstanding
refusal
emotional response instead of factual response
Tension rule
A question creates tension.

An immediate straightforward answer releases it.

Sometimes let the question remain alive.

Answer later, indirectly, or unexpectedly.

33. Silence Is Dialogue Material
    Characters do not always need to respond verbally.

A look, hesitation, movement, or refusal to answer can carry more weight than a line.

During revision, ask:

Could the actor’s reaction replace this sentence?

Use silence when the audience already understands the pressure.

34. Avoid Talking Heads
    Do not forget that screen dialogue occurs in physical space.

Characters can:

walk
work
drive
cook
repair
pack
hide
search
clean
move through a location
interact with objects
The environment can punctuate the dialogue.

Do not insert meaningless business merely to avoid stillness.

The action should ideally:

reveal character
escalate conflict
express emotion
create interruption
change status
provide visual storytelling 35. Physical Actions Can Divide Dialogue Acts
A valuable example from Charlie Wilson’s War demonstrates how a long dialogue scene can be structurally punctuated by physical action.

The scene roughly progresses:

Section 1
Two men appear to be discussing an apology.

Each initially believes the other should apologize.

Conflict rises.

Physical punctuation
A maintenance worker appears because glass was broken during an earlier confrontation.

This gives the scene a visible beat and transition.

Section 2
The real issue becomes the denied Helsinki station-chief position.

The character argues rationally for why he deserved it.

Midpoint / personal turn
The superior says the problem is that he is “coarse.”

The issue becomes personal rather than merely professional.

Escalation
The subordinate reveals personal leverage / knowledge.

The superior threatens his job.

Physical punctuation
The character breaks the newly repaired glass again.

Final section
The argument reaches the deeper identity claim behind the entire scene: the character defines himself through his professional identity as an American spy.

Lesson
A four-minute argument works because it is not four minutes of one argument.

It changes:

subject
tactic
stakes
personal exposure
physical behavior
power 36. Long Dialogue Requires Structure
The source analyzes the opening of The Social Network as a long dialogue scene with a multi-part shape.

Use the logic, not the exact scene, as a model.

Part 1 — establish mismatch
One character is obsessed with status/final clubs.

The other is trying to have a normal relationship conversation.

The conflict exists before either fully acknowledges it.

Part 2 — partner engages
The girlfriend makes a genuine attempt to follow his topic.

He interprets a question through his insecurity.

Midpoint reversal
He treats her question as an insult.

The tone becomes personal.

Part 3 — relationship enters danger
She begins observing him differently.

He keeps failing to recognize the danger.

The breakup emerges.

Low point
He realizes the relationship may actually be ending.

Final section — desperate tactics
He attempts to reverse the breakup.

His tactics reveal the exact traits that caused it.

The final exchange crystallizes the character flaw and becomes memorable because the whole scene earned it.

Lesson
A long conversation survives when:

intentions collide
tactics change
the emotional state changes
stakes rise
the scene contains reversals
the ending reveals character
Do not imitate the length unless the scene has comparable structural density.

37. Build the Runway Before Critical Dialogue
    A powerful speech often needs preparation.

The source uses the boardroom speech in Network as an example.

The key lesson is not the speech itself.

The scene first:

changes location
establishes authority
builds unease
frames the speaker
creates anticipation
Then the exposition-heavy speech lands.

Without the runway, the audience may reject the amount of information.

Rule
Before a major speech, ask:

Has the scene earned the audience's attention?

Have I built tension?

Does the listener have a reason to stay?

Does the audience want the answer?

Is the person speaking dramatically dangerous, surprising, authoritative, vulnerable,
or otherwise compelling enough to hold the moment? 38. Distinct Voice Is More Than Vocabulary
A character voice can differ across many axes.

Create a voice profile.

Voice matrix
Dimension Questions
Verbosity terse or long-winded?
Formality formal or casual?
Directness says it straight or circles?
Confidence asserts, hedges, asks permission?
Pace quick, slow, interrupted?
Vocabulary simple, technical, literary, regional?
Register casual, professional, elevated, crude?
Syntax fragments, long sentences, inversions?
Fillers “like,” “you know,” none?
Pauses frequent or rare?
Emotional leakage controlled or obvious?
Metaphors sports, military, religion, tech, family?
Humor dry, defensive, absurd, cruel, none?
Profanity frequent, rare, specific words?
Questions interrogative, rhetorical, avoids asking?
Repetition catchphrases, echoing, obsessive words?
Status behavior commands, requests, apologizes?
Do not make every category exaggerated.

A few strong distinctions can be enough.

39. Same Situation, Different Character
    A useful source example observes that coffee-shop workers may all need to say roughly the same functional lines, yet their personalities alter the delivery.

One employee filters everything through optimism.

Another makes every customer’s choice about himself and his preferences.

Same job. Same basic informational task. Different worldview.

Exercise
Give five characters the same required line:

“What would you like?”

Rewrite it through five distinct character engines.

The point is not to create gimmicks.

The point is to reveal attitude.

40. The “Names Removed” Test
    Remove character labels from a scene.

Can you still identify the speakers?

If not, inspect:

vocabulary
rhythm
worldview
sentence length
tactics
confidence
social position
humor
formality
recurring word choices
Do not make every character bizarre.

Make them specific.

41. Repeated Words Need Character Ownership
    For common responses such as:

yes
no
okay
hello
goodbye
curse words
ask:

How does this character say it?

Examples:

No.
Nope.
Absolutely not.
Not happening.
Nah.
No, sir.
Forget it.
Tiny choices accumulate into voice.

42. Character-Specific Profanity
    If every character swears in the writer’s personal vocabulary, voices blur.

You can individualize:

preferred curse
severity
frequency
euphemism
religious vs bodily language
invented phrases
regional profanity
The source gives an example where inventing a distinctive mild phrase led the writer to reconceive the character as a southern surfer type, which then generated more specific language.

Lesson
Sometimes a line of dialogue reveals a better character design.

Allow voice discoveries to rewrite character conception.

43. Syntax Can Be a Signature
    Yoda is offered as an extreme example: unusual word order becomes immediately recognizable.

Do not imitate that gimmick broadly.

The lesson is:

Sentence structure itself is character voice.

Track:

subject-first vs delayed subject
complete vs fragmentary sentences
subordinate clauses
inversion
rhetorical questions
repetition
sentence length 44. Do Not Put Your Jokes into the Wrong Mouth
If a character tells jokes, there should usually be a character reason.

Example logic:

A comic character may use humor as:

defense mechanism
avoidance
status display
flirtation
attack
anxiety release
If the writer inserts a good joke into a character solely because the joke is funny, the character can temporarily disappear and the writer becomes visible.

Test
Ask:

Why would this character make a joke right now?

If the answer is:

because I thought of a funny line

consider cutting or relocating it.

45. Professional Language Reveals Life
    Specific terminology carries biography.

Do not write:

“Give me the big nail.”

if the character would know the object is a spike.

Do not force jargon for authenticity.

Use the exact terms the character would naturally know.

Vocabulary can communicate:

profession
class
education
hobbies
geography
history
without explanation.

46. Listen to Real People
    Dialogue writing is an observational craft.

Listen for:

interruptions
unfinished sentences
avoidance
repeated words
filler
mispronunciation
verbal habits
emotional changes in pace
how people get tongue-tied
how anger changes rhythm
how intimacy changes vocabulary
how people talk differently to different people
Keep notes.

Useful lines and speech patterns often come from:

parties
airports
gas stations
cafés
supermarkets
family gatherings
workplaces
Do not copy private people’s identities into the work.

Study behavior.

47. The Writer Should Be a Listener
    A repeated source principle:

The best dialogue writers are often the best listeners.

The LLM equivalent is:

When given source material containing a person’s speech, do not immediately normalize it into generic polished English.

Observe:

sentence length
preferred metaphors
hedges
slang
grammatical patterns
emotional temperature
rhythm
Preserve meaningful idiosyncrasy.

48. Read Dialogue Aloud / Table Read
    Multiple speakers in the source recommend:

reading dialogue aloud
acting it out
table reads
hearing actors perform it
Use this to detect:

tongue-twisters
accidental repetition
dead rhythm
lines too long to breathe

unnatural phrasing

The source transcript contains one internally inconsistent phrase around this advice, but the larger body of the material repeatedly supports auditory testing.

LLM simulation
Even without audio, inspect a line for speakability:

breath length
stress pattern
consonant collisions
repeated clause shape
unnatural grammar
over-written vocabulary 49. Perfect Imperfection
Characters should not all speak in pristine written sentences.

Naturalistic speech may include:

fragments
corrections

interruptions
abandoned thoughts
But do not make it unreadable.

The screenplay still needs proper spelling and professional clarity.

Do not merely transcribe mess.

50. Dialogue Rhythm and Meter
    Dialogue has music.

Listen for:

stressed words
sentence length
repetition
acceleration
pause
interruption
cadence
contrast between speakers
Memorable lines often have:

concision
tension
rhythm
Do not write lines while trying to manufacture a quote.

A memorable line should emerge from:

character
conflict
timing
scene pressure 51. Avoid “Look How Clever I Am” Dialogue
If a line calls attention to the writer’s cleverness, interrogate it.

Ask:

Would this character say it?

Does it serve the scene?

Is it speakable?

Does it change anything?

Would I still keep it if I knew nobody would quote it?
If not, cut it.

52. Word Echo Can Create Flow
    A useful dialogue technique:

One speaker uses a word.

The next speaker picks up or transforms that word.

This can make an exchange feel linked.

Example pattern:

A: "I don't think I caught your name."
B: "I don't think I pitched it."
The exact words are less important than the device:

WORD / IDEA
→ ECHO
→ VARIATION
Use sparingly.

53. Rhetorical Repetition Can Build Pressure
    The source gives epistrophe—repeating the same ending—as an example.

Pattern:

Do X → consequence.
Do Y → same consequence.
Do Z → same consequence.
Repetition can communicate:

institutional control
threat
obsession
ritual
comedy
inevitability
Do not add rhetorical devices merely to show craft.

They must fit the speaker.

54. Alliteration and Other Devices: Use Carefully
    A character can use:

alliteration
parallelism
antithesis
repetition
metaphor
rhetorical questions
But conspicuous rhetoric can make the character sound artificially written.

Use stronger stylization when:

the character is theatrical
speechmaking is part of the setting
comedy depends on verbal excess
the world supports heightened language
Otherwise hide the craft.

55. Exposition Must Arrive When Needed
    Do not dump:

biography
worldbuilding
backstory
rules
relationships
past events
before the audience needs them.

Information is easier to accept when it solves an active dramatic question.

Exposition timing model
AUDIENCE NEEDS TO KNOW

- CHARACTER HAS REASON TO SAY IT
- # SCENE CONFLICT ABSORBS IT
  DRAMATIZED EXPOSITION

56. Trust the Audience
    Over-exposition often comes from believing the audience will not understand.

Before explaining, ask:

Did the image already show it?
Did the previous scene imply it?
Can the audience infer it?
Can one word do the work of five lines?
Is ambiguity currently useful?
Do not make the audience do impossible detective work.

But do not solve every inference for them.

57. Description: Project a Movie into the Reader’s Head
    Screenplay description is not novelistic interior prose.

Its job is to cause the reader to imagine the film.

Aim for:

vividness
economy
visual specificity
action
necessary spatial information
Avoid directing every shot.

Use only the visual setup information necessary to create the intended experience.

58. Concrete Nouns Beat Vague Adjectives
    Instead of:

a big nail

use:

a spike

Instead of:

a big expensive house

consider:

a mansion / villa / estate

The exact noun carries image and meaning.

A specific noun can eliminate multiple adjectives.

59. Active Verbs Beat Adverb Piles
    Instead of:

He walks slowly across the room.

choose the action:

ambles
pads
saunters
creeps
limps
drifts
The verb should name what we see.

Do not choose a fancy verb merely to sound literary.

Choose the precise action.

60. The “Is / Are” Diagnostic
    One source exercise suggests searching screenplay description for “is” and “are” and challenging static construction.

Do not treat this as a literal ban.

Use it as a revision diagnostic.

Weak:

There is a villa on the hill.

More active:

A villa sprawls across the ridge.

The second produces motion in the imagination.

61. Description Can Be Elaborate or Minimal
    The source contrasts writers with very different description styles.

One may write rich description.

Another may simply write something equivalent to:

INT. KITCHEN

and move immediately to dialogue.

The principle is not:

always write minimal description.

The principle is:

Write the amount of description your screenplay needs to project the film.

Find a personal economical style.

62. Do Not Over-Direct
    Avoid filling the page with:

unnecessary camera instructions
every glance
every breath
every hand movement
every line reading
actor psychology
unless the action is essential to story meaning.

Give actors playable material.

Let actors act.

63. Description Should Support Character Point of View When Useful
    The source mentions prose writers whose descriptive narration changes depending on whose perspective governs the scene.

Screenplay prose can also gain energy from selective tonal alignment.

Do not write omniscient literary internal narration.

But word choice can subtly reflect:

threat
wonder
disgust
comedy
urgency
if appropriate to the project’s voice.

64. Diction, Register, Syntax, Cadence, Pace
    The Richard Powers material emphasizes that prose effects emerge from multiple language layers working together.

Useful layers:

DICTION
What exact word?

REGISTER
Casual? Formal? Technical? Vulgar? Elevated?

SYNTAX
How is the sentence built?

CADENCE
How does it sound / move?

PACE
How quickly does it deliver information?

SCENE TEMPO
How does sentence movement support dramatic movement?
These layers should work in harmony.

Use them in screenplay description selectively; do not turn action lines into a novel.

65. Tension Is the Perception that Stakes Are Rising
    Conflict is not just loudness.

Tension rises when a character realizes that the consequences matter more than before.

Track:

WHAT CAN BE LOST?
WHAT CAN BE GAINED?
WHAT JUST BECAME HARDER?
WHAT NEW INFORMATION MAKES THIS WORSE?
WHAT OPTION DISAPPEARED?
WHAT COST INCREASED?
Tension can rise quietly.

66. Escalation Should Not Descend Accidentally
    The source gives a simple story-logic observation:

If a prince defeats the hardest dragon first and then later fights an easier one, the progression may feel dramatically backwards unless there is another dimension of stakes.

Escalation does not always mean:

bigger explosion
stronger villain
It can mean:

more personal
more morally costly
more irreversible
more intimate
more psychologically exposing 67. Critical Dialogue Should Change Tactics
When writing a scene, mark each tactic.

Example:

TACTIC 1 — flatter
TACTIC 2 — appeal to logic
TACTIC 3 — guilt
TACTIC 4 — threaten
TACTIC 5 — confess
If a character repeats the same tactic for three pages, the scene may feel static.

A new tactic is often a new beat.

68. Don’t Confuse Argument with Conflict
    Two characters shouting opposite opinions is not automatically dramatic.

Conflict becomes meaningful when:

both want something
those wants are incompatible
both have stakes
both can act
each action affects the other
A scene can contain conflict without raised voices.

69. Show Expertise in Action
    The source notes the pleasure of watching characters be good at their jobs.

Workplaces provide:

hierarchy
shared mission
professional language
status
competence
conflict
surrogate family
When writing a professional environment, let competence become characterization.

Do not only tell us:

She is brilliant.

Show her solving something.

70. Long-Term Character Arc Can Be a Persistent Void
    Some characters are driven by a lack that achievement cannot fully solve.

Track:

WHAT DOES THE CHARACTER THINK WILL FILL THE VOID?

WHAT DO THEY KEEP ACHIEVING?

WHY DOES THE VOID REMAIN?

WHAT NEW TACTIC DO THEY TRY?
This can produce dynamic high-achieving characters.

71. Study Good Work in Your Genre
    Do not copy a universal “best screenplay.”

Study works related to what you are trying to write.

Ask:

How is tension built?
How is exposition hidden?
How are scenes turned?
How much description is used?
How do character voices differ?
What conventions does the genre fulfill?
Which expectations does it bend?
The goal is not imitation.

The goal is craft literacy.

72. Study Bad Work and Rewrite It
    A particularly useful source exercise:

Study a movie or script that fails for you.

Diagnose:

why is it boring?
what do I not believe?
what is missing?
where did tension die?
why is the antagonist empty?
where is the dialogue false?
why is the protagonist passive?
what change does the scene fail to create?
Then rewrite it.

Why?

Because your own first drafts will also be broken.

Practicing repair is direct training for rewriting your work.

73. Feedback: Filter Through the Work, Not Ego
    When someone says:

“These characters all sound the same.”

The first reaction may be:

They think I am a bad writer.

That is ego processing.

Replace it with:

If they are right, how are they right?

Maybe:

both use the same metaphors
both agree on everything
both have the same rhythm
both swear the same way
both have the same comic tone
both share an unnecessary preference
You do not have to agree with every note.

But diagnose the signal before rejecting it.

74. Notes Are Problems, Not Always Solutions
    If a reader says:

“Make this character funnier.”

The underlying problem may be:

scene lacks contrast
character has no defense mechanism
tension is monotonous
voice is flat
Do not mechanically apply the suggested fix.

Find the underlying failure.

76. Dialogue Drafting Procedure
    When asked to write dialogue, use this internal order:

Step 1 — Establish wants
What does each character want from the other right now?

Step 2 — Establish concealment
What is each refusing to say?

Step 3 — Establish voice
How does each person speak differently?

Step 4 — Establish the environment
What are they doing physically?

Step 5 — Write actions first if needed
Where do interruptions or physical beats occur?

Step 6 — Draft dialogue as tactics
Each line should attempt to move the other person.

Step 7 — Prevent Q&A
Add evasion, misinterpretation, counterattack, or silence where truthful.

Step 8 — Find the turn
What changes the direction?

Step 9 — Compress
Remove repeated thought.

Step 10 — Subtext pass
Where can literal statement become behavior or implication?

Step 11 — Voice pass
Could another character say this line unchanged?

Step 12 — Speakability pass
Check rhythm and length.

77. Dialogue Diagnostic
    Flag a line if:

it explains what both characters know
it explains what the image already shows
it exists only for the audience
it has no intention
it could be said by anyone
it repeats the previous line’s function
it answers a question too cleanly
it names the emotion instead of dramatizing it
it is cleverer than the character
it is written for quotability
it contains unnecessary setup before the actual point 78. The Compression Exercise

    For every paragraph of action:

Identify what the audience must see.
Remove internal explanation that cannot be filmed.
Replace vague nouns with exact nouns.

83. Avoid Novelistic Psychology in Action Lines
    Do not write:

He remembers how lonely childhood was and wonders if this marriage is another version of his father’s abandonment.

unless the project has a deliberate nonstandard screenplay voice.

Write playable/visible behavior.

For example:

He reaches for the ring.
Stops.
Leaves it on the sink.
The actor and context can carry the rest.

84. Environment Should Stay Present
    Dialogue does not erase the room.

Periodically orient the audience through:

object use
movement
location change
spatial relation
interruption
physical consequence
Do not add description every two lines.

Keep the setting alive.

85. Action and Dialogue Should Affect Each Other
    Best case:

DIALOGUE CHANGES ACTION
and
ACTION CHANGES DIALOGUE
Example:

A character lies while packing a suitcase.

The other person notices what is being packed.

The next line changes.

The physical world is now part of the argument.

86. Monologues
    Long speeches can work.

But interrogate them.

A monologue should usually have:

a reason the speaker has the floor
an objective
an audience inside the scene
internal turns
escalation
revelation
rhythm
payoff
Do not justify length by saying:

Tarantino does monologues.

The style and scene must earn it.

87. “No More Than X Lines” Is a Heuristic, Not a Law
    Some teachers advise keeping dialogue blocks relatively short.

Treat this as a readability warning.

Long blocks require stronger:

structure
voice
tension
reason to listen
Do not break a powerful speech into unnatural fragments solely to follow a numerical rule.

88. Critical Line Placement
    A great final line of a scene often works because everything before it changes how we hear it.

Do not start with the quote.

Build the structure that earns the quote.

This is especially important for:

breakup lines
threats
identity claims
admissions
punchlines
reversals 89. Conflict Escalation Map
For a dialogue-heavy scene, track:

LEVEL 1 — disagreement
LEVEL 2 — personal implication
LEVEL 3 — hidden history
LEVEL 4 — leverage / threat
LEVEL 5 — irreversible decision
The exact ladder varies.

The principle is progressive pressure.

90. The Character-Revealing Mistake
    In strong scenes, characters often use the wrong tactic because of who they are.

This is valuable.

In the Social Network example, the character tries to save a relationship using the same status-conscious logic that helped destroy it.

Rule
Do not make characters strategically perfect.

Let flaws determine tactics.

That is how plot and character become one mechanism.

91. External Goal, Internal Goal, Hidden Drive
    For major characters, maintain:

EXTERNAL GOAL:
What concrete result do they pursue?

INTERNAL GOAL:
What emotional condition do they seek?

HIDDEN DRIVE:
What are they really trying to repair, prove, escape, or repeat?
Dialogue should be influenced by all three.

92. Point of View Changes Speech
    A character does not simply possess a vocabulary.

They possess a way of interpreting reality.

One person’s default spin may be:

everything is opportunity
everything is insult
everything is competition
everything is evidence
everything is joke
everything is threat
Voice grows from worldview.

93. Character Speech Changes by Relationship
    Do not give a character one fixed “voice setting.”

A person may be:

terse with father
charming with clients
crude with friends
formal with boss
childish with sibling
silent with spouse
Identity is relational.

Maintain consistency while allowing relational variation.

94. Research for Voice
    When writing a place, profession, or subculture unfamiliar to you:

listen before inventing.

Research:

words
cadence
what people do not say
hierarchy
in-group shorthand
insult patterns
politeness
code-switching
Do not scatter slang randomly.

Understand context.

95. Don’t Make Everyone as Articulate as the Writer
    A character’s ability to express themselves depends on:

education
personality
emotional state
social context
speed of thought
confidence
profession
Some know exactly what they feel but cannot say it.

Some speak beautifully and do not understand themselves.

That difference is dramatic.

96. Dialogue Can Reveal What a Character Notices
    A botanist, mechanic, lawyer, and child may describe the same object differently.

This is character without exposition.

Use selective attention as voice.

97. Scene Rewrite: “Would They Really Say That?”
    After the first draft, read every line and ask:

Would this exact person choose these exact words
to this exact person
at this exact moment
given what they want and what they fear?
This is stricter than:

Is the line good?

A good line in the wrong mouth is a bad line.

98. Scene Rewrite: “Would They Really Stay?”
    Sometimes the problem is not dialogue.

The realistic character would:

leave
hang up
call security
refuse to answer
hit someone
change the subject
laugh
shut down
Do not trap characters inside a scene merely because you wrote more dialogue.

Give them reasons to remain.

99. Scene Rewrite: Remove Repeated Functions
    Label each line by function:

persuade
attack
defend
explain
deflect
threaten
etc.
If six consecutive lines are all “explain,” condense or change tactics.

100. Scene Rewrite: Find the Missing Turn

122. Anti-Patterns
     Do not produce these unless the story deliberately requires them:

Exposition dump
Character explains information for the audience.

Emotional labeling
“I am sad because…”

Q&A dialogue
Question, answer, question, answer.

Identical voices
Everyone sounds like the writer.

Witty fog
Every line is clever, no one is pursuing anything.

Fake conflict
People argue but nothing meaningful is at stake.

Static scene
Entry state equals exit state.

Repeated tactic
Three pages of the same persuasion attempt.

Plot puppet
Character acts against their psychology because the outline requires it.

Generic description
“big house,” “walks slowly.”

Novel prose
Unfilmable internal explanation dominates action.

Over-direction
Every eyebrow and camera angle is prescribed.

Quotable-line hunting
Writer tries to manufacture a meme.

Unmotivated jokes
Funny line belongs to writer, not character.

Over-explained subtext
Character states what the scene already lets us infer.

125. Final Principle
     Strong script writing does not begin with clever lines.

It begins with:

TRUTHFUL HUMAN BEHAVIOR

- SPECIFIC DESIRE
- REAL RESISTANCE
- TACTICAL ACTION
- CHANGE
  Strong dialogue grows from that engine.

Strong character grows from choices under pressure.

Strong structure is the progression of meaningful change.

Strong description makes the reader see the film without smothering it.

And strong rewriting comes from stepping outside the work, identifying precisely what is false, static, generic, repetitive, or unclear, and repairing it until the behavior feels inevitable and surprising at the same time.
